Timeline of the French Revolution 1789–1815

A chronological framework. Event names with wikilinks have dedicated pages; others are recorded here in narrative form and await their own articles.

1789: The Revolution Begins

The year opens with France in the grip of a fiscal and political crisis that the king, louis-xvi, has proven unable to resolve. The Assembly of Notables, convened in 1787, having refused to consent to new taxes, the king is forced to summon the Estates-General for the first time since 1614. The elections of the spring of 1789, accompanied by the drafting of cahiers de doléances (lists of grievances), unleash a flood of hope, expectation, and political argument across the country.

When the Estates-General assembles at Versailles on 5 May 1789, the Third Estate immediately challenges the traditional voting by order, demanding that the three orders deliberate together and vote by head. The deadlock lasts six weeks. On 17 June, the Third Estate declares itself the National Assembly. On 20 June, finding their meeting hall locked, the deputies swear the Tennis Court Oath not to separate until France has a constitution. The king’s attempts to reassert control — the royal session of 23 June, the gathering of troops around Paris and Versailles — only inflame the situation.

The dismissal of the popular minister Necker on 11 July sparks the insurrection in Paris. The crowds arm themselves, the Hôtel des Invalides is raided for muskets, and on 14 July the fall-of-the-bastille delivers the first great victory of the Revolution. besenval, commanding the royal troops around Paris, finds his regiments mutinous and cannot restore order. The king, visiting Paris on 17 July, accepts the tricolour cockade. The émigration of the court conservatives — the Comte d’Artois, the Polignacs, the Prince de Condé — begins within days.

In the countryside, the Great Fear of July–August sweeps the provinces: rumours of aristocratic brigands provoke peasant revolts against château and manor. On the night of 4 August, the National Assembly decrees the abolition of feudal privileges. On 26 August, it adopts the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen.

The king hesitates to sanction the August decrees and the constitutional articles. Meanwhile, the bread shortage in Paris and the court’s provocative banquet of the Flanders Regiment on 1 October produce the the-october-days — the women’s march to Versailles, the invasion of the château, and the forced return of the royal family to Paris on 6 October. The king takes up residence at the Tuileries; the National Assembly follows, establishing itself in the Manège. The court-at-versailles has ceased to exist.

1790: The Constitutional Experiment

The year is dominated by the attempt to build a new political order. The National Assembly continues its work of reconstruction: the old provinces are abolished and replaced by eighty-three départements; the Civil Constitution of the Clergy is adopted (July), subordinating the Church to the state and requiring all clergy to swear an oath to the nation; the hereditary nobility and titles of nobility are abolished (19 June); the judicial system is reformed; the system of assignats — paper currency backed by the sale of church lands — is introduced.

The Fête de la Fédération on 14 July, the first anniversary of the Bastille, is staged on the Champ de Mars in Paris as a grand festival of national unity. louis-xvi takes an oath to the constitution; Talleyrand, the constitutional bishop of Autun, celebrates mass. Lafayette, the hero of two worlds, commands the National Guard and seems, for a moment, the arbiter of the Revolution. gouverneur-morris, the American diplomat and diarist, watches these events with a shrewd and increasingly sceptical eye.

But the fissures are already visible. The Civil Constitution of the Clergy splits the Church and alienates the devout. The king, whose piety is deep and sincere, is tormented by the religious question. The émigré army gathers at Coblentz under the Prince de Condé. The Jacobin Club, founded in 1789, grows into a nationwide network of political societies. marmontel, watching from his retirement, notes the growing radicalism of the popular societies with concern.

1791: The King’s Flight and the Rupture

The year begins with the king a prisoner in his own capital. His relations with the Revolution deteriorate over the Civil Constitution of the Clergy, which he cannot accept in conscience. On 18 April, a mob prevents the royal family from leaving the Tuileries to spend Easter at Saint-Cloud — a clear sign that they are no longer free.

On the night of 20–21 June, the king, the queen marie-antoinette, and their children attempt to flee Paris in a large berline, heading for the royalist army at Montmédy. The flight-to-varennes ends at the posting station of Varennes-en-Argonne, where the king is recognized from his portrait on the assignat and arrested. The royal family is brought back to Paris under guard, a silent procession through crowds that watch in ominous stillness.

The flight shatters the constitutional experiment. The king is suspended from his functions; the Cordeliers Club (Danton, Marat, Desmoulins, Hébert) agitates for the abolition of the monarchy; a republican movement appears for the first time. On 17 July, a petition demanding the king’s deposition is presented at the Champ de Mars; Lafayette’s National Guard fires on the crowd — the Champ de Mars Massacre — killing perhaps fifty. The split between the moderate Feuillants (Lafayette, Bailly) and the radical Jacobins becomes irreparable.

In August, the Declaration of Pillnitz — issued by the Emperor Leopold II (the queen’s brother) and King Frederick William II of Prussia — threatens intervention in support of the French monarchy, though in terms so conditional that it serves more as a provocation than a promise. The Assembly completes the Constitution of 1791, and on 14 September the king, having accepted it, is reinstalled. The Legislative Assembly, the new one-house parliament elected under the Constitution, meets on 1 October. The Revolution has entered a new phase.

1792: War, the Fall of the Monarchy, and the Republic

The Legislative Assembly is dominated by the Girondins — deputies from the Gironde and their allies, led by Brissot, Vergniaud, and Guadet — who press for war against Austria, hoping to unite the nation and force the king’s hand. The king, who also wants war (in the hope that defeat would restore his authority), appoints a Girondin ministry. On 20 April, France declares war on Austria. the-girondins have their war.

The war goes badly from the start. The French armies retreat; the Austrian and Prussian forces advance. The Legislative Assembly decrees the deportation of refractory priests (May) and the formation of a camp of fédérés (federal volunteers) outside Paris (June). On 20 June, a crowd invades the Tuileries and forces the king to wear the red bonnet and drink to the nation’s health.

On 25 July, the Brunswick Manifesto, issued by the allied commander, threatens Paris with exemplary punishment if the royal family is harmed. The effect is the opposite of what was intended: it inflames Parisian opinion and convinces the sections that the king is in league with the invaders. On the night of 9–10 August, the tocsin rings across Paris; the insurrectionary Commune seizes the Hôtel de Ville; at dawn on 10-august-1792, the crowd attacks the Tuileries Palace. The king takes refuge in the Legislative Assembly, but the monarchy is effectively overthrown. The royal family is imprisoned in the Temple.

In the first week of September, as the Prussian army approaches Verdun, the september-massacres break out in the Paris prisons: more than a thousand prisoners — common criminals, refractory priests, and suspected counter-revolutionaries — are summarily executed by gangs organized by the Commune. The National Convention, elected by universal male suffrage, meets on 20 September. On the same day, the French army halts the Prussian advance at the cannonade of Valmy — a victory that saves the Revolution. On 21 September, the Convention abolishes the monarchy and proclaims the Republic.

1793: The King’s Execution, the Terror, and the Federalist Revolt

The new year opens with the Convention debating the fate of the former king. The discovery of the armoire de fer — a secret iron chest containing proof of louis-xvi’s correspondence with the émigrés and foreign powers — seals his fate. The trial-louis-xvi ends with his conviction and execution by the guillotine on 21 January 1793. The queen will follow on 16 October.

The execution of the king unites Europe against France. Britain, Holland, Spain, Piedmont, and the Empire join the war against the Republic. The crisis is total: military reverses on all frontiers, the defection of General Dumouriez (the victor of Valmy and Jemappes), the collapse of the assignat, and the outbreak of the Vendée — a massive peasant insurrection in western France against conscription and the revolutionary Church.

In Paris, the conflict between the Girondins and the Mountain (the Jacobin left, led by Robespierre, Danton, and Marat) reaches its climax. On 31 May–2 June, the insurrectionary Commune and the National Guard under Hanriot surround the Convention and compel the arrest of twenty-nine Girondin deputies. The fall-of-the-girondins breaks the moderate republican centre. madame-roland, the intellectual heart of the Girondin salon, is arrested and will be executed in November.

The Convention adopts the Constitution of 1793 (the Jacobin constitution, with universal suffrage and the right of insurrection), but it is never implemented. In September, the Convention declares terror the order of the day. The Revolutionary Tribunal is reorganized, the Law of Suspects is passed, the Committee of Public Safety (dominated by Robespierre, Saint-Just, and Couthon) becomes the effective government of France. The the-terror has begun. The the-meaning-of-terror — as a lived experience of arbitrary power, surveillance, and daily fear — takes shape in the prisons and on the streets.

The military situation stabilizes by the end of the year: the Vendée rebels are crushed at Le Mans and Savenay; Toulon is recaptured from the British, a young artillery officer named bonaparte distinguishing himself in the siege; the Austrian armies are pushed back. The levée en masse (August) has created the first modern national army.

1794: The Height of the Terror and Thermidor

The spring of 1794 sees the Terror at its most systematic. The Revolutionary Tribunal accelerates its work; the Law of 22 Prairial (10 June) suppresses defence lawyers and witness testimony, making the tribunal a machine of pure condemnation. In the six weeks between the law’s passage and 9 Thermidor, more than 1,300 people are executed in Paris alone. The guillotine in the Place de la Révolution works without pause.

The revolutionary factions devour one another. The Hébertists (the extreme left, advocates of dechristianization and economic control) are executed in March. Danton and Desmoulins (the indulgents, who called for an end to the Terror) follow in April. Robespierre stands alone at the summit of power, with only Saint-Just and Couthon as his trusted allies.

The Festival of the Supreme Being (8 June), organized by the painter David, is Robespierre’s attempt to give the Republic a civil religion. But the extermination of the factions has deprived him of any political base. On 8 Thermidor (26 July), Saint-Just’s speech in the Convention goes awry; on 9-thermidor (27 July 1794), Robespierre and his allies are arrested after a tumultuous session. The Commune rises in their defence, but the Convention responds by outlawing them. Robespierre is shot in the jaw (by his own hand or a gendarme’s bullet), and on 10 Thermidor he and twenty-one of his followers are guillotined without trial. The Terror ends not by decree but by the collapse of its engine.

The Thermidorian Reaction sets in: the Jacobin Club is closed, the Revolutionary Tribunal is reformed, the Law of 22 Prairial is repealed. The prisons begin to empty. But the reaction has its own violence — the jeunesse dorée (gilded youth) harrass and beat former Jacobins in the streets.

On the frontiers, the French armies continue to advance. The Battle of Fleurus (26 June) secures Belgium. By the end of 1794, the Republic’s military position is stronger than it has ever been.

1795: The Directory Established

The year is one of political consolidation and repression. In January, French cavalry captures the Dutch fleet frozen in the ice at the Texel — a unique feat. The Republic annexes Belgium and the left bank of the Rhine. Prussia and Spain make peace (the Treaties of Basel, April and July).

In Paris, the Thermidorian Convention drafts a new constitution — the Constitution of the Year III — designed to prevent any repetition of the Terror. It establishes the the-directory-government: an executive of five Directors, a bicameral legislature (the Council of Five Hundred and the Council of the Ancients), and a property-based electoral system. The Two-Thirds Decree requires that two-thirds of the first legislature be drawn from the sitting Convention — a self-protecting measure that provokes a royalist rising.

On 5 October (13 Vendémiaire Year IV), a royalist insurrection in Paris is crushed by artillery under the command of General Bonaparte — his first decisive intervention in national politics. The Directory assumes power on 26 October 1795. The the-emigres remain in exile, their hopes of a counter-revolutionary restoration briefly raised and then dashed.

1796: Bonaparte’s Italian Campaign

The war continues against Austria and its allies. The Directory’s great strategic plan — three armies converging on Vienna — fails in Germany but succeeds spectacularly in Italy. General Bonaparte, appointed to command the Army of Italy at the age of twenty-six, undertakes the italian-campaigns that will make his reputation.

In a series of lightning campaigns — Montenotte (April), Lodi (May, where Bonaparte leads the charge across the bridge), Castiglione (August), Arcola (November), and Rivoli (January 1797) — Bonaparte defeats the Piedmontese and Austrian armies in detail. He forces Piedmont out of the war, lays siege to Mantua (which falls in February 1797), and pursues the Austrians into the Tyrol and Carinthia, advancing to within a hundred miles of Vienna. He also extracts immense contributions from the Italian states, sending millions in gold and artworks back to the Directory in Paris — and establishing himself as a political force independent of the government.

At home, the Directory faces economic crisis and the Babeuf Conspiracy (the Conspiracy of Equals, led by Gracchus Babeuf, a proto-communist uprising that is crushed in May 1796). The assignat collapses; the Directory replaces it with the mandat territorial, which collapses in turn. By the end of the year, the Directory has lost the confidence of nearly every faction.

1797: The Republic Victorious

Bonaparte’s advance on Vienna forces Austria to seek peace. The Treaty of Campo Formio (October 1797) gives France Belgium, the left bank of the Rhine, and the Cisalpine Republic in northern Italy. Only Britain remains at war.

In Paris, the Directory conducts the coup of 18 Fructidor (4 September 1797) against a royalist majority in the Councils. Three Directors (Barras, La Révellière-Lépeaux, Rewbell) purge the legislature, annul elections, and deport opponents to Guiana. The republican dictatorship of the Directory is now open. The coup of Fructidor destroys the regime’s constitutional legitimacy and confirms that the Revolution can only be sustained by force.

Bonaparte, now the most famous man in France, does not return to Paris to share in this political crisis. He remains in Italy, organizing the Cisalpine Republic, negotiating with the Italian states, and cultivating his own network of loyalty.

1798: The Egyptian Expedition

The Directory, eager to remove Bonaparte from the centre of politics and to strike at Britain’s route to India, authorises the egyptian-campaign. Bonaparte sails from Toulon in May with 35,000 men and a commission of scholars (the savants). He captures Malta en route and lands in Egypt in July.

The land campaign is a success: the french-colonial-empire briefly expands as Bonaparte defeats the Mamluk armies at the Battle of the Pyramids (July) and enters Cairo. But the naval campaign is a disaster: on 1 August, Nelson destroys the French fleet at the battle-of-the-nile (Aboukir Bay), stranding the French army in Egypt and giving Britain command of the Mediterranean.

The Second Coalition against France begins to form: Britain, Austria, Russia, Naples, and the Ottoman Empire. The Directory faces war on multiple fronts while its best general is trapped in Egypt. The French expedition to Ireland (August–October) ends in failure. The Neapolitan campaign (General Championnet’s capture of Naples) briefly creates the Parthenopean Republic, but it soon collapses.

1799: The Crisis and Brumaire

The year sees the Republic in mortal danger on every front. Russian armies under Suvorov overrun Bonaparte’s Italian conquests — the battles of the Trebbia (June) and Novi (August) drive the French out of Italy. The Austrians advance in Switzerland and Germany. The British land in Holland (August). The Chouan insurrection in the west — the the-chouannerie — revives.

The Directory, bankrupt and despised, lurches from crisis to crisis. The coup of 30 Prairial (18 June) forces three Directors to resign; Barras is the only survivor of the original five. The Jacobin Club briefly revives; the Law of Hostages (July) targets the relatives of émigrés; the military reverses continue.

Bonaparte, having heard of the crisis, abandons his army in Egypt and returns to France in October, landing at Fréjus. He is greeted as the saviour of the nation. In November, he joins the Abbé Sieyès (who has entered the Directory in May) in a conspiracy. On 18-brumaire (9–10 November 1799), Bonaparte overthrows the Directory by a coup d’état. The Constitution of the Year VIII establishes the Consulate: Bonaparte as First Consul, with two subordinate consuls, and an elaborate legislative system designed to concentrate power in his hands. The Revolution is over; the age of Bonaparte has begun.

1800: Marengo and the Consolidation of the Consulate

Bonaparte moves to consolidate his power and restore the military situation. He crosses the Great Saint Bernard Pass with the Army of Reserve in May, surprising the Austrian army in Italy. On 14 June, he wins the Battle of Marengo — a near-run engagement saved by the timely arrival of Desaix’s division, a victory that recovers Italy. Desaix is killed in the charge; Bonaparte later erects a monument to him.

In Switzerland, General Moreau defeats the Austrians at the Battle of Hohenlinden (3 December), opening the road to Vienna. The Austrians sue for peace; the Treaty of Lunéville (February 1801) confirms the terms of Campo Formio.

At home, Bonaparte consolidates the regime. The Bank of France is founded (January); the administrative system of prefects is established; the pacification of the Vendée is achieved through a combination of military repression and conciliation. The Compromise of 1801–2 with the Papacy — the Concordat — restores the Catholic Church in France while keeping it under state control. Peace negotiations with Britain begin.

In the Baltic, the armed-neutrality-1800 — a league of Russia, Denmark, Sweden, and Prussia against British maritime rights — is shattered by Nelson’s victory at the battle-of-copenhagen (2 April 1801) and the assassination of Tsar Paul I in March.

1801–1802: The Peace of Amiens

The peace-of-amiens, signed on 27 March 1802, brings the first general peace in a decade. Britain returns most of its colonial conquests; France leaves Egypt and the Papal States; the Peace of Amiens is greeted with universal relief. Bonaparte uses the peace to consolidate his domestic position: the Legion of Honour is instituted (May 1802), the Concordat is promulgated (Easter 1802), and Bonaparte is made Consul for Life by plebiscite (August 1802).

The continental-system is not yet conceived; the peace allows a brief resumption of trade and travel between France and Britain. But the peace is fragile: Britain refuses to evacuate Malta; Bonaparte continues to expand French influence in Italy, Switzerland, and the Low Countries. War, both sides know, will resume.

1803: The Rupture of the Peace

The Peace of Amiens breaks down over the Malta question and Bonaparte’s interventions in Germany, Switzerland, and Italy. Britain declares war on France on 18 May 1803. Bonaparte responds by ordering the arrest of all British subjects in France and by assembling the Army of England — the Boulogne flotilla — for an invasion of Britain that will never come.

The Louisiana Territory is sold to the United States (April 1803) — a strategic decision driven by the loss of Saint-Domingue and the certainty that France cannot defend a North American empire while fighting Britain. The french-colonial-empire in America is liquidated.

In France, the regime grows more autocratic. The Code Civil (the Napoleonic Code) is promulgated in March 1804, and in December of that year Bonaparte crowns himself Emperor Napoleon I at Notre-Dame — the pope present, the crown placed on his own head.

1805: Austerlitz and Trafalgar

The year of two epochal battles. The Third Coalition (Britain, Austria, Russia, Sweden, Naples) forms against France. Napoleon abandons the invasion of England and turns the Army of England into the Grande Armée, marching east to strike Austria before the Russians can arrive.

The Ulm campaign (September–October) surrounds and captures an entire Austrian army without a major battle. On 13 November, the French enter Vienna. On 2 December, Napoleon wins the battle-of-austerlitz — his masterpiece — destroying the Austro-Russian army on the Pratzen Heights. The Treaty of Pressburg (26 December) dissolves the Holy Roman Empire and establishes French hegemony over Central Europe.

But at sea, the trafalgar-campaign ends in catastrophe. On 21 October, Nelson destroys the Franco-Spanish fleet off Cape Trafalgar. Napoleon will never challenge British naval supremacy again. The sea-power-as-concept — the thesis that British command of the sea determined the outcome of the wars — is already visible in embryo. Trafalgar, as Mahan would later argue, forced Napoleon to fight at Austerlitz before winter closed the campaign season.

1806: The End of the Holy Roman Empire

The year opens with Napoleon dominant in Europe. The Holy Roman Empire is dissolved (August 1806); Francis II becomes Francis I of Austria. The Confederation of the Rhine is established: a French client federation of German states. Prussia, humiliated by Napoleon’s creation of the Confederation and by the violation of its territory, finally declares war in October.

The Prussian campaign of 1806 is the most rapid of Napoleon’s wars. At Jena-Auerstädt (14 October), the Prussian army is destroyed in a single day. Napoleon enters Berlin, where he issues the Berlin Decree (21 November), establishing the continental-system — a blockade of British trade designed to destroy Britain’s economic power. The system will become the central strategic problem of Napoleon’s reign.

1807: Tilsit

The campaign against Russia begins in the Polish winter. The Battle of Eylau (7–8 February) is a bloody stalemate; the Battle of Friedland (14 June) is a decisive French victory. Napoleon and Tsar Alexander I meet on a raft on the River Niemen at Tilsit (July). The Treaties of Tilsit divide Europe between France and Russia: Alexander accepts the Continental System and the partition of Prussian Poland (the Duchy of Warsaw is created); Prussia is reduced to a secondary power.

Napoleon is at the height of his power. The Empire extends from the Pyrenees to the Vistula. But the system has its weaknesses: Britain is untouched, the Continental System is already being evaded, and the Tsar’s commitment to the alliance is uncertain.

1808: The Peninsular War Begins

Napoleon intervenes in the dynastic crisis of the Spanish Bourbons. French troops enter Spain; Napoleon forces the abdication of Charles IV and Ferdinand VII and places his brother Joseph on the Spanish throne (May 1808). On 2 May, the people of Madrid rise against the French — the Dos de Mayo uprising, crushed by Murat’s Mamelukes. But the insurrection spreads across Spain, and on 22 July, a French army under Dupont surrenders at Bailén — the first French defeat on land since the Revolution.

The Peninsular War — a six-year guerrilla struggle that will drain French resources and morale — has begun. Napoleon himself enters Spain with the Grande Armée in November, restoring the situation with victories at Gamonal, Espinosa, and Tudela, and entering Madrid on 4 December. But the British, under Sir John Moore, intervene in the north; Moore’s retreat to Corunna ends in his death and the evacuation of his army (January 1809), but it has demonstrated that Britain can operate on the continent.

The the-chouannerie of the Vendée finds its Spanish counterpart in the guerrilla.

1809: Wagram and the Austrian War

Austria, encouraged by Napoleon’s difficulties in Spain, declares war in April. The Austrian army under Archduke Charles advances into Bavaria but is contained. Napoleon takes Vienna in May but is repulsed in his first attempt to cross the Danube at Aspern-Essling (21–22 May) — his first personal defeat on the battlefield, and the death of his friend Marshal Lannes.

In July, Napoleon crosses the Danube in force and defeats the Austrians at the Battle of Wagram (5–6 July). The Treaty of Schönbrunn (October) deprives Austria of Salzburg, Galicia, and the Illyrian provinces, and reduces its army to 150,000 men.

Napoleon’s divorce of Joséphine (December) and his search for a new empress — a dynastic marriage that would seal his legitimacy among the old monarchies — signals his ambition to transform the Empire into a hereditary monarchy.

1810: The Napoleonic Empire at Its Height

The Empire reaches its maximum territorial extent: France now has 130 départements, from Hamburg to Rome. Napoleon marries Marie-Louise of Austria (March–April 1810), the daughter of Francis I — a Habsburg marriage that gives the Empire the dynastic legitimacy it has lacked. A son, the King of Rome, is born in March 1811.

But the weaknesses of the Napoleonic system are already growing. The Continental System cannot be enforced: smuggling is rampant, Portugal refuses to comply and is occupied by French troops, and Russia chafes under the trade restrictions. The Peninsular War continues without resolution; Wellington’s Anglo-Portuguese army, entrenched at the Lines of Torres Vedras, cannot be dislodged. The French garrison in Spain consumes men and resources at an accelerating rate.

1811: The Gathering Storm

The year is one of preparation and tension. Napoleon prepares for war with Russia, whose withdrawal from the Continental System is now open. The Grande Armée is assembled on the Vistula — more than 600,000 men, including contingents from all of Napoleon’s client states. In Spain, Wellington defeats Masséna at Fuentes de Oñoro and Albuera; the French position in the peninsula deteriorates steadily. The economic effects of the Continental System — shortages, inflation, unemployment — produce unrest across the Empire.

1812: The Russian Campaign

Napoleon invades Russia in June 1812 with the largest army Europe has ever seen — the Grande Armée of more than 600,000 men. The Russian army falls back, avoiding battle and drawing the French deeper into the vastness of the country. The Battle of Borodino (7 September) is a bloody French tactical victory — 70,000 casualties — but the Russian army survives. Napoleon enters Moscow on 14 September to find the city abandoned and set ablaze by its own governor, Rostopchin.

The Tsar refuses to negotiate. Napoleon waits in the smouldering city for five weeks, then orders the retreat (19 October). The autumn rains turn to winter cold; the retreat from Moscow becomes a catastrophe of cold, hunger, and harassing attacks by Cossacks and partisans. Of the 600,000 who crossed the Niemen in June, perhaps 40,000 recross it in December. The Grande Armée has ceased to exist.

The disaster of 1812 is the turning point of the Napoleonic Wars. The Berlin Decree and the continental-system lie in ruins; the Tsar is determined to destroy Napoleon; the armies of the Sixth Coalition are gathering.

1813: The War of Liberation

The year sees the formation of the Sixth Coalition: Russia, Prussia, Austria (after the failure of Napoleon’s peace offers), Sweden, Britain, and the smaller German states. Napoleon raises a new army — young conscripts, the Marie-Louises — and demonstrates that he is still the greatest commander in Europe.

The spring campaign (Lützen, Bautzen) is inconclusive. An armistice (June–August) fails to produce a peace. The autumn campaign reaches its climax at the Battle of the Nations at Leipzig (16–19 October), where Napoleon, outnumbered and outflanked, is defeated by the combined armies of Russia, Prussia, Austria, and Sweden. He retreats across the Rhine; the Confederation of the Rhine collapses; the Empire west of the Rhine is lost.

In Spain, Wellington defeats the French at Vitoria (21 June), driving the French out of the peninsula and crossing the Pyrenees into France. By the end of the year, France is invaded on all sides.

1814: The Fall of the Empire

The campaign in France (January–March) is Napoleon’s last demonstration of military genius — a defensive campaign of manoeuvre against overwhelming odds, fought with a handful of veterans and raw conscripts. He wins a series of victories (Brienne, Champaubert, Montmirail, Montereau, Craonne) but cannot prevent the advance of the allied armies. The Austrians, under Schwarzenberg, advance up the Seine valley; the Prussians, under Blücher, advance along the Marne; the British, under Wellington, advance from the south.

On 30 March, Paris capitulates. The Senate and the rump legislature, under Talleyrand’s direction, depose Napoleon on 2 April. On 6 April, Napoleon abdicates at Fontainebleau. The Treaty of Fontainebleau (11 April) exiles him to Elba — a Mediterranean island of which he is made sovereign.

Louis XVIII, the brother of the executed king, enters Paris on 3 May and assumes the throne under the Charter of 1814 — a constitutional monarchy that retains many of the Revolution’s reforms. The the-emigres return, but the settlement is fragile: the army remains loyal to Napoleon, the people are burdened by occupation and economic hardship, and the allies are watchful.

1815: The Hundred Days and Waterloo

Napoleon escapes from Elba on 26 February and lands at Golfe-Juan on 1 March. His march to Paris — the Vol de l’Aigle — gathers force as regiments sent to arrest him join his standard. The Bourbon court flees; Louis XVIII departs for Ghent. Napoleon enters Paris on 20 March and resumes power without firing a shot.

He attempts to negotiate peace, but the allies declare him an outlaw and prepare for war. Napoleon raises another army — perhaps 280,000 men — and strikes at the allied armies in Belgium before they can concentrate. The Waterloo campaign (June) is a desperate gamble.

On 16 June, Napoleon defeats the Prussians at Ligny but cannot destroy them; Marshal Ney fights an indecisive engagement at Quatre-Bras against Wellington. On 18 June, at the Battle of Waterloo, Napoleon attacks Wellington’s position on the Mont-Saint-Jean ridge. The French assault — including the massive cavalry charges against British squares and the final attack of the Imperial Guard — fails to break the Anglo-Allied line. The Prussians, under Blücher, arrive in the late afternoon and turn the battle into a rout. Napoleon abandons his army and returns to Paris.

On 22 June, he abdicates for the second time. The Chamber of Representatives appoints a Provisional Government; Napoleon attempts to flee to America but is blockaded at Rochefort. He surrenders to Captain Maitland of HMS Bellerophon on 15 July, and is exiled to Saint Helena, where he will die in 1821.

Louis XVIII is restored for the second time; the Second Treaty of Paris imposes a heavy indemnity and an occupation of five years. The Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars — a quarter-century of conflict — are over.
