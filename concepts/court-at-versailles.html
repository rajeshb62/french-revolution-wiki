The Court at Versailles

The Living Machine

The court at Versailles under Louis XVI was not a museum of bygone splendour, nor was it merely the king’s residence. It was the central nervous system of the French monarchy — a vast, intricate, and unceasingly active institution that governed how power was distributed, how patronage flowed, how the nobility justified its privileges, and how the Bourbon dynasty presented itself to the world. Between 1774, when the young king ascended the throne, and October 1789, when the Parisian mob dragged the royal family back to the Tuileries, the court functioned according to a rhythm and logic that had been refined over more than a century. It was a world of relentless ceremony, ferocious ambition, private pleasures, and public display — and it was dying even as it danced.

The palace itself was the most visible expression of this system. Built to house the entire apparatus of governance and to cage the nobility in plain sight of the sovereign, the château of Versailles contained within its walls not only the royal apartments but the offices of ministers and secretaries of state, the military household, the stables, the kitchens, the chapel, the theatre, and the endless galleries and antechambers where courtiers spent their lives waiting — waiting for an audience, for a favour, for a pension, for a word from the king that might change their fortunes. The town of Versailles, which had grown up around the palace, housed the thousands of functionaries, servants, merchants, and hangers-on whose existence depended on the court’s appetite.

The physical scale was staggering. The main château stretched nearly a quarter-mile along the terrace, its five thousand rooms spread over four floors. The Marble Court, the Royal Court, the Ministers’ Wings — each space had a precise social gradient, from the public outer courts where any well-dressed person might wander, to the Oeil de Boeuf antechamber where only those with the grandes entrées might penetrate, to the intimate apartments of the king and queen where only the family and a handful of privileged servants might enter. The park stretched for miles, its groves and fountains designed for promenades, hunts, and clandestine rendezvous. Beyond the park lay the Grand Trianon, a palace of rose marble built for private retreat, and further still the Petit Trianon, the queen’s domain. At the Petit Trianon, Marie-Antoinette had created the Hamlet — a rustic village of thatched cottages, a working farm, a mill, and a lake — where she could escape the suffocating formality of the main court and play at being a milkmaid with her closest friends.

The Etiquette System

Every moment of the day at Versailles was governed by a code of precedence and procedure so detailed that it bordered on the liturgical. The etiquette system, inherited from Louis XIV and refined by a century of practice, determined who might hand the king his shirt in the morning, who might sit on a tabouret in the queen’s presence, who might accompany the royal hunt, and who might embrace the queen when she was enceinte. It was not mere fussiness: in a world where favour and fortune depended entirely on proximity to power, the procedures that controlled access were the very mechanisms of distribution.

The king’s day began with the lever, a highly structured ceremony that unfolded in stages. At the appointed hour, the first valet de chambre woke the king. The petit lever admitted only those with the entrées de la chambre — the princes of the blood, the highest officers of the crown, the gentlemen who held the most coveted positions. As the king was washed and shaved, these privileged courtiers might exchange a few words with him, present a petition, or simply stand in attendance, their presence a visible claim on the monarch’s attention. The grand lever followed, opening the doors to a wider circle: the military officers on duty, the gentlemen of the wardrobe, the physicians, the courtiers of sufficient rank. The king dressed, each garment handed to him by the officer whose privilege it was to hold that particular piece of clothing. The shirt was the great prize — only the highest-ranking prince present had the right to present it.

The queen’s lever was similarly elaborate, though Marie-Antoinette came to chafe against its rigours. Madame Campan, her première femme de chambre, recorded the precise order in which the queen’s toilette unfolded. The princesses of the blood and the duchesses had the right to attend, to hand the queen her shift, her handkerchief, her fan. The entrées to the queen’s toilette were jealously guarded; to be admitted was a mark of favour, to be excluded a public slight. Marie-Antoinette, increasingly impatient with these formalities, would sometimes slip away to the Petit Trianon precisely to avoid them — a retreat that the court, and eventually the public, read as an affront to the established order.

The grand couvert was the public meal, held in the king’s antechamber, where any properly dressed person might watch the royal family eat. It was not a private affair but a performance of kingship — the king’s appetite, his table manners, his casual remarks to those standing nearby were all observed and reported back to Paris. The Sunday chapel was a major ceremonial event, the royal family processing through the galleries in full court dress, the king taking his place in the royal tribune while the courtiers filled the nave below. The hunt was perhaps the most important social event of the court calendar: the king rode out with a vast retinue of mounted nobles, and the invitation to join the hunt was one of the surest marks of favour. Louis XVI, whose passion for hunting bordered on the obsessive, spent more time in the saddle than he did in council.

Presentations at court followed a formidable ritual. A noblewoman presented for the first time had to be sponsored by a lady already presented, had to wear the prescribed costume — the grand panier hoopskirt, the elaborate headdress, the train of precisely regulated length — and had to execute a series of reverences before the king and queen, backing out of the presence without once turning her back. A single misstep could ruin a family’s hopes for years. The honneurs de la cour, the right to be presented, belonged only to families who could prove their nobility back to 1400 — a requirement that excluded the newer nobility of the robe and kept the court a preserve of the ancient sword nobility.

The Factions

The court was a battlefield of factions, their alignments shifting with the marriages, deaths, and disgraces that punctuated court life. Under Louis XVI, the most significant division was between the party of the old Choiseul faction and the Polignacs, who rose with the queen’s favour.

The Duc de Choiseul, minister under Louis XV, had been the great patron of a generation of nobles, military officers, and philosophers. Though disgraced in 1770, his network survived him, clustering around the hope of his return. The Choiseul party — or what remained of it — was associated with the reformist, anti-clerical, pro-Austrian policies of the previous reign. They looked to the king’s minister Maurepas with suspicion and to the rising Polignac circle with outright hostility.

The Polignac faction was the queen’s party. The Comtesse Jules de Polignac, a woman of charm, beauty, and exquisite manners, had become the queen’s favourite in the late 1770s, displacing the Princesse de Lamballe, who held the revived office of Surintendante of the Queen’s Household. Campan describes the Polignacs as a clan rather than a couple — the countess, her husband, her brother-in-law the Comte d’Artois’s governor, and most notably the Vicomte de Vaudreuil, a brilliant and dangerous figure who dominated the circle. The queen showered the Polignacs with favours: the comtesse received a pension of 400,000 livres, her husband became the premier écuyer, their friends and relatives received sinecures, offices, and benefices. The court groaned under the weight of this obvious partiality. Besenval, a court insider who observed the factional struggles from close range, noted that the queen’s preference for the Polignacs over the Lamballe circle created a lasting wound in the court’s fabric — and, by extension, in the monarchy’s moral authority.

The Mesdames — Louis XV’s daughters, Adélaïde, Victoire, Sophie, and Louise — formed a separate, devout, influential bloc. They had survived their father’s reign and the ascendancy of Madame du Barry; they now occupied a secure place in the court hierarchy, wielding quiet but persistent influence over ecclesiastical appointments and devotional policy. They lived in a separate wing of the château, maintaining their own ceremonial and their own networks. To the queen, they were figures of respect but also of constraint — they represented the old piety, the old formality, the old way of being royal, which the young queen’s innovations seemed to threaten.

The princes of the blood — notably the Comte de Provence (the king’s brother, later Louis XVIII), the Comte d’Artois (later Charles X), and the Duc d’Orléans — were themselves centres of faction. Provence was clever, ambitious, and, it was widely suspected, waiting for his brother to stumble. Artois was frivolous, spendthrift, and devoted to the queen’s social circle. Orléans was worst of all — a prince of the blood who had been alienated by the queen’s coldness and who cultivated popularity among the Parisian bourgeoisie, frequenting the Palais-Royal and positioning himself as an alternative to the king’s authority. The queen, Campan notes, always kept Orléans at arm’s length; the estrangement became a political weapon in his hands.

Daily Life at Court

The king’s routine was that of a methodical, private, somewhat melancholy man. Louis XVI rose early, heard mass, attended council on appointed days, dined in public, hunted, and spent his evenings in his private apartments where he pursued his two great passions: locksmithing and geography. He had a forge set up in the mezzanine above his cabinet, where he worked with iron and tools, often in the company of his locksmith, Gamin. The court found this habit undignified; pamphleteers would eventually use it as evidence of the king’s unfitness — a monarch who preferred tinkering with locks to governing his kingdom. But for Louis XVI, the forge was a refuge from a world he never quite mastered.

The queen’s life followed a different rhythm. Marie-Antoinette had been a child of fourteen when she came to France; as queen, she threw herself into pleasure with an energy that the court alternately admired and condemned. Her days were filled with gaming — faro, lansquenet, cavagnole — often until the early hours of the morning. She attended the opera and the theatre, both at the court and in Paris. She gave balls, masked balls, private concerts. She filled the Petit Trianon with a society of her choosing, a closed circle that excluded many of the highest-ranking courtiers and that became, in the public imagination, the scene of nameless debauchery. The Queen’s Hamlet was the final expression of this need for escape — a fantasy of rural simplicity, complete with a working dairy, a dovecote, a mill, and a lake, where the queen and her friends could dress as peasants and forget that they were the centre of the most scrutinised court in Europe.

For the ordinary courtier — the six hundred families who were expected to maintain a presence at Versailles — life was a perpetual, grinding pursuit of favour. To be noticed by the king, to be greeted by the queen, to receive a pension, a benefice, a regiment, a governorship, a ribbon of an order — these were the prizes for which a family might devote generations. The cost was enormous. A courtier needed an apartment in the château or a hôtel in the town, a carriage, servants in livery, costumes for ceremony after ceremony, and the endless round of gifts, bribes, and gambling losses that oiled the social machine. Many nobles ruined themselves in the attempt. Those who succeeded found themselves no more than glorified attendants, standing in galleries for hours, hoping for a nod.

Montesquieu had written that the court was the natural element of the monarchy, the place where honour was the principle of government. But by the 1780s, the court had become synonymous with waste and frivolity. The pensions paid to courtiers consumed a staggering portion of the royal budget. The Polignac circle alone absorbed millions of livres. The queen’s building projects at Trianon, her theatricals, her gambling — each was a drop in a bucket that was already overflowing with the debts of the American war.

The Political Function

Despite its reputation for triviality, the court remained the seat of government. The Council of State met in the king’s cabinet at specified hours — the Conseil d’En Haut for the most sensitive matters of state, the Conseil des Dépêches for internal administration, the Conseil Royal des Finances for the budget that was always on the edge of crisis. Ministers came to Versailles to report, to receive instructions, to negotiate with the king and, increasingly, with the queen. It was here that Necker was summoned, dismissed, and recalled; here that Calonne presented his doomed plan for tax reform; here that the Assembly of Notables met in 1787, hoping to stave off the financial collapse that would trigger the Revolution.

Ambassadors were received in the Salon de la Paix or the Salon d’Apollon, where the throne was placed on a dais beneath a ceiling painting of the Sun God. The reception of ambassadors was a piece of theatre designed to display French power to the world — the king seated, the ambassador approaching through a double line of courtiers, the ceremonial bowing, the exchange of speeches in a language that both sides understood to be about the relative standing of their sovereigns.

Patronage was distributed through the court, and patronage was the substance of politics. A minister who lost the king’s ear ceased to be able to reward his clients; a minister who could not reward his clients lost his faction; a faction that lost its cohesion could not govern. The court was therefore the mechanism through which the entire edifice of the French state was held together — and the mechanism through which it was finally torn apart. When the king could no longer satisfy the demands of those who depended on him for place and pension, the loyalty of the nobility began to fray.

The Breakdown

The fiscal crisis of the 1780s exposed the court to a scrutiny that it could not survive. The deficit, the failure of successive ministers to reform the tax system, the summoning of the Estates-General — each blow diminished the court’s legitimacy. The nobility had been expected to sacrifice their privileges to save the state; they had refused. The court’s profligacy was now held up as evidence that the monarchy itself was unreformable. The pamphlets that flooded Paris — Les Nuits de Versailles, the endless libelles against Marie-Antoinette — turned the court into a symbol of everything that was rotten in France.

The October Days of 1789 were the end. On 5 October, a crowd of Parisian women — and men in women’s clothing, and National Guardsmen — marched on Versailles, demanding bread and the king’s return to Paris. The château was invaded; the queen’s apartments were stormed; two guardsmen were killed and their heads paraded on pikes. Lafayette, who commanded the National Guard, persuaded the king to return to Paris. On the morning of 6 October, the royal family climbed into a carriage and began the slow journey to the Tuileries. Behind them, the vast, empty palace stood as a monument to a world that had already ended.

The court would never return.
