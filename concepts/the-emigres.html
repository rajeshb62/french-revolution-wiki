The Émigrés

The Flight of the Princes

On the night of July 16, 1789, two days after the fall of the Bastille, the comte d’Artois — the younger brother of Louis XVI and the most hated man in Paris after the queen — slipped out of Versailles with a handful of companions. The king himself had ordered his departure, fearing for his life. Accompanied by the prince d’Hénin, the comte de Vaudreuil, and a few gentlemen of his household, Artois rode first to Chantilly, where the prince de Condé’s carriage awaited them, then pressed on toward Valenciennes and the frontier. Behind him came Condé himself with his son the duc de Bourbon and his grandson the duc d’Enghien. Behind them poured the great families of the court: the Lauzuns, the Villequiers, the Durases, the Polignacs, the Broglies — all fleeing what they took to be a passing storm, convinced they would be home within three months.

“Nous rentrerons dans trois mois,” Artois told the comte Eszterhazy, the governor of Valenciennes, with characteristic insouciance. It was the universal conviction of the first émigrés. None paused to settle their affairs or secure resources beyond a few weeks. They departed as if for a country excursion, in carriages and fiacres, with their mistresses and their jewel-cases, certain that the revolutionary tempest would soon blow over and that the old order would reassert itself with a minimum of fuss.

From Valenciennes, Artois made his way to Turin, the capital of his father-in-law Victor-Amédée III, king of Sardinia. The journey took him through Brussels — where the Austrian governor, the archduchess Marie-Christine, ordered him to move on at her brother Joseph II’s command, lest his presence provoke the French government — and across Germany and Switzerland. By mid-September he was installed at Moncalieri, the Sardinian summer residence, with his wife and children, the three Condés, and a suite of eighty-two persons. The court in exile had begun.

Even as Artois settled in Piedmont, the emigration gathered momentum. The October Days — the march on Versailles and the forcible transfer of the royal family to the Tuileries — unleashed a new wave. The duc d’Orléans fled to England; nobles from Provence and Dauphiné poured into Chambéry and Nice. After the Civil Constitution of the Clergy in 1790, priests and bishops swelled the exodus. Entire families, from the greatest lords down to the shopkeepers and seamstresses who had served them, took to the roads, choking the post-roads from Paris to the frontiers, begging for horses at every relay, camping in barns and village inns, sustained by the illusion that their exile would be brief.

Turin and the Concert of Europe

At Turin, the comte d’Artois expected that the crowned heads of Europe would fly to his brother’s aid. He was quickly disabused. The courts of Europe had their own calculations, and the French Revolution, for all its alarming novelty, was not yet seen as a mortal threat to the monarchical order.

The emperor Joseph II, absorbed in his projects of aggrandizement in the east, regarded the weakening of France with something close to satisfaction. His successor Leopold thought similarly: neither would move against the Revolution except on terms that gave Austria territorial advantages. Prussia, under Frederick William II, was fixated on Poland and on keeping Austria in check; the French imbroglio was a distraction. William Pitt’s England, still recovering from the American war and focused on commercial recovery, remained studiously neutral. Catherine the Great, though she thundered against revolutionary ideas and took sanitary measures to keep French propaganda out of Russia, was consumed by the war with Turkey and the final partitions of Poland. Only Gustavus III of Sweden, a romantic enthusiast for royal legitimacy, offered spontaneous support — but Sweden was distant and weak.

The one power that might have acted, Spain, where a Bourbon still reigned, proved maddeningly elusive. The comte d’Artois bombarded Madrid with appeals; Florida Blanca, the Spanish minister, responded with polite silence. The prince de Condé, whose correspondence with his agent Larouzière provides the most intimate record of these months, wrote in February 1790: “We will not have a single foreign soldier if Spain is not at the head of everything.”

To direct the diplomacy of the emigration, Artois turned to Calonne, the former controller-general of finances, who had installed himself in London. Calonne was the fatal man of the emigration — presumptuous, credulous, convinced of his own infallibility. He dictated the first approaches to Vienna and Berlin, both of which failed. The emperor’s answer, Condé reported, was “affreuse, repoussante, insultante même.” The Prussian response was hardly better.

Money, meanwhile, was already running out. The comte d’Artois had arrived in Turin with no more than pocket-money. The king of Sardinia would feed and lodge his daughter and son-in-law, but he would not finance a war against France. Loans were sought from the treasury of the Grande Chartreuse and the rich abbeys; nothing came of them. The year 1789 ended with the princes’ treasury empty and their diplomacy barren.

Worse still, the first seeds of discord between the émigrés and the king they professed to serve had already been sown. From the Tuileries, Louis XVI and Marie-Antoinette watched their brother’s activities with mounting alarm. Every letter from Turin — and there were many, for the émigrés observed no discretion — seemed designed to compromise the royal family further. “Le comte d’Artois excédé de toutes les lettres qu’il reçoit,” Condé wrote in January 1790. The king reproached; the queen pleaded. From Turin, the response was defiance: “Secourez-les malgré eux,” as Madame Élisabeth herself wrote. “Labourer la terre, gagner mon pain à la sueur de mon front, périr enfin, fût-ce de misère,” Artois wrote to the bailli de Crussol, “voilà ce que je préférerai toujours à un accommodement quelconque.”

Coblentz and the Army of the Princes

By the summer of 1791, the center of gravity of the emigration had shifted from Turin to the Rhineland. In June, Louis XVI’s failed flight to Varennes demonstrated — if demonstration were needed — that the king was a prisoner and that a counter-revolution could not wait upon his pleasure. The comte de Provence, the king’s elder brother, who had escaped separately and successfully, reached Brussels and then joined the émigré gathering on the Rhine. The electoral court of the archbishop of Trier, at Coblentz, became the headquarters of the emigration.

It was at Coblentz that the émigré enterprise assumed its most characteristically theatrical form. The princes established a court in miniature, with chancelleries, uniforms, regiments, and a ceaseless traffic in deputations, intrigues, and memoranda. The Army of the Princes — the émigré military force — was assembled, its officers drawn from the noblesse of France, its rank and file from a miscellany of German adventurers, Swiss mercenaries, and discharged soldiers. Its uniforms were magnificent; its discipline was not.

The politics of Coblentz were those of intransigence. The princes and their circle — Calonne among them — insisted on the complete restoration of the old order: the monarchy of 1788, the privileges of the nobility and clergy, the abolition of everything the Revolution had done. Any talk of compromise, of a constitutional monarchy on the English model, was treated as treason. The “monarchiens” — Malouet, Mounier, Lally-Tollendal, Montlosier, Cazalès — who had tried to steer the Revolution toward a moderate settlement, were regarded by the Coblentz émigrés with a hatred scarcely less intense than that reserved for the Jacobins. D’Antraigues, the most violent of the ultras, declared: “Je ferai tomber cent mille têtes — et la sienne la première,” speaking of Montlosier.

From Coblentz, the princes conducted their diplomacy. Austria, Prussia, and the smaller German states were canvassed for support. The Declaration of Pillnitz, issued in August 1791 by Leopold II and Frederick William II, was the result — a cautiously worded statement that the restoration of order in France was “a matter of common interest to all sovereigns of Europe,” but hedged with conditions that made immediate action impossible. The émigrés read it as a blank cheque; it was nothing of the kind.

Nevertheless, by the spring of 1792, the coalition had taken shape. Austria and Prussia agreed on war. The Brunswick Manifesto — the famous declaration threatening Paris with “exemplary and never-to-be-forgotten vengeance” if the royal family were harmed — was drawn up by a French émigré, the marquis de Limon, and issued on July 25, 1792. It was intended to terrify the Revolution into submission. It had precisely the opposite effect. The invasion that followed, under the duke of Brunswick, was a series of humiliations: the cannonade of Valmy on September 20, the retreat of the Prussian army, the dissolution of the invasion as a fighting force. The Army of the Princes, which had accompanied the main force, melted away. The first military effort of the emigration was over, and in its wake came the September Massacres in Paris, the fall of the monarchy, and the establishment of the Republic.

The émigrés scattered. The Army of the Princes, reconstituted under the prince de Condé and maintained first by Austria and then by England, continued to fight — but always as a subsidiary force, a political ornament to the campaigns of the great powers rather than an independent military instrument. Condé’s army would see action on the Rhine in 1793, in the disastrous campaign of 1796, and again in 1799, but it never again approached French territory in force. It was a symbol, a standard around which the fading hopes of the royalist cause gathered, but a standard that receded ever further from the frontiers of France.

Hamm and Verona: The King in Exile

On January 21, 1793, Louis XVI was guillotined. The comte de Provence, who had been living quietly at Hamm in Westphalia since his escape from France, proclaimed himself Regent for the imprisoned Louis XVII. When the child-king died in the Temple in June 1795 — from neglect, disease, or worse — the comte de Provence declared himself Louis XVIII, king of France. The emigration now had a sovereign, but one without a throne, without an army, and without a territory.

The first years of Louis XVIII’s reign in exile were spent at Verona, in the territories of the Republic of Venice. There he established a court, appointed ministers — the comte d’Avaray as his closest confidant, the duc de La Vauguyon as foreign minister — and issued his first proclamation to the French people. It was uncompromising: a return to the “antique constitution” of the kingdom, the restoration of the three orders, the maintenance of the Catholic religion as the religion of state, and severe punishment for the regicides. “I abandon to all the severity of the judgments they deserve,” the declaration read, “all those who, when I pardon, would abandon themselves to sentiments of vengeance.” But it excepted from clemency “the assassins of the king my brother, of the queen, of Madame Élisabeth.”

This was not the language of a prince seeking reconciliation with his people. The émigré court at Verona was consumed — as it would be for the next twenty years — by two obsessions: the problem of finance, which was never solved, and the problem of the comte d’Artois, which was more intractable still.

Quiberon

The most tragic episode of the emigration was Quiberon. In the summer of 1795, the British government — eager to exploit the royalist insurrection in the Vendée and Brittany — launched a combined naval and military expedition to land émigré troops on the Breton coast. The plan had been pressed by the comte de Puisaye, a former constitutionalist turned royalist organizer, who had won the confidence of the British war minister Windham. The comte d’Artois was to follow with a second wave; the Breton chouans, under Charette and Stofflet, were to rise in concert.

On June 27, 1795, an émigré force of some three thousand men landed at Carnac, on the Quiberon peninsula. A second landing at Auray, under the chevalier de Tinténiac, penetrated inland. For a few weeks, the royalists held the peninsula. Then the republican army under General Hoche arrived.

On July 16, the royalist sortie from the peninsula was crushed at Sainte-Barbe. The survivors fell back into the trap of the peninsula itself, hemmed between Hoche’s advancing columns and the sea. On the night of July 20-21, during a violent storm, republican grenadiers, guided by deserters from the royalist ranks, scaled the cliffs of the fort of Penthièvre and seized it in a coup de main. The peninsula was overrun. The British squadron, under Admiral Warren, could do little but pick up survivors from the water.

The scene at Port-Halliguen, at the tip of the peninsula, was one of unspeakable horror. Officers, soldiers, and peasants — men, women, and children — threw themselves into the sea, trying to reach the English boats that the violence of the waves prevented from approaching the shore. Hundreds drowned. Others were pulled into overloaded boats, whose first passengers beat back those who tried to climb aboard. Some, seeing no escape, threw themselves from the cliffs or fell on their own swords.

Sombreuil, the young general who had commanded one of the émigré regiments, held out with his legion on the rocks of the Fort-Neuf until surrender was the only option. Hoche, who admired his bravery, promised — or was understood to promise — that the prisoners would be treated as prisoners of war. Sombreuil handed over his sword after kissing the blade. But the Convention, which had sent commissioners to the scene, was in no mood for clemency. Seven hundred and fifty-one émigrés were executed by military commissions in the weeks that followed. Sombreuil himself was shot at Vannes on July 28, along with the bishop of Dol and thirteen priests.

The British squadron, carrying the survivors — among them Puisaye himself, who had made his way to the flagship Pomone early in the battle — sailed for England. Puisaye, who had abandoned his men, wrote to Windham blaming the émigrés for the disaster: “The greatest number, especially those paid too dearly by you, did not bring among us the spirit one might have expected after six years of exile and misfortune.” Calonne, writing from Southampton, was more measured but no less damning: “A lack of vigilance, which does great harm to M. de Puisaye’s reputation, delivered them to treachery.”

The comte d’Artois, who had been expected to lead the second wave, arrived at Portsmouth on August 7 to learn that the expedition had been crushed. He was kept in England; the British government, sobered by the catastrophe, abandoned further landing attempts. From the island of Yeu, where he was briefly stationed, Artois attempted to coordinate with Charette, but the opportunity had passed. Charette was captured and executed the following year. “Sire,” he was supposed to have written to Louis XVIII, “the cowardice of your brother has lost everything.” Whether he wrote those exact words is uncertain, but the sentiment was widely shared.

Émigré Politics under the Directory and Consulate

The failure of Quiberon marked the end of the émigrés’ military hopes. From 1795 onward, the struggle became one of conspiracy, diplomacy, and the slow, grinding war of influence at Europe’s courts.

Louis XVIII was expelled from Verona in April 1796 at the demand of the Directory, which threatened Venice with French invasion. He fled to the Army of Condé at Riégel, in Baden, but the Austrians would not tolerate his presence there. He searched for a fixed residence — first at Blankenberg, in the duchy of Brunswick, where he stayed from 1796 to 1798; then at Mitau, in the Russian Baltic, to which Tsar Paul I invited him in 1798. There he remained until Paul’s murder in 1801, when Alexander I, the new tsar, reluctantly asked him to leave under French pressure. He spent the next years in Warsaw, then at the invitation of Frederick William III of Prussia at Memel, and finally, from 1807, at Hartwell in England.

These were years of endless negotiation, of agents sent and missions failed, of plots discovered and betrayed. The royalist network inside France — the “Agence de Paris,” directed by Brottier, Duverne de Praile, and Despomelles — was uncovered and broken in January 1797. The arrest of its leaders in the École Militaire, after a colonel named Malo whom they had tried to recruit betrayed them to the Directory, was a devastating blow. In its wake came the coup of 18 Fructidor (September 4, 1797), which purged the government of royalist sympathizers, annulled elections, and reimposed the Terror by other means. All the patient work of infiltration and subversion — the cultivation of the moderate deputies, the secret correspondence with generals like Pichegru — was undone in a single stroke.

The conspiracy of the Rue Saint-Nicaise, a bomb attack on Napoleon as he drove to the Opéra on Christmas Eve 1800, was the work of royalist plotters from the west. It failed, killing several bystanders but leaving Napoleon unhurt. The response was ferocious: the deportation of 130 Jacobins implausibly blamed for the attack, the execution of the real conspirators, and the intensification of police surveillance over the surviving royalist underground.

The Cadoudal-Pichegru conspiracy of 1803-1804 was the most ambitious attempt to assassinate the First Consul and restore the Bourbons. Georges Cadoudal, the Breton chouan leader, crossed the Channel with a band of accomplices; General Pichegru, the former commander of the republican Army of the Rhine who had turned royalist, returned from exile to join him. The plot extended to the highest levels: the comte d’Artois was to appear on the frontiers of France the moment Napoleon was eliminated. But the police, directed by Fouché, had penetrated the conspiracy from the start. Cadoudal was captured and executed; Pichegru was found strangled in his prison cell — whether by his own hand or by agents of the state was never clearly established. The due d’Enghien, the young Bourbon prince whom Napoleon suspected of involvement, was seized from neutral Baden, tried by military commission, and shot in the ditch of the Château de Vincennes. It was the most controversial act of Napoleon’s career, and it sent a shudder through every royalist court in Europe.

Mitau, Warsaw, Hartwell

The assassination of the due d’Enghien convinced Louis XVIII that his cause could not be advanced by conspiracy alone. From Hartwell, the modest English estate where he installed himself in 1807, he pivoted to a strategy of patience. The European war would, he believed, eventually produce circumstances favorable to the Bourbon restoration — if only he could survive, maintain the unity of his family, and avoid the fatal lure of premature action.

The court at Hartwell was a curious affair: a little Versailles in Buckinghamshire, complete with the arcana of etiquette, the distribution of empty honors, and the perpetual struggle for the king’s favor. The comte d’Avaray, Louis’s devoted companion, was the most trusted figure — until his health collapsed and he was forced to retire to Madeira in 1810, dying there the following year. The comte de Blacas succeeded him as the king’s right hand. The correspondence between Blacas and Joseph de Maistre, the Savoyard philosopher who served as the king’s eyes and ears in St. Petersburg, provides the most vivid record of these years: the intellectual excitement of the European struggle, the long intervals of despair, the gradual, almost imperceptible accumulation of hope.

The Russian campaign of 1812 transformed everything. Napoleon’s Grande Armée was destroyed in the snows. The prisoners-of-war taken by the Cossacks, the accounts of cannibalism in the retreat, the burning of Moscow — all of it was reported to Hartwell by de Maistre in letters of savage joy. “Bonaparte n’a plus d’armée,” he wrote on November 13, 1812. But the Allies, though they defeated Napoleon in Germany in 1813, were in no hurry to restore the Bourbons. The emissaries Louis XVIII dispatched to the camp of the sovereigns — Alexis de Noailles, the comte de La Ferronnays, the comte de Bouillé — were received with courtesy and given nothing. Alexander I was sympathetic but non-committal; Metternich was hostile; Bernadotte, the former revolutionary general who now ruled Sweden, was pursuing his own ambitions. “Le moment n’est pas encore propice,” was the invariable reply.

The Return

The moment came at last in the spring of 1814. The Allies entered Paris on March 31. The due d’Angoulême — the comte d’Artois’s elder son — landed at Bordeaux, where the city received him with acclamations. The comte d’Artois himself entered Paris as lieutenant-general of the kingdom. On April 2, the Senate proclaimed the deposition of Napoleon; on April 6, it offered the crown to Louis XVIII, on condition that he accept a constitution.

Louis XVIII landed at Calais on April 25, 1814 — twenty-four years after he had fled France, a fugitive. He entered Paris on May 3, passed through crowds that shouted “Vive le roi!” from the same streets that had roared for the guillotine a generation earlier. The emigration was over.

But it had left its mark. The years of exile had not softened the émigrés. They returned — those who survived — with their grievances intact, their estates sold, their châteaux in ruins, their world turned inside out. The comte d’Artois had learned nothing and forgotten nothing. The prince de Condé was old and broken, mourning the wife he had married at last after forty-five years of devotion. The Army of the Princes was a memory. The great names of the emigration — the Polignacs, the La Trémoïlles, the Noailles — came back demanding restitution, demanding revenge, demanding the restoration of everything the Revolution had taken.

Louis XVIII, wiser than his followers, knew that such a restoration was impossible. “Bien rétablie — cela dépend,” he said to the duc de Duras on the eve of his departure from England. He gave France the Charter of 1814, which preserved the essential achievements of the Revolution — equality before the law, religious liberty, the Napoleonic Code — while restoring the monarchy. It was a compromise, and like all compromises, it satisfied no one fully. The émigrés of the old school thought it a betrayal; the liberals thought it insufficient. The king walked a narrow path between them, and it was not enough to save his dynasty. But for a moment, in the spring of 1814, the long exile was over. The Bourbons had come home.
