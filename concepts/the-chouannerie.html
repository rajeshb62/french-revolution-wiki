The Chouannerie

I. Two Faces of the Same Resistance

The Chouannerie was never one thing. It was two — a southern movement and a western one, separated by geography, by chronology, and by circumstance, yet united by a single thread: the refusal of rural France to accept the Revolution. Ernest Daudet, in two works composed fourteen years apart, traced this double history. His Histoire des Conspirations Royalistes du Midi (1881) examined the southern variant from 1790 to 1793 — the Camp de Jalès, the Saillans conspiracy, the Charrier uprising — a movement driven by religious hatred and aristocratic intrigue in the sun-baked valleys of the Gard, the Ardèche, and the Lozère. His La Police et les Chouans sous le Consulat et l’Empire (1895) took up the western variant from 1800 to 1815 — the Clément de Ris kidnapping, the Aché-De Combray conspiracy, the English agencies, the final rising of the Hundred Days — a guerrilla war fought in the misty hedgerows of Brittany, Normandy, and Maine against the police state of Napoleon Bonaparte. Between them, these two books trace a continuous arc of rural royalist resistance that ran from the first stirrings of counter-revolution in 1790 to the last campfires of 1815.

II. The Midi: Religion and Royalism in the South (1790–1793)

The southern Chouannerie was born not of peasant loyalty to the seigneur but of fire and blood between Catholics and Protestants. In the Midi, the Revolution had reopened wounds that had been healing for a century. The Protestants of Nîmes, Montauban, and the Cévennes embraced the Revolution as their deliverance from Catholic domination; the Catholics, in turn, saw the Revolution as a Protestant conspiracy against throne and altar. The result was massacre.

On 13 June 1790, violence erupted in Nîmes. Protestant National Guards, armed and organised under revolutionary authority, fell upon the Catholic quarter. Houses were pillaged and burned, hundreds of Catholics were killed in the streets, and the municipal authority collapsed. The Catholic notables fled the city, and the survivors were left with nothing but the memory of the slaughter and a thirst for vengeance. It was this thirst that filled the first Camp de Jalès.

In the summer of 1790, in the solitary plain of Jalès in the Ardèche, the royalists of the Midi attempted what the Vendée would later achieve: to raise a mass army for the king. The pretext was a federal gathering of the National Guards of the Vivarais, summoned to renew the civic oath. But the National Guards who assembled around an altar erected near the château of Jalès heard mass, and passed resolutions that amounted to a declaration of war on the Revolution. A permanent Committee of the Camp of Jalès was established, composed of elected delegates, which from that day coordinated the royalist movement in the South.

The first assembly of Jalès dispersed without action. The conspirators lacked arms, lacked unity, and lacked a commander. But the idea did not die. Through 1791 and into 1792, the Committee maintained its correspondence with the émigré princes, who were gathering at Coblentz for the invasion of France. The princes, for their part, saw the Midi as a diversion — a second front that would draw republican forces away from the eastern frontier. They authorised the comte de Saillans, a major in the regiment of Roussillon, to return to France in disguise and take command of the southern rising.

Saillans arrived in the Ardèche in the spring of 1792, a man of undoubted energy but fatal arrogance. He clashed immediately with the local royalist leaders, who resented his authority and suspected his motives. The second assembly of Jalès, convened in July 1792, brought together perhaps thirteen hundred men — a thousand of them National Guards from the Catholic parishes of the Vivarais. Saillans issued a manifesto calling for the restoration of the monarchy and marched from Alais toward Saint-Ambroix.

It was too late. The revolutionary authorities had been forewarned by their own intelligence networks. The columns of National Guards that marched to meet Saillans were stronger and better armed. The rising was crushed almost as soon as it began. Saillans himself was killed or captured — the sources are ambiguous — and his papers, seized by the Directory of the Ardèche and printed, revealed the full scope of the conspiracy, implicating priests, nobles, and merchants across the Midi. The Camp de Jalès was finished.

But the Midi was not pacified. In the winter of 1792–1793, a new leader emerged from the mountains of the Lozère. Marc-Antoine Charrier, a notary of Nasbinals and a former deputy to the Estates-General, was a man of a different stamp from the aristocratic Saillans. He had fled to the high plateaus of the Aubrac after the failure of an earlier conspiracy in Mende, and there, in a landscape of frozen moors and wolf-haunted forests, he gathered around him the Catholic peasantry of the region. In March 1793, Charrier raised the banner of revolt. He rallied several hundred men and held the field against the republican army under General Carteaux for weeks, operating in the high country between the Lozère and the Aveyron. He captured Mende, marched on Marvejols, and attempted to coordinate with the Lyonnais federalists who had risen against the Convention.

It was the last serious royalist rising in the Midi before the Terror. Charrier was eventually defeated, captured, and executed. The republicans responded with pitiless repression: the refractory priests were driven out, the châteaux of the nobles were pillaged, and the bands of armed patriots roamed the countryside burning feudal titles. The southern Chouannerie was crushed, but the religious hatreds that had fuelled it remained, waiting to reignite at the next opportunity. Daudet’s judgment was clear: the Midi was not the West, and never could be. The open plains of Languedoc and the broad valleys of the Rhône did not favour the hit-and-run tactics of the hedgerow fighter. The southern movement had burned hot and died young, destroyed by its own internal divisions, by the lack of popular depth, and by the effectiveness of republican intelligence.

III. The West: The Chouannerie Under the Consulate and Empire (1800–1815)

The western Chouannerie was a different phenomenon entirely. Where the southern movement had been a series of convulsive risings, the West sustained a permanent guerrilla war for a generation. The geography of Brittany, Normandy, and Maine — the sunken lanes, the dense hedgerows, the forests, the endless coastline of coves and inlets — was made for the partisan. The social structure was different, too: the West was a region of deeply rooted Catholic piety, where the non-juring priest was more influential than the revolutionary official, and where the peasant fought for his faith as much as for his king.

When Bonaparte became First Consul in November 1799, the West was still in arms. Cadoudal commanded in Brittany, Bourmont in Maine, Frotté in Normandy, Châtillon in the Vendée. The Consulate’s first task was to pacify this region, and Bonaparte pursued a dual strategy: military pressure combined with amnesty offers. Frotté was lured into negotiations and then executed by General Guidal in a treacherous ambush that stained the new regime’s reputation. Cadoudal was courted, offered command in the republican army, and refused. The negotiations produced a fragile peace, but it was a peace built on police surveillance rather than genuine reconciliation.

The kidnapping of Senator Clément de Ris in September 1800 demonstrated how fragile that peace truly was. On the evening of 19 November 1800 — the date is fixed in Daudet’s narrative — a band of about thirty armed men, remnants of the Chouan bands who had turned to banditry, seized the senator from his château in the Touraine. They dragged him from his house before the eyes of his terrified wife and son and vanished into the darkness of the surrounding woods. The kidnapping of a senator — a former member of the Council of Five Hundred, a supporter of Bonaparte — was an act of spectacular audacity, a direct challenge to the authority of the new regime.

Fouché and Bonaparte mobilised the full machinery of the state. The prefect of Indre-et-Loire, the gendarmerie, and the military authorities all threw themselves into the pursuit. The kidnappers held Clément de Ris for several weeks at the château de Saut-de-Sabot, demanding a ransom. The affair was resolved when the police tracked down the kidnappers and liberated the senator. But the consequences were far-reaching. The kidnapping hardened Bonaparte’s attitude toward the West, confirmed his conviction that only relentless policing could break the Chouan resistance, and inaugurated an era of special military commissions operating without restraint.

The same month saw another Chouan blow against the revolutionary order: the assassination of Yves Audrein, the constitutional bishop of Quimper. Audrein was an old man, a former Oratorian, a deputy to the Convention, a regicide who had voted for the death of Louis XVI under three different names. He was the most hated figure in Brittany, the living symbol of the constitutional church that the peasantry despised. On the night of 19 November 1800, as Audrein travelled by diligence from Quimper to Morlaix, the carriage was stopped on the road by a band of armed men. A man in the typical dress of a Chouan — blue blouse, red waistcoat, leather-trimmed trousers — climbed into the carriage, seized the old bishop by the collar, held a lantern to his face, and pronounced his death sentence for the triple vote of regicide. Audrein was dragged from the carriage and shot dead on the road. The killers were never caught.

The Clément de Ris affair and the Audrein murder, occurring almost simultaneously, revealed the two faces of the western Chouannerie in the Napoleonic period: on one hand, the organised political conspiracy aimed at destabilising the state; on the other, the popular religious vendetta that needed no organisation at all. Both were beyond the reach of Fouché’s police to prevent, though both triggered crackdowns that progressively reduced the Chouan networks.

IV. The Aché-De Combray Conspiracy and the English Agencies

The most elaborate of the Napoleonic conspiracies was that of the vicomte d’Aché and the chevalier de Combray. D’Aché, a grandson of the admiral of that name, had emigrated in 1792, served under the princes, and fought in the bands of Frotté. After Frotté’s death, he returned to Normandy, where he became the organising centre of royalist resistance in the Calvados, moving through the countryside under assumed names — Deslauriers, Alexandre — sheltered by a network of sympathisers that the police could never fully penetrate. In 1802, he entered into relations with De Combray, a naval officer, and together they formed a conspiracy to raise the West. The plan was audacious: to seize a port on the Norman coast, open it to an English landing, and simultaneously raise the Chouan legions of Brittany and Normandy. The comte d’Artois, the Bourbon prince in exile, promised — as he had promised many times before — to come and take command in person.

The conspiracy was penetrated by Fouché’s police. Daudet, working from the police dossiers, reconstructs the slow accumulation of evidence: the intercepted couriers, the raided safe houses, the betrayals that gradually exposed the network to the authorities. D’Aché was arrested, imprisoned, and subjected to the full rigour of the imperial police. Even in prison, he remained defiant, but the conspiracy was broken. The Aché-De Combray affair was the last serious attempt to organise a general rising in the West during the Empire. After its failure, the Chouannerie in Normandy subsided into isolated acts of brigandage.

What had sustained the western movement through all these years, through the defeats and the betrayals and the executions, was English support. Daudet devotes his longest chapter to the Jersey agency, the nerve centre of Chouan operations after 1800. From the island of Jersey, British officers, French émigré intermediaries, and a network of smugglers and fishermen maintained a constant flow of correspondence, money, and arms across the Channel. The English agency in Bordeaux performed a similar function for the South, channelling English gold through the great Atlantic port into the Midi. The utility of this support was questionable — the sums of money were never enough, the arms shipments always too small, the promised English soldiers never came. But without English money, the Chouannerie would have died in 1796. English support gave the insurrection the means to survive, even if it could never give it the means to win.

V. The Last Campfire: The Rising of 1815

When Napoleon returned from Elba in March 1815, the Chouannerie of the West had been dormant for a decade. The old leaders were still alive — d’Autichamp, Suzannet, d’Andigné, Sapinaud — but they were no longer young men. They had accepted the Bourbon restoration of 1814, had received military ranks from Louis XVIII, and were living in honourable retirement. The return of the Emperor threw them into confusion. Suzannet rushed to Paris to offer his services to the king, but found only paralysis at the Tuileries. The duc de Bourbon was dispatched to the West, but without a plan. The Chouan leaders were left to improvise.

And improvise they did. In May 1815, the West rose for the last time. D’Autichamp raised five thousand men in Anjou, d’Andigné thirteen thousand on the right bank of the Loire, Suzannet, Sapinaud, and Charette in the Vendée, De Sol de Grisolles in the Morbihan. A council of war was held at Cholet. The plan was to drive the imperial troops out of the West and to coordinate with the expected arrival of English arms and Bourbon princes.

The reality was a disaster of miscommunication and false hope. The promised English flotilla arrived at Saint-Gilles-Croix-de-Vie on 16 May — with only two thousand muskets and a few barrels of powder instead of the fourteen thousand muskets, cannon, and ammunition that had been promised. A second convoy on 2 June was no better. There was no money, no cannon, no English soldiers. The Chouans fought with hunting rifles and pitchforks against the veterans of the imperial army.

General Travot struck first at Aizenay, scattering Suzannet’s corps in a night attack. Charette was mortally wounded. The Chouan bands, poorly armed, poorly supplied, and torn by rivalries between their commanders, were defeated in a series of obscure skirmishes across the West. By the end of June, the rising was over. Napoleon had been defeated at Waterloo, but the Chouans had played no part in his downfall. Thousands of imperial troops pinned down in the West — men whom Daudet notes might have turned the tide at Waterloo — had been held away from the decisive theatre by the last, futile convulsion of the Chouannerie.

With the second Bourbon restoration, the surviving Chouan leaders were rewarded with ranks and pensions. The peasants of the West returned to their fields. The hedgerows and forests that had sheltered the Chouans for a generation fell silent. The war that had begun in 1790, that had burned in the South and smouldered in the West, was finally over.

VI. The Thread

Daudet’s two books, read together, reveal the Chouannerie as a continuous thread running through the entire revolutionary and Napoleonic period — a thread that connected the Camp de Jalès of 1790 to the final campfire of 1815. The southern and western variants were different in character: the one religious and convulsive, the other social and enduring. But both were expressions of the same refusal: the refusal of rural France, Catholic France, royalist France, to accept the Revolution and its works. The Chouannerie failed. It never came close to restoring the Bourbons, never seriously threatened the republic or the empire. But it lasted, and its longevity was a reproach to the Revolution’s claim to have united the nation. For twenty-five years, a substantial part of the French people remained in arms against the state. That fact, which Daudet documents with the patience of the archival historian and the vividness of the novelist, is the true significance of the Chouannerie. It was not a war that could be won, but it was a war that could not be ended — not by massacre, not by amnesty, not by the most sophisticated police system in Europe. It ended only when the men who had fought it were too old to fight any longer.
