The Directory Government

The Constitution of the Year III

The Constitution of the Year III, ratified in August 1795, was the most elaborate political mechanism the Revolution had yet produced — an attempt to give France a stable government after the Terror by dividing authority so minutely that no faction could ever again seize the state at a stroke. The framers, the Commission of Eleven chosen by the Thermidorian Convention, had drawn the lesson that absolute power, whether lodged in a single assembly or a single man, led inevitably to catastrophe. They designed a republic of checks so carefully distributed that in practice they could scarcely move at all.

Executive power was vested in a Directory of five members, elected by the two legislative councils from a list drawn up by the Council of Five Hundred. One director retired each year, replaced by a newly elected member, the order determined by lot. Each presided in rotation for three months; decisions were by majority; no director could be removed except by slow impeachment. The Directory appointed ministers and generals, conducted diplomacy, and executed the laws — but could not initiate legislation.

Legislative power was divided between two chambers: the Council of Five Hundred, which proposed laws, and the Council of the Ancients — two hundred and fifty members, all married or widowed men over forty — which approved or rejected them. Neither could dissolve the other. The electoral system was indirect and property-weighted: only citizens who paid a direct tax equivalent to three days’ labour could vote in the primary assemblies; only those who paid the equivalent of one hundred and fifty days’ labour could serve as electors. The deputies themselves had to meet still higher property qualifications. It was a republic of the propertied, designed by men who believed that the Terror had its roots in the participation of the propertyless in politics.

The two-thirds decrees, adopted before the Constitution took effect, required that two-thirds of the first legislative corps be drawn from the sitting Convention. This self-protecting measure was a confession of fragility: the framers dared not trust the electorate to choose a republican majority on its own. The Constitution was ratified by plebiscite with an overwhelming majority, but the abstention rate was enormous. The country was exhausted; what it wanted was simply an end to convulsions.

From the first, the Constitution bore the mark of the fear that had produced it. It was a document that trusted no one and nothing — not the people, not the executive, not the legislature. It created a government that was strong enough to be dangerous but not strong enough to be effective.

The Five Directors

The first Directory, chosen in November 1795, reflected the cross-currents of the Thermidorian reaction. Louis-Marie de La Révellière-Lépeaux was a regicide of the Convention, a provincial lawyer from Anjou, a fervent deist who dreamed of founding a national cult of theophilanthropy. Jean-François Rewbell, a former deputy of the Third Estate from Alsace, was coarse, energetic, domineering, his bluntness masking a shrewd political intelligence — he was, by general agreement, the most capable of the five. Étienne-François Letourneur, a military engineer and a constitutional monarchist of the 1791 school, had made his peace with the Republic. Paul Barras, the nobleman turned revolutionary, a man of pleasure and intrigue, governed through corruption, mistresses, and favours; his venality was already legendary. Lazare Carnot, the Organizer of Victory — the austere mathematician and military strategist who had directed the war from the Committee of Public Safety — commanded the respect of the army as no other civilian could.

The Directory was split from its first session. La Révellière-Lépeaux, Rewbell, and Barras formed a loose alliance of the republican left, determined to suppress Jacobins and hold royalists at bay. Carnot and Letourneur inclined toward the moderate right, favouring conciliation with constitutional monarchists and relaxation of the laws against émigrés and priests. This division between the three — the dominant faction, increasingly associated with Barras — and the two persisted until the final convulsions of the regime.

The Babeuf Conspiracy

The first great crisis of the Directory came not from the royalists but from the extreme left. Gracchus Babeuf — François-Noël Babeuf by birth — was a journalist and agitator of formidable energy who had emerged from Thermidor as the apostle of a new revolution, more radical than the first. His newspaper, Le Tribun du Peuple, preached the abolition of private property and the establishment of a true community of equals — a doctrine he called absolute equality. He gathered around him a circle of former Jacobins, Montagnard deputies proscribed after Thermidor, and disaffected army officers: Filippo Buonarroti, the Italian revolutionary and former agent of the Committee of Public Safety; Sylvain Maréchal, the atheist philosopher; Darthé, the former juror of the Revolutionary Tribunal; Rossignol, the general of the Vendée.

Fantin-Desodoards, writing in 1801, gives the fullest account of the conspiracy among our sources. He notes that Babeuf himself was “only the obscure and secondary agent of a faction of whose secrets he perhaps himself knew only a part.” The operational structure was elaborate: a secret directory of public safety directing the movement, with agents in each of Paris’s twelve arrondissements who reported on food stores, arms depots, workshops, and workers’ numbers; compiled lists of well-off patriots who could house provincial militants; organized subscriptions to fund the insurrection; and formed teams of groupeurs who circulated daily in the Tuileries gardens reading patriot journals aloud to crowds.

The conspiracy was betrayed by an agent of the Directory, Georges Grisel, who infiltrated the inner circle. On the night of 21 Floréal, Year IV — May 10, 1796 — the police struck. Babeuf, Buonarroti, Darthé, and the principal conspirators were arrested in their lodgings. Seized in Babeuf’s rooms was the complete archive of the conspiracy: lists of adherents, correspondence, plans for the insurrection, drafts of proclamations. The trial at Vendôme began in February 1797 and dragged on for months. Babeuf conducted his own defence with extraordinary eloquence, turning the courtroom into a tribune. He was condemned to death and guillotined on 27 May 1797. Buonarroti, whose life was spared, would write a history of the conspiracy in prison that became a sacred text for the revolutionary movements of the nineteenth century.

Fantin quotes at length from a letter found at Babeuf’s address in which Babeuf defended the Robespierre Terror as correct in principle: “I think that government was the one best suited to make a revolution succeed. A regenerator must see on a grand scale; his duty is to mow down everything that hinders him, everything that obstructs his passage — scoundrels, idiots, the presumptuous alike, it is all the same; too bad for them, why were they in the way?” This was not the voice of a single fanatic; it was the ideology of a faction that had never accepted that the Terror was wrong, only that it had failed.

The Elections of Year V and the Royalist Resurgence

Barely had the Babeuf conspiracy been crushed when the Directory faced a threat from the opposite direction. The elections of Germinal, Year V — March–April 1797 — to renew one-third of the legislative councils resulted in a stunning victory for the royalist and constitutional monarchist opposition. The Clichyens — so called because they gathered at the Club de Clichy in Paris — were not openly royalist in the sense of demanding the immediate restoration of Louis XVIII; they were rather a coalition of constitutional monarchists, moderate royalists, and conservative republicans who wanted to roll back the Revolution’s most extreme measures. They demanded freedom for the royalist press, relaxation of the laws against émigrés and refractory priests, and an end to the Directory’s financial mismanagement.

The new third of the councils included figures of known royalist sympathies: General Pichegru, the conqueror of Holland who had returned from exile and was elected president of the Council of Five Hundred; the abbé Brottier, a former agent of the princes; and a substantial minority that looked to the restoration of the monarchy as the only way to end the Revolution. The effect was immediate. A state of war established itself between the legislative and executive powers. The royalist deputies attacked the Directory’s financial administration, demanded the repeal of the laws against the émigrés, and manoeuvred to remove the directors allied with Barras.

The government was in a precarious position. The treasury was empty. The armies were unpaid. The Clichyen press grew bolder by the day. Pichegru, who had been secretly in correspondence with the Bourbon princes, was manoeuvring to become a figurehead of the restoration. In the councils, motions were introduced to recall the proscribed priests, to reopen the churches, to revise the laws against the émigrés. The Republic seemed to be dissolving from within.

The Coup of 18 Fructidor

The three directors who formed the governing core — Barras, La Révellière-Lépeaux, and Rewbell — resolved to strike. They had the support of the army, and they had the precedent of every revolutionary government before them: when the constitution failed to protect the Republic, the Republic must protect itself by extra-constitutional means. General Augereau, a rough and energetic commander of the Army of Italy, was brought to Paris by Bonaparte and placed in command of the military forces of the capital. On the morning of 18 Fructidor, Year V — September 4, 1797 — Augereau’s troops surrounded the Tuileries. Carnot, forewarned, escaped through a window and fled to Switzerland. Barthélemy, who had replaced Letourneur, was arrested. The royalist deputies — Pichegru and the leaders of the opposition — were seized in their seats and sent to the Temple prison.

The Directory then unleashed a package of repressive measures. The elections of forty-nine departments were annulled. A hundred and seventy-seven deputies were deprived of their seats; fifty-three were condemned to deportation to Guiana. The laws against émigrés and refractory priests were revived with new severity; all unauthorized returnees were given two weeks to leave France on pain of death. The royalist press was suppressed; a dozen newspapers were closed, their editors deported. Constitutional guarantees of personal liberty were suspended; administrative arrests were authorised on the order of the Directory alone. Carnot and Barthélemy were proscribed as directors.

Fantin gives the full sequence: the Directory’s intelligence about royalist contacts with the Bourbon pretender (the Duverne de Presles revelations), the Directory’s session of 10 Fructidor, the coup itself, the permanent session of the surviving councils, the deportation law. He is critical but not simplistic — he accepts that a genuine royalist threat existed while questioning whether the Directory’s methods were constitutional.

Constant, who had watched the events unfold from his retreat at Hérivaux, was caught between his principles and his fears. He had argued, in De la force du gouvernement actuel (1796), that the Directory was strong enough to govern legally, without recourse to arbitrary power — that its real strength lay in the material interests created by the Revolution: the purchasers of national lands, the holders of Republic-issued contracts, the new class of proprietors who had no stake in a royalist restoration. The arbitrary power had come, nevertheless, and Constant, fearing a royalist restoration more than he feared despotism, accepted the coup as a necessary evil. But the necessity was a confession of failure: the Directory had proven that it could not govern through the Constitution. The resort to force was the strategy of a regime that had lost its political legitimacy.

Constant’s Analysis

Benjamin Constant’s three pamphlets of 1796–97 constitute the most sustained contemporary analysis of the Directory’s constitutional predicament. In De la force du gouvernement actuel, Des réactions politiques, and Des effets de la terreur, he developed a double argument: the Directory had genuine sources of strength in the new property settlement, but it was destroying those sources by its own recourse to arbitrary power.

The first argument was sociological. The Revolution had created interests — in land, in contracts, in careers — that were now widespread and could not survive a restoration. The purchasers of biens nationaux, the army officers who had risen from the ranks, the civil servants of the Republic, the suppliers who had grown rich on war contracts — all these men had a material stake in the Republic that no ideology could match. The Directory’s real base, Constant argued, was not the theory of republicanism but the practice of property. This was a brilliant insight: it grounded the Republic not in abstract principle but in the concrete interests of a new ruling class.

The second argument was a warning. The Directory was destroying this base by its own methods. When it resorted to arbitrary arrests, to annulled elections, to military force against its political opponents, it reproduced the logic of the Terror in the name of opposing it. “What did France the most harm,” Constant wrote, “the harm most difficult to repair, was precisely the impatience to do good.” When honest Thermidorian men used revolutionary means against revolutionary brigands, they consecrated illegality by making it serve good purposes — thereby destroying the only moral argument against illegality: that it always produces harm.

In Des effets de la terreur, Constant attacked a doctrine he saw spreading: that the Terror had been justified by its outcomes — that its violence had saved the Republic at its moment of maximum external danger. His refutation had two parts. First, the factual claim was false: the Republic was saved by the armies, by geography, and by the incompetence of the coalition — not by the guillotine. Second, and more important, even if the Terror had beneficial effects, accepting it as justified set a precedent that destroyed constitutional government: any emergency could be used to justify any measure, and once that principle was accepted, there was no logical stopping point. The Directory was proving the point by its own actions.

The deepest argument in Constant’s analysis was structural: the Directory was a government that tried to be constitutional while governing through coups. It had no popular base of its own — it was neither Jacobin nor royalist, neither democratic nor monarchical — and it had no way to acquire one without abandoning the very centre ground it claimed to occupy. The revolutionary mean was not a stable equilibrium; it was an unstable oscillation where each resort to force narrowed the base of support on which the regime rested.

The Coup of Floréal Year VI

The elections of Floréal, Year VI — April 1798 — produced the next crisis. This time, the danger came from the left. The neo-Jacobins, taking advantage of the discredit into which the royalists had fallen after Fructidor, won a substantial number of seats in the renewed third of the councils. The Directory, once again unwilling to accept a result that threatened its control, resorted to extra-constitutional action. On 22 Floréal — May 11, 1798 — a law was passed by the purged councils annulling the elections in twenty-six departments and excluding a hundred and six deputies from their seats.

The coup of Floréal was different in character from Fructidor. It was not a military operation but a legislative manipulation, carried out by a commission of the Ancients that examined the electoral returns and struck out the names of Jacobins on grounds of illegality. But its effect was the same: the Constitution was violated, the will of the electorate was set aside, and the regime’s already slender claim to legitimacy was further eroded. Pagès, in his Histoire secrète de la Révolution, notes the irony: those who had regarded the Republic’s institutions with indifference now aspired only to be masters of them, and the Directory had taught them that the only way to be master was to cheat.

The Coup of Prairial Year VII

The third and final coup — 30 Prairial, Year VII — June 18, 1799 — marked the disintegration of the regime. The elections of Year VII had returned a strengthened neo-Jacobin minority to the councils, and the military disasters of 1799 — the defeat of Jourdan at Stockach, the loss of Italy to Suvorov’s Russian army, the financial collapse — had destroyed what remained of the Directory’s prestige. The legislative councils turned on the executive.

In a single session, the councils forced the resignation of three directors: Treilhard (who was replaced on a technicality regarding his eligibility), La Révellière-Lépeaux, and Merlin de Douai. Only Barras remained of the original governing core. Sieyès, who had been elected to the Directory in May 1799, now became the dominant figure — and he brought with him a conviction that the Constitution of Year III was a dead letter that needed to be replaced entirely, not reformed.

The coup of Prairial was the reverse of Fructidor and Floréal: this time it was the legislature that purged the executive, not the executive that purged the legislature. The neo-Jacobins in the councils pushed through a forced loan on the rich, a law of hostages against the Chouan insurgents, and a revival of revolutionary taxation. The Directory, now headed by Sieyès, resisted these measures passively, refusing to enforce what it could not openly oppose. The government had ceased to function. Paganel, writing in 1815 from inside the French state apparatus, describes this period as one of accelerating dissolution: the treasury empty, the armies unpaid, the departments in insurrection, and the generals — Bonaparte above all — watching from afar as the civilian regime consumed itself.

The War and the Generals

While the Directory struggled with its internal contradictions, the war created the forces that would destroy it. Bonaparte’s Italian campaign of 1796–97 — the crossing of the Alps, the victories at Lodi, Castiglione, Arcole, Rivoli, the Treaty of Campo Formio — had made the Republic the dominant power on the continent. The spoils of Italy filled the empty French treasury. The prestige of the army sustained the government against its domestic enemies.

But the war also created a new set of problems. The generals had become independent political actors. Bonaparte had signed the preliminaries of Léoben without consulting the Directory, and the directors had been forced to accept the fait accompli. The condition of the Republic depended on the goodwill of men whose power was military in origin and whose ambitions were personal. The Egyptian expedition, launched in May 1798, was at once a strategy of imperial expansion and a means of removing Bonaparte from the political stage. The Directory, alarmed by the general’s popularity, was happy to see him depart for the Orient. But Nelson destroyed the French fleet at Aboukir Bay, and Bonaparte was stranded — watching from Cairo as the Republic drifted, but also gathering the prestige of a conqueror in the East.

The Second Coalition — England, Austria, Russia, Naples, the Ottoman Empire — formed against France in 1798, and the Republic suddenly found itself fighting on six fronts with its best general trapped in Egypt. The war turned decisively against the Directory in 1799. Masséna was defeated in Switzerland; Schérer was routed in Italy; Suvorov’s Russian army swept across the Po valley undoing all of Bonaparte’s conquests. The Republic was threatened with invasion. The departments of the west rose in a new Chouan insurrection; the south was ravaged by brigandage. The regime seemed to be dying by inches.

The Financial Collapse

The Directory inherited a fiscal catastrophe and made it worse. The assignats had depreciated to virtually nothing by February 1796 — a one-hundred-franc note worth barely five sous in coin. The government attempted a drastic remedy: a new paper currency, the mandats territoriaux, exchangeable for national lands. They were issued in March 1796 to the value of two billion four hundred million francs. Within six months, they had followed the assignats into the abyss. On February 4, 1797, the fabrication of paper money was definitively abandoned. France returned to a metallic currency — but there was almost none in circulation.

The bankruptcy was declared on September 30, 1797. The consolidated debt was reduced to one-third of its nominal value, payable in bonds that could be used to purchase national lands. The rentiers, the widows and orphans whose annuities had been paid in assignats and then in nothing, were ruined. The state had defaulted on its obligations, and the financial confidence that is the foundation of any stable government was destroyed for the remainder of the Directory’s existence.

The consequence was that the state came to depend on the army for its survival — not merely for defence against foreign enemies, but for its daily bread. The military commanders financed their operations by requisitions, contributions, and outright plunder, remitting a portion to the empty treasury in Paris. This arrangement kept the state afloat, but at a terrible cost: it made the civilian government a pensioner of its own generals, and it placed in the hands of men like Bonaparte a power that no constitution could contain.

The Political Factions

The Directory was perpetually caught between three political forces that it could neither absorb nor destroy. On the left, the neo-Jacobins — survivors of the Montagnard party, Jacobin club militants, and radical democrats — demanded the revival of revolutionary taxation, the prosecution of the remaining émigrés, and the defence of the Republic by any means necessary. On the right, the Clichyens — constitutional monarchists, moderate royalists, and conservative republicans — pressed for peace with the European powers, the return of the émigrés, and the restoration of Catholic worship. In the centre, the Directorials — the men who actually held power — had no program beyond survival. They governed by playing the extremes against each other, but the game required periodic coups to correct the election results, and each coup alienated the very interests that Constant had identified as the regime’s natural base.

The regime could not expand its support because it dared not trust the electorate. Every election between 1795 and 1799 produced the “wrong” result: first a royalist majority, then a neo-Jacobin resurgence, then another leftward shift. The Directory responded each time by annulling the elections it did not like. It was a republic that could not win a popular mandate and could not govern without one. Pagès, in his synthetic volume (Tome VI), notes the extreme penury of the national treasury — needs so great and pressing that the Directory could not borrow at any interest. A state that cannot borrow has no future; a government that cannot trust its own elections has no legitimacy.

The Approach of Brumaire

By the summer of 1799, the Directory was in its death agony. Sieyès, now the dominant figure, concluded that a new constitution was necessary — and that a new constitution required a new executive, with concentrated powers, backed by the army. He began to search for the “sword” — a general who would lend his military prestige to the enterprise of overthrowing the Directory. Paganel, in his Histoire de Napoléon Bonaparte (1815), gives the sharpest contemporary account of what followed. Bonaparte had returned from Egypt in October 1799, landing at Fréjus and travelling to Paris through crowds that hailed him as the saviour of the Republic. He was young, famous, ambitious, and loyal to no regime. Paganel insists that the coup was a personal political achievement of extraordinary craft: Bonaparte cultivated Sieyès not as a collaborator but as an instrument he intended from the first to use and discard. He “attached himself to Sieyès to deceive him; he deceived him in order not to have to fear him.”

Paganel’s sharpest observation is about the reception: military glory was the avowed title of Bonaparte’s elevation; “fear and venality were its true causes.” The Directory had governed for four years by the periodic resort to force; now force had found a face, and the regime that had lived by the army would die by it.

The conspirators — Sieyès, Talleyrand, Fouché, Lucien Bonaparte — laid their plans through the autumn. The coup was fixed for 18 Brumaire, Year VIII — November 9, 1799. The Council of Ancients was persuaded to transfer the legislative sessions to Saint-Cloud and to appoint Bonaparte commander of the military forces of the capital. The directors were dealt with one by one: Sieyès and Ducos resigned; Barras was persuaded to resign by Talleyrand; Gohier and Moulin were placed under house arrest. The Directory was finished.

The drama at Saint-Cloud on 19 Brumaire was less smooth. Bonaparte’s attempt to address the Council of Five Hundred degenerated into chaos — the deputies shouted “Down with the dictator!” and Bonaparte was manhandled. It was Lucien Bonaparte who saved the situation by calling in Murat’s grenadiers, who cleared the hall with fixed bayonets. That night, a rump of the Council voted the abolition of the Directory and the establishment of the Consulate.

The Verdict

The Directory had governed France for four years — from November 1795 to November 1799. It had preserved the Republic against the royalists, crushed the Babeuf conspiracy, maintained the war effort, and given France the most brilliant military victories of the Revolution. But it had failed to establish any durable foundation for republican government. The Constitution of the Year III, the most carefully designed of the revolutionary constitutions, had been violated by its own executives — elections annulled twice, a military coup once, a legislative purge once more — and finally collapsed into the personal rule of a general.

The verdict of the period sources is remarkably consistent. Constant’s analysis — that a government which governs through arbitrary power destroys the only foundation on which a durable republic can rest — proved prophetic. Fantin’s narrative shows a regime perpetually reacting to crises it could not prevent. Pagès, in his synthetic volume, catalogues the corruption of the directors, the penury of the treasury, the moral exhaustion of the nation. Paganel traces the logic by which a republic that could not govern itself produced a general who would govern instead.

The Directory expired without convulsions, its end scarcely noticed by the inhabitants of Paris, who watched the military promenade of Brumaire with the indifference of a people that had seen too many revolutions to be surprised by one more. It had been, in its mortal illness, a government that could neither live by the law nor openly abandon it — a regime caught between the Constitution it could not make work and the coup it could not stop making.
