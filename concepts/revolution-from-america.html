The Revolution from America

The Man Who Saw It Coming

Gouverneur Morris arrived in Paris in February 1789 with an American’s calm assumption that the world could be remade by reason. He was thirty-six years old, tall and commanding despite the wooden leg below his left knee, and he carried introductory letters from George Washington and Benjamin Franklin to the leading men of France. He had written the Constitution of the United States with his own hand as chairman of the Committee of Style — the words “We the People” were his — and he had helped frame a system of government that balanced executive authority against popular representation with a precision he considered nearly perfect. He came on commercial business for Robert Morris, the Philadelphia financier, but he brought with him something more valuable than any contract: the conviction of an American who had seen a revolution succeed and who believed he understood what made that success possible.

Within days of his landing, he bought a blank-book bound in apple-green vellum at a shop called À l’Espérance and began keeping a diary. The first entry, March 1, 1789, recorded a morning of business negotiations, a dinner at M. Du Molley’s, an evening at the French theatre watching Molière’s Le Misanthrope. Already the pattern was set: commerce and society and political observation all pressed together in the same compressed paragraphs, each given the same flat emphasis, each recorded with a precision that would make the green diary the most sustained American eyewitness account of the French Revolution ever written.

The America that Morris carried in his head was not the America that most French revolutionaries imagined. He was a New York Federalist, an aristocrat of the manor-born sort, a man who believed in strong executive power, in property rights as the foundation of liberty, in the necessity of a senate to check popular passion. The French reformers who welcomed him — Lafayette, Jefferson, the constitutional politicians of the National Assembly — assumed that the American Revolution and the French Revolution were the same movement on different soil, that the principles of 1776 would naturally and easily translate to the circumstances of 1789. Morris knew they were wrong, and he said so from the beginning.

“Like the cut of a Coat and the Form of a Constitution,” he wrote. “Like the English, they want what they know not.”

The Sister Revolution That Wasn’t

The American conviction that the French Revolution was a sister to their own ran deep and lasted long. Thomas Jefferson, who had been American Minister in Paris since 1785 and who watched the events of 1789 from the same salons and Assembly galleries as Morris, believed with an intensity that bordered on faith that France was repeating the American experiment. The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, adopted in August 1789, was in its essentials a French rendering of the Virginia Declaration of Rights and the American Declaration of Independence. The men who wrote it — Lafayette consulted Jefferson on the draft — had read the American texts with the reverence due to scripture, and they believed they were applying the same principles to a society that needed them even more urgently than the American colonies had done.

Jefferson’s letters from Paris, dispatched to friends in America, carried the news of revolution with an excitement he did not trouble to conceal. “The spirit of the nation is unfathomable,” he wrote. “The American revolution seems to have awakened thinking men in all parts of Europe.” He sent home copies of the French declarations, the debates on constitutional questions, the proceedings of the National Assembly, as though they were dispatches from a second front in the same war of liberty against despotism. He dined with Lafayette, with Mirabeau, with the leaders of the reforming party, and he read their speeches and their pamphlets with the sympathetic attention of a man who expected the same results to follow.

Morris, sitting in the same rooms, hearing the same debates, reaching the same dinners, drew a different conclusion each time. His diary for the spring and summer of 1789 records a series of conversations with Lafayette in which he tried to impress upon the French commander the structural defects of the constitution being drafted. The Assembly was creating a single chamber with unchecked legislative authority, reducing the king to a figurehead with a suspensive veto that could be overridden, and providing no independent executive power that could resist the will of the legislature. This was not the American system. Morris had helped write a constitution that balanced three branches of government — a strong executive, a bicameral legislature, an independent judiciary — precisely because the Americans had learned from their own experience that popular assemblies could be as tyrannical as kings. The French, he told Lafayette, were making a constitution that would produce anarchy, and anarchy would produce a dictator.

Lafayette listened, and did not believe him. The American general who had fought at Yorktown, who had returned to France the hero of two worlds, who commanded the National Guard of Paris and believed he could control the Revolution he had helped unleash — Lafayette was not a man who took advice easily, even from a friend who had written the document he claimed to admire. Morris recorded his assessment with the clinical precision of a portraitist who both admires his subject and sees through him completely. “He is the Lover of Freedom from Ambition, of which there are two Kinds, the one born of Pride, the other of Vanity and his partakes most of the latter.”

The Green Diary Witness

Morris’s diary is the American Revolution’s most unexpected legacy to the history of the French Revolution — a record kept by a man who refused to be swept along by the emotional currents of his time, who recorded the fall of the Bastille in the same tone he recorded the price of tobacco, who watched heads carried on pikes and noted the weather on the same page.

The entries for July 12–14, 1789, are among the most vivid in the diary. On the 12th, Morris learned of Necker’s dismissal while dining with the Maréchal de Castries. The news broke Paris open. He rode through the city, witnessed a cavalry charge in the Place Louis Quinze — sabres drawn, stones thrown, pistols fired without effect — and noted what he saw with economy that makes the violence more present for being understated: “The People are employed in breaking open the Armorers’ Shops.” On the 13th, he walked to the Louvre with a green bow in his hat, the fashion of the day that every man was obliged to wear who meant to walk in peace. “I apprehend nothing from the Populace,” he wrote. On the 22nd, walking under the arcade of the Palais-Royal, he saw the head and body of Foulon, the old minister, carried through the streets on a pike, the body dragged naked on the earth, and then Bertier his son-in-law, also torn to pieces. “Gracious God what a People!” he wrote.

The juxtaposition is everything. Morris went from seeing a man torn to pieces to visiting a painter’s studio, where he saw a canvas called Love Escaped from His Cage and proposed a different subject: the guardsman who, seizing the Bastille gate and unable to bring it down, cried to his comrades to pull on his legs, and held while a dozen men pulled him like a rope, racking his joints, until the gate gave way. “To represent him drawn out of Joint with his Head turned round, encouraging them to draw still harder, must I think have a fine Effect.” Talleyrand, the Bishop of Autun, who was present, agreed entirely.

This is the Revolution as an American saw it: astonishing, violent, terrible, and yet — like the Constitution of the United States — a subject for aesthetic judgment, for practical advice, for the calm exercise of a mind trained to assess probabilities. Morris never lost his nerve. He never doubted his own judgment. And he never stopped believing that the French were making mistakes that would cost them everything.

Through 1790 and 1791, as the Revolution radicalized, his diary records a steady accumulation of evidence that his predictions were being fulfilled. The assignat depreciated; the émigrés gathered on the borders; the king’s authority drained away month by month. Morris advised the court through intermediaries — the Abbé Bertrand, Madame de Flahaut, the Comte de La Marck — urging the king to accept the constitution, however flawed, and to work within it rather than attempting to flee or resist. “I preach incessantly Respect for the Constitution under which we live and for that which is forming here,” he wrote. But the March 1791 entry where the king visited the National Assembly to accept the Constitution’s authority — it rang hollow, and Morris knew it. The flight to Varennes in June 1791 confirmed his darkest assessment: “He changed every Thing and now the general wish seems to be for a Republic, which is quite in the natural Order of Things.”

The Royalist Conspiracy

By 1792, Morris was no longer merely an observer. His position as United States Minister — he had been appointed by Washington in May 1792, despite French republican protests — gave him diplomatic cover for activities that would have destroyed any ordinary citizen. And by the summer of that year, he was deeply involved in a royalist counter-conspiracy that would become the most sensitive episode of his French years.

The network was organized through Monciel, a former interior minister, and a Provençal agent named Brémond. The scheme was to organize armed men inside Paris who would rally to the king’s defense at designated points in the event of an insurrection. Morris held 5,000 louis d’or — gold coins, convertible currency, the real thing in a world of depreciating assignats — in his house on the rue de la Planche, earmarked for paying reliable men when the moment came. The money had been paid out of royal funds through Brémond; the purses of twenty louis were divided and kept at Morris’s house pending activation of the network.

The moment never came. On August 10, 1792, the insurrectionary sections of Paris stormed the Tuileries before the network could be activated. The Swiss Guard was massacred. The king took refuge with the Assembly. The monarchy fell. Morris’s house became a shelter for Monciel and his family, along with others implicated in the royalist resistance. The diary was suppressed — it would have been a death warrant for everyone under his roof if discovered. The editor who later published it noted simply: “The events of August tenth are too well known to admit of repeating their painful story.”

The 5,000 louis became a prolonged ordeal. Brémond had removed the gold to Swiss bankers, and Morris spent years afterward pursuing its recovery, finally rendering an account to Madame Royale — the Princess Marie-Thérèse, Louis XVI’s daughter — after her release from the Temple prison. The accounting survives in the diary’s appendix: a document of remarkable clarity and moral seriousness, tracing what had become of every coin. It is the record of a man who had been entrusted with royal gold to save a monarchy and who had failed, not through any fault of his own, but through the sheer speed of events that overtook every plan.

Morris’s recall by Washington in 1794 was quietly inevitable. The United States had recognized the French Republic; the Girondins and then the Jacobins knew his sympathies; his usefulness as a diplomat was exhausted. He returned to Morrisania, the manor on the Harlem River where he had been born, and spent his remaining years as a farmer, a Federalist senator, and a determined opponent of Jefferson’s embargo. But the French years stayed with him. The green diary, the Louvre salon, the heads on pikes on the rue Saint-Honoré, the 5,000 louis that never bought what it was meant to buy, the king he had tried to save — they formed the great subject of his life, and he knew it.

The Neutrality of the New Republic

The American government watched the French Revolution from three thousand miles away, with a mixture of sympathy and alarm that shifted as the Revolution itself shifted. In 1789, when the news of the Bastille’s fall reached Philadelphia, the response was nearly unanimous enthusiasm. The United States had shown the way; France was following; the alliance that had won American independence was bearing fruit in the liberation of Europe. But by 1793, when the news arrived that France had declared war on Great Britain, the enthusiasm had curdled into the most difficult strategic question the young republic had yet faced.

The issue was simple and agonizing. The United States had a treaty of alliance with France, signed in 1778, that had been essential to winning the War of Independence. The treaty was still in force. France was now at war with Great Britain, which was still the United States’ most important trading partner and which controlled Canada, the Great Lakes posts, and the Atlantic sea lanes. To honor the treaty meant war with Britain — a war the United States was in no condition to fight, with a tiny army, a navy that barely existed, and a treasury still recovering from the Revolution. To abandon the treaty meant betraying the ally who had made independence possible and risking war with France. There was no good option.

George Washington’s decision was the most consequential of his presidency. On April 22, 1793, he issued a Proclamation of Neutrality, declaring that the United States would “pursue a conduct friendly and impartial toward the belligerent powers.” The word “neutrality” did not appear in the proclamation — Washington was careful not to use a term that might imply a legal status requiring the consent of other nations — but the meaning was unmistakable. The United States would not enter the war on either side.

The proclamation split the American government as profoundly as anything would split it before the Civil War. Alexander Hamilton, the Secretary of the Treasury, defended neutrality as the only rational policy for a weak republic in a world of warring empires. The United States needed peace to build its institutions, to pay its debts, to develop its commerce; war would destroy everything the Constitution had been created to secure. Thomas Jefferson, the Secretary of State, argued with equal passion that neutrality was ingratitude, dishonor, and strategic blindness. France was the republic’s natural ally against monarchy; Britain was the old enemy, still occupying frontier posts, still impressing American seamen, still treating American commerce with contempt. To abandon France was to abandon the principles of the Revolution itself.

The debate was not merely about foreign policy. It was about the meaning of the American Revolution. Hamilton saw the French Revolution as a descent into anarchy that proved the necessity of strong government, social hierarchy, and respect for property. He read Morris’s dispatches from Paris — Hamilton was one of the few men who received Morris’s private assessments — and found in them a confirmation of everything he feared about popular democracy unchecked by constitutional restraints. Jefferson saw the French Revolution as the continuation of the American struggle, a rising of the people against the same forces of monarchy and privilege that the American colonists had defeated. He read the same dispatches and found in them evidence that the old order was crumbling everywhere, that the future belonged to republics, and that the United States had a duty to stand with its sister republic against the monarchies of Europe.

Washington, as he so often did, found the middle path and held it against pressure from both sides. The Proclamation of Neutrality was not a betrayal of France, he argued; it was the only policy that would allow the United States to survive long enough to be of real use to the cause of liberty. A weak republic that committed itself to an unwinnable war would be crushed; a strong republic that preserved its independence could one day fulfill its promises. He was right, but being right did not make the policy popular.

The Citizen Genêt Affair

The first test of neutrality came in the form of a thirty-year-old French diplomat named Edmond-Charles Genêt, who arrived in Charleston, South Carolina, on April 8, 1793, with instructions from the Girondin ministry to recruit American privateers, organize expeditions against British and Spanish territory, and mobilize American popular opinion in favor of France. Genêt was handsome, eloquent, and utterly without diplomatic judgment. He believed — because the Girondins had told him — that the American people were fervently pro-French and that the Washington administration was out of touch with its own population. He proceeded to act on this belief with catastrophic energy.

From Charleston, Genêt traveled overland to Philadelphia, commissioning privateers, recruiting volunteers, and issuing military commissions to American citizens along the way. In Charleston alone he commissioned four privateers that sailed under the French flag to prey on British shipping. He organized an expedition against Spanish Florida. He encouraged French consuls in American ports to exercise admiralty jurisdiction — to adjudicate prizes captured by French privateers on American territory, a direct violation of American sovereignty. By the time he reached Philadelphia in May, he had already created a series of incidents that left Washington and his cabinet little choice but to demand his recall.

Genêt, for his part, was not repentant. He received the Proclamation of Neutrality with contempt, arguing that the Franco-American treaty of alliance took precedence over any presidential proclamation. He appealed over the head of the administration directly to the American people, publishing letters and addresses that denounced Washington’s policy as monarchical and pro-British. He threatened to go to the Supreme Court to challenge the administration’s interpretation of the treaty. He even threatened to appeal to Congress against the President.

The crisis came to a head in August 1793, when Genêt commissioned a British prize, the Little Sarah, as a French privateer, renaming her La Petite Démocrate, and ordered her to sail from Philadelphia in direct defiance of the administration’s prohibition. The French minister refused to recognize American authority over a French warship in American waters. Washington’s cabinet met in emergency session and resolved to demand Genêt’s recall. Jefferson, who had initially welcomed Genêt as a symbol of Franco-American friendship, was forced to acknowledge that the minister’s conduct was indefensible. The letter requesting Genêt’s recall was drafted by Jefferson himself — one of the most painful acts of his political career, since it required him to repudiate a man whose excesses had undermined the cause Jefferson believed in.

The Girondins had fallen from power by the time the request reached Paris. The Committee of Public Safety, which now governed France, ordered Genêt’s arrest and sent a replacement. Genêt, knowing what awaited him in revolutionary France, requested asylum in the United States, settled on a farm on Long Island, and became an American citizen. He married the daughter of Governor George Clinton of New York and lived quietly for the rest of his life. The French Revolution had consumed its own, and the American Republic had watched it happen.

The Jay Treaty

The British, meanwhile, had not been passive. The same year that Genêt was mobilizing American ports for France, the Royal Navy was seizing American merchant ships bound for French ports, impressing American seamen into British service, and applying a definition of contraband that treated nearly everything as lawful prize. By 1794, more than two hundred and fifty American vessels had been taken, their cargoes confiscated, their crews dispersed. The Western posts on American territory — Detroit, Michilimackinac, Niagara, Oswego — remained in British hands despite the promise of the Treaty of Paris of 1783 to surrender them. And from those posts, British agents supplied arms to the Native American tribes of the Ohio Valley, who were resisting American expansion with a ferocity that terrified the frontier settlements.

Washington dispatched John Jay, the Chief Justice of the United States, to London in the spring of 1794 to negotiate a settlement. Jay’s instructions were to secure British evacuation of the posts, compensation for the seized ships, a commercial treaty, and a definition of neutral rights that would protect American commerce in future wars. He returned in March 1795 with a treaty that gave the United States some of what it wanted — the British agreed to evacuate the posts by June 1796, and they conceded limited commercial access to the West Indies — but that said nothing about impressment, nothing about neutral rights on the high seas, and nothing about compensation for the slaves carried off by British forces during the Revolutionary War.

The treaty was a disaster for American politics. The Senate ratified it by exactly the required two-thirds majority, twenty votes to ten, in a session so contentious that Jay’s Federalist supporters barely held the line. The House of Representatives, controlled by Jefferson’s Republicans, refused for months to appropriate the funds necessary to implement it. Washington was denounced in the press as a monarchist and a traitor; Jay was burned in effigy from Charleston to Boston; the treaty became the central issue in the presidential election of 1796, which John Adams won by a margin so narrow that the result was uncertain for weeks.

In Paris, the Jay Treaty was received as a deliberate insult. The Republic was fighting for its survival against a coalition of monarchies, and its oldest ally had negotiated a commercial treaty with the enemy. The French Directory, which had replaced the Committee of Public Safety after the fall of Robespierre, interpreted the treaty as evidence that the United States had abandoned the alliance and thrown in its lot with Britain. French privateers began to take American ships with the same abandon that the British had shown. French warships stopped American merchantmen on the high seas and confiscated their cargoes. By 1796, the Franco-American alliance had effectively ceased to exist, replaced by a state of undeclared hostility that could tip into war at any moment.

The Quasi-War

The crisis that followed the Jay Treaty — the undeclared naval war between the United States and France that historians call the Quasi-War — was fought entirely at sea and almost entirely over the question of neutral rights. It began when French privateers, acting under decrees of the Directory that authorized the seizure of neutral vessels carrying British goods, fell upon American shipping in the Caribbean with devastating effect. Between 1796 and 1797, French privateers and warships captured more than three hundred American merchantmen; insurance rates for vessels bound for the Caribbean rose to ruinous levels; the entire American carrying trade in the West Indies, which had grown fat on wartime demand, withered almost overnight.

President John Adams, who took office in March 1797, was caught between the same impossible alternatives that Washington had faced. War with France would ally the United States with Britain — the old enemy, still resented by the Jeffersonian Republicans — and would risk dragging the young republic into the European war it had struggled so hard to avoid. Submission to French depredations would destroy American commerce, humiliate the nation, and confirm the European assumption that American independence was a temporary inconvenience that the great powers could ignore when it suited them. Adams chose the middle path: he would try diplomacy one more time, but he would prepare for war.

The diplomatic effort produced the most famous humiliation in American diplomatic history. Adams sent a three-man commission — Charles Cotesworth Pinckney, John Marshall, and Elbridge Gerry — to Paris in October 1797 to negotiate a settlement of the disputes between the two republics. The Directory, which was then dominated by Paul Barras and the most corrupt elements of the French government, refused to receive them. Instead, three intermediaries — identified in the American dispatches only as agents X, Y, and Z — informed the commissioners that before negotiations could begin, the United States would have to pay a bribe of 50,000 pounds sterling to Talleyrand, the French Foreign Minister, and provide a loan of twelve million dollars to the French government. The Americans refused. “No, no, not a sixpence,” Pinckney was reported to have said. The negotiations collapsed.

When Adams’s dispatches reached Philadelphia in March 1798 — the names of the French agents suppressed as “X, Y, Z” — the effect on American public opinion was explosive. The insult was compounded by the fact that Adams had sent the commissioners in good faith, expecting the French to honor the alliance that had bound the two republics since 1778. Instead, they had been treated as supplicants to be shaken down for money. The Federalists, who had been demanding war with France for two years, had the proof they needed. Congress abrogated the treaties of 1778, authorized the raising of a standing army, and directed the Navy Department — created just three weeks earlier — to capture French armed vessels wherever they could be found.

The naval war that followed was small by European standards but fierce by American ones. The United States Navy in 1798 consisted of a handful of frigates and sloops, none of them designed for a major naval conflict. The three great frigates authorized in 1794 — the United States, the Constitution, and the Constellation — had been built to fight the Barbary pirates, not to engage French warships in the Caribbean. Yet it was the Constellation that wrote the Quasi-War’s most dramatic page.

On February 9, 1799, the Constellation, commanded by Captain Thomas Truxtun, encountered the French frigate L’Insurgente off the coast of Nevis. The L’Insurgente was a fine ship, fast and well-armed, one of the best in the French navy. Truxtun closed despite a gale that made gunnery nearly impossible; within seventy-five minutes, the L’Insurgente had struck her colors, with seventy dead and wounded to one dead and two wounded on the Constellation. The battle was the first significant American naval victory and a shock to the French, who had assumed that the American navy was a negligible force. A year later, the same Constellation fought a savage engagement with the La Vengeance, a larger and more heavily armed French frigate; though neither ship was captured, the French commander broke off the action and fled to Curaçao, and the battle confirmed what Truxtun had proved: that American frigates, American gunners, and American officers could meet the French navy on equal terms and prevail.

Mahan, writing a century later, used the Quasi-War as evidence for a larger argument about sea power and national character. The American navy that fought the French in 1798–1800 was the creation of a single generation — the officers had learned their seamanship in the merchant service and their gunnery on the forefathers’ decks of the Revolution — and it had achieved, in two years of operations, a tactical efficiency that the French navy, with its centuries of tradition, could not match. The reason, in Mahan’s analysis, was structural: the French navy had been destroyed by the Revolution’s ideological contempt for professional standards, while the American navy was being built from scratch by a nation that understood that skill at sea was the product of training, discipline, and experience. The Constellation did not win because Americans were naturally better sailors; she won because her captain was a professional of the old school who had trained his crew to a pitch of efficiency that the French, with their revolutionary officers and politicized crews, could not achieve.

The Convention of 1800

By the end of 1799, the war had settled into a pattern that neither side could break. American frigates and privateers controlled the Caribbean sea lanes; French privateers still occasionally escaped to prey on American shipping; the French army had not invaded, and the American army had not attempted any European expedition. Adams, who had resisted the war fever that swept the Federalist party in 1798, and who had maneuvered against the ambitions of Alexander Hamilton to expand the conflict into a full-scale Anglo-American alliance, began to look for a way out.

The moment came in September 1799, when Talleyrand — the same Talleyrand who had demanded bribes through agents X, Y, and Z — let it be known that the French government was willing to receive an American minister. Adams, without consulting his cabinet or informing the Federalist leadership, nominated William Vans Murray, the American minister to the Netherlands, as minister plenipotentiary to France. The Federalist party was stunned; Hamilton, who had hoped to use the war to build a great army and, perhaps, to conquer Spanish Florida, saw his plans dissolve. But Adams held firm. He had been elected to keep the peace, he wrote, and he would keep it.

The negotiations dragged through 1800. Napoleon Bonaparte, who had seized power in the coup of 18 Brumaire (November 1799), was now First Consul of the French Republic. He had no interest in continuing a naval war with the United States while he was planning the invasion of England and the reconquest of Italy. The treaty signed at Mortefontaine on September 30, 1800 — the Convention of 1800 — was a compromise. The United States agreed to abandon its claims for damages arising from French seizures; France agreed to release the United States from the treaties of 1778; the two nations agreed to mutual most-favored-nation trading status. It was not a peace that satisfied anyone completely, but it was a peace that preserved American neutrality and allowed the United States to grow in strength while the European war raged on.

The Empire Looks West

The Convention of 1800 had barely been ratified when Napoleon Bonaparte began to re-imagine the French empire in America. The Treaty of San Ildefonso (October 1800), signed in secret between France and Spain, retroceded the vast territory of Louisiana — the entire watershed of the Mississippi River, from the Gulf of Mexico to the Canadian border — from Spain back to France. Napoleon’s vision was clear: a new French empire in the Americas, centered on the sugar island of Saint-Domingue and the Mississippi Valley, with the port of New Orleans as the hinge between the two. The army that would take possession of Louisiana would first pacify Saint-Domingue, restoring French authority over the richest colony in the New World, and then march up the Mississippi to claim the continental interior that Jefferson had long coveted for the United States.

The plan failed in the mountains of Saint-Domingue. The army that Napoleon sent to reconquer the island — more than thirty thousand men under his brother-in-law General Leclerc — was destroyed by yellow fever, by the guerrilla tactics of Toussaint Louverture’s former soldiers, and by the British blockade of the island’s ports. By the spring of 1803, barely a third of the original force remained alive. Napoleon, preparing to resume his war with Britain and needing money above all else, made a decision as sudden as it was momentous: he would sell Louisiana to the United States.

Jefferson, now President of the United States, had sent James Monroe to Paris with instructions to negotiate the purchase of New Orleans and the Floridas for up to ten million dollars. Monroe and the American minister Robert R. Livingston found themselves presented with an offer they had not dared to imagine: the entire Louisiana Territory, eight hundred thousand square miles of unmapped land stretching from the Mississippi to the Rocky Mountains, for fifteen million dollars. They signed the treaty on April 30, 1803, without waiting for instructions from Washington. They knew the Constitution gave no explicit authority for the acquisition of foreign territory; they knew the Federalists would oppose the purchase on constitutional grounds; they knew Jefferson himself had argued for a strict construction of federal power. They signed anyway, because the opportunity was too great to lose.

The Louisiana Purchase doubled the territory of the United States at a cost of less than three cents an acre. It made the American republic a continental power, secured the Mississippi Valley for American settlement, and removed the most dangerous potential rival from the western frontier of the United States. It also marked the definitive end of the French Revolution’s American dimension. The France that had been the ally of 1778, the revolutionary sister of 1789, the antagonist of the Quasi-War of 1798, was now a European empire that had abandoned its American ambitions. The United States had gained more from the failure of the French empire than it could ever have gained from the success of the French Revolution.

The Struggle for the Seas

The end of the Quasi-War did not mean the end of conflict between the United States and the European belligerents. The Napoleonic Wars, which resumed in May 1803 after the brief Peace of Amiens, placed American commerce and American neutrality under a pressure far more intense than anything the 1790s had produced.

Napoleon’s Berlin Decree of November 21, 1806, issued from the Prussian capital after the destruction of the Prussian army at Jena, declared the British Isles in a state of blockade. No vessel coming from Britain or its colonies was permitted to enter any French-controlled port; all British subjects in French-controlled territory were declared prisoners of war; all British goods, wherever found, were declared lawful prize. The Decree was not a military measure in any ordinary sense — Napoleon did not have a navy capable of blockading the British coast — but an economic weapon designed to destroy British commerce by closing the entire European continent to its trade.

The British responded with Orders in Council that blockaded any port that excluded British goods. The practical effect for American shipping was devastating. An American merchantman sailing from New York to Bordeaux could be seized by a French privateer for carrying British goods — or by the Royal Navy for trading with France. If she called at a British port to obtain a certificate that her cargo was neutral, she risked being condemned by the French as having submitted to British authority. If she sailed directly to France, she risked being seized by the British for trading with the enemy. The entire structure of neutral maritime commerce, which American merchants had built over two decades of European war, collapsed into a maze of regulations and confiscations that no honest trader could navigate.

The Milan Decree of December 1807 made the situation impossible. Napoleon declared that any vessel that submitted to British search, or that sailed to or from British ports, or that was bound for any port under British blockade, was denationalized and could be taken as lawful prize by French warships. The decree was Napoleon’s attempt to close the last loophole in his Continental System — the ability of neutrals to carry colonial goods to Europe via British ports — but its effect was to make American commerce the victim of both belligerents, each determined to prevent the other from trading at the expense of every neutral right.

The most dramatic single incident of this period was the attack on the American frigate Chesapeake by the British ship-of-the-line Leopard on June 22, 1807, off the coast of Norfolk, Virginia. The Leopard hailed the Chesapeake and demanded the right to search her for British deserters; when the American commander, Commodore James Barron, refused, the Leopard fired three broadsides into the unprepared frigate, killing three men and wounding eighteen. Barron struck his colors and the British boarded, seized four men (three of them American citizens), and departed. The Chesapeake limped back to Norfolk with her blood still on the decks.

The attack was an act of war. No sovereign nation could permit its warship to be fired upon without response. Jefferson, now in his second term as President, faced the same impossible choice that had confronted Washington and Adams. He could declare war on Britain — which would ally the United States with Napoleon, ruin American commerce, and risk the loss of the American experiment — or he could absorb the insult and seek redress through diplomacy. He chose diplomacy, reinforced by an embargo that prohibited American vessels from trading with any foreign port.

The embargo was a catastrophe. American exports fell from 108millionin1807to22 million in 1808. American shipping rotted at the wharves. The merchants of New England, who had grown rich on wartime commerce, were ruined. The sailors and dockworkers who depended on maritime trade were thrown out of work. Smuggling across the Canadian border became a major industry. The embargo was evaded, resented, and eventually repealed in March 1809, replaced by the Non-Intercourse Act, which prohibited trade only with Britain and France. It satisfied no one and solved nothing.

The War of 1812

The war that came in 1812 was fought over the same issues that had produced the Quasi-War and the crises of the 1790s: neutral rights, impressment, the freedom of the seas. It was also fought over the American land frontier, where the British in Canada were arming the Native American confederacy led by Tecumseh, who threatened American settlement in the Ohio and Mississippi valleys. The War Hawks of the new Congress — Henry Clay of Kentucky, John C. Calhoun of South Carolina, Felix Grundy of Tennessee — argued that the only way to secure American sovereignty on the continent was to conquer Canada and break British influence over the Native tribes.

The War of 1812 was not, in any direct sense, a continuation of the French Revolution. The revolutionaries of 1789 had been dead or scattered for years; Napoleon was fighting his war against Russia; the issues that had divided Washington and Hamilton and Jefferson in the 1790s had been resolved by time and circumstance. And yet the war was the culmination of the long American struggle to define its relationship to the French Revolution and its consequences. The United States had been founded in alliance with France, had been divided by the French Revolution, had fought an undeclared naval war with France, had purchased Louisiana from Napoleon, and had been caught between France and Britain for two decades of global conflict. The War of 1812 was the final act of that long entanglement — the moment when the United States, no longer able to avoid choosing between the belligerents, chose to fight the power that threatened its maritime rights and its territorial ambitions.

The war was a draw. The American invasions of Canada failed; the British burned Washington in August 1814; Andrew Jackson’s victory at New Orleans came after the peace treaty had been signed at Ghent. But the war settled the questions that had hung over American foreign policy since 1793. The United States had demonstrated that it would defend its neutral rights by force. It had fought the world’s greatest naval power to a standstill. It had ended the influence of the Native American confederacies in the Old Northwest. And it had established, at last, that the American republic was not a temporary experiment that would be crushed between the anvil of French power and the hammer of British sea power, but a permanent nation that would survive the collapse of every European empire that had tried to control its destiny.

The View from Three Thousand Miles

The French Revolution, seen from the United States, was never the same event that it was in Paris. The Americans watched it through a particular lens — the lens of their own revolution, their own constitution, their own ambitions for the continent they intended to possess. They saw in the French Revolution what they needed to see: a confirmation of their own principles, if they were Jefferson; a warning against popular excess, if they were Hamilton; an opportunity for commercial profit, if they were the merchants of Boston and Philadelphia; a source of diplomatic danger, if they were Washington; a subject for a diary of astonishing intimacy and penetration, if they were Gouverneur Morris.

Morris’s green diary remains the indispensable American witness to the French Revolution, not because Morris was typical of American opinion — he was not — but because he saw the Revolution with a clarity that no American partisan could match. He was inside the events that Jefferson only read about; he was present at the conversations that Hamilton analyzed from across the Atlantic; he held the gold that the royalists could not use and sheltered the men the revolutionaries were hunting. He predicted the failure of the Constitution of 1791, the collapse of the monarchy, and the descent into military dictatorship, and he was right about all of them. And he wrote it all down in a voice so distinct that two centuries later it still sounds like a man speaking directly across a dinner table.

The American experience of the French Revolution ended, as it had begun, with a question about sea power. Mahan, writing in 1892, looking back at the entire period from a perspective that neither Morris nor Jefferson could have imagined, framed the question in its final form. The French Revolution had produced the most formidable army Europe had seen since the legions of Rome. It had conquered Belgium, Holland, the Rhineland, Italy, Switzerland, and Egypt. It had overthrown every coalition that the monarchies of Europe could mount against it. And under Napoleon it had dominated Europe from the Atlantic to the Niemen. But it could not break the control of the sea that Britain maintained, and that failure was ultimately fatal. The French army could not cross the Channel; the French treasury could not draw upon its colonies once the navy was destroyed; the French people could not obtain the colonial goods their economy required; and the French government could not prevent British subsidies from keeping continental armies in the field.

The United States, watching from across the Atlantic, learned the same lesson from the opposite direction. A nation that could not command the respect of the maritime powers could not protect its commerce, could not control its own waters, could not prevent its ships from being seized and its seamen from being impressed. The Quasi-War, the embargo, and the War of 1812 were the costs of learning what sea power meant. The American navy that fought the French in 1798, the British in 1812, and eventually the empires of the Old World in the centuries that followed was born in the struggle to define what the French Revolution meant for the American republic. It was not the only legacy of that long entanglement, but it was one of the most lasting.

Morris, who died at Morrisania in 1816, did not live to see the navy his country built, the continent his country conquered, or the power his country would eventually become. He had known too much about revolutions to believe in their easy promises. He had seen the heads on pikes, held the gold that could not save a king, and watched the most brilliant political experiment of the age descend into war and dictatorship. But he had written it down — the tobacco contracts and the massacres, the love affairs and the constitutions, the hope of 1789 and the terror of 1793 — in a green diary that survives as the record of an American who saw the French Revolution from inside and never lost his nerve.
