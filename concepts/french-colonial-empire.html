French Colonial Empire (1789–1803)

The Empire at the Revolution

On the eve of the Revolution, France possessed the most valuable colonial empire in the world. The jewel was Saint-Domingue — the western third of the island of Hispaniola — which in 1788 alone shipped to France 42.7 million kilograms of raw sugar, 37.6 million kilograms of clayed sugar, 11.8 million kilograms of sugar heads, 38.4 million kilograms of coffee, nearly 5 million kilograms of cotton, and over half a million kilograms of indigo. The total value of colonial produce reaching French ports that year was 218.5 million francs, a sum that gave France a commercial trade balance in its favour of nearly sixty million francs. The colonial trade kept French ports alive: 677 ships carried goods worth 76.8 million francs out to the colonies; 686 ships returned laden with sugar, coffee, cotton, and dyestuffs. The value of the triangular trade — French goods to Africa, African slaves to the Caribbean, colonial produce back to Europe — generated an estimated annual profit of 76 million francs in freight and commercial margins.

To the east lay the other American possessions: Martinique, whose harbour at Fort Royal was the finest naval station in the eastern Caribbean; Guadeloupe, comprising Basse-Terre and Grande-Terre, with its dependent islands of Marie-Galante, La Désirade, and Les Saintes; the restored islands of Sainte-Lucie and Tobago, returned by the Treaty of Amiens in 1802; the fishing outposts of Saint-Pierre and Miquelon off Newfoundland, recovered in 1783; and the sprawling, underdeveloped territory of French Guiana (Cayenne) on the South American mainland. Louisiana — a vast territory encompassing the entire Mississippi basin from Canada to the Gulf of Mexico — was not yet in French hands at the outbreak of the Revolution, having been ceded to Spain in 1763; but the retrocession had been secretly arranged in 1800, and the colony was being reoccupied when the Statistique Générale went to press in 1803.

Across the Atlantic, on the western coast of Africa, France maintained the trading posts that fed the slave economy: the island of Arguin, the oldest; the Senegal River establishments centred on the island of Saint-Louis, with upriver posts at Podor and Galam, trading in gum arabic, gold dust, ivory, and above all in captives; and the island of Gorée, off the Cape Verde peninsula, whose superb natural harbour made it the most important slave-trade entrepôt on the coast. The 1789 merchandise table in the Statistique records the precise commodities by which captives were obtained on the African coast — Indian cottons, European woolens, brandy and rum, firearms, iron bars, copper basins, tobacco — and the price of a prime captive in that year.

In the Indian Ocean lay the Mascarene islands: Île de France (Mauritius) and Île Bourbon (Réunion), administered together under a single captain-general and colonial prefect. Port-Louis on the Île de France was the hub of French Indian Ocean operations, a strategically placed victualling station and naval base for the Asian trade. Île Bourbon produced coffee and spices. The smaller dependencies — Rodrigue, the Seychelles archipelago, Praslin, and Diego Garcia — extended French reach across the western Indian Ocean.

On the Indian subcontinent, the Treaty of 1783 had restored the five comptoirs: Pondichéry on the Coromandel Coast, with its dependent districts of Valanour and Bahour; Karikal to the south, with its four contiguous magans (rural districts); Chandernagor on the Hooghly River near Calcutta, the French factory in the heart of Bengal; and Mahé on the Malabar Coast, the western Indian establishment. These were not territorial possessions in the American or Caribbean sense — they were fortified trading posts, remnants of Dupleix’s vanished empire — but they represented France’s foothold in the commerce of Asia.

The demographic engine of this entire system was slavery. In 1779, according to Necker’s figures, the French American colonies held 426,984 slaves, 61,285 whites, and 12,379 free people of colour — a total of 500,648 souls. By 1788, the slave population had risen to 592,410, the free people of colour to 31,785, while the white population had actually declined to 55,252. Saint-Domingue alone held 405,564 slaves in 1788 — more than two-thirds of the entire colonial enslaved population. The free people of colour, growing from 7,055 to 21,808 in the same period, represented a complex social layer whose legal status the Revolution would fatally fail to resolve.

The Revolutionary Storm

The Revolution struck the colonial empire with devastating force. The first shock was political: the National Assembly’s Declaration of the Rights of Man and the Citizen raised the question of whether free people of colour — who owned property, paid taxes, and often held slaves themselves — were entitled to citizenship. The planters resisted fiercely, the Assembly equivocated, and the resulting confusion destroyed the legitimacy of metropolitan authority in the colonies.

But the second shock was far more consequential. In August 1791, the slaves of the northern plain of Saint-Domingue rose in a coordinated insurrection that within weeks destroyed hundreds of sugar plantations and killed thousands of whites. The revolt spread across the colony, and by 1792 a former slave named Toussaint Bréda — later Toussaint Louverture — had emerged as a military and political leader of extraordinary ability. The colony descended into a three-cornered war among whites, free people of colour, and the insurgent slave army, with Spain and Britain watching from their neighbouring colonies.

The National Convention, in a dramatic bid to secure the allegiance of the insurgents, voted to abolish slavery in all French colonies on 4 February 1794 (16 Pluviôse an II). The decree was universal in language and revolutionary in intent — all men in the colonies, regardless of colour, were declared French citizens. In Saint-Domingue, the effect was to bring Toussaint Louverture over to the French side. By 1798, he had expelled both the Spanish and the British from the colony and was master of the entire island, governing it as a de facto autonomous state while nominally recognising French sovereignty.

The Colonial Naval War: Mahan’s Analysis

Admiral Mahan’s account of the colonial dimension of the war is a study in the strategic logic of sea power. The West Indies, he wrote, were among the leading objects of British war policy — not merely for their direct commercial value, but because they produced about one-quarter of all British trade, export and import combined. The French islands were the wealthiest in the region, and the British strategy was straightforward: seize them, deny their harbours to French privateers, and cut off the colonial revenue that sustained the French war effort.

The campaign opened in 1793 with a British squadron under Admiral Gardner sailing for the West Indies. In February 1794, a larger force under Sir John Jervis (later Earl St. Vincent) and General Grey appeared off Martinique. The island capitulated on 22 March 1794. Sainte-Lucie fell on 4 April; Guadeloupe on 20 April; Tobago had been taken the year before. Within three months, Great Britain possessed every French Windward Island of importance.

But the triumph was short-lived. In June 1794, a French expedition that had sailed from Rochefort before the news of the islands’ loss reached Europe — two frigates and a handful of transports — appeared off Guadeloupe. Its commander was Victor Hugues, a former merchant and now a revolutionary commissioner of terrifying energy. Landing almost unopposed, Hugues armed the free coloured population and the newly freed slaves, re-established the authority of the Republic, and conducted a guerrilla war that the British could not suppress. The British garrison wasted away from yellow fever; reinforcements arrived only to fall to the same disease. By the end of 1794, Guadeloupe was firmly in French hands, and Hugues was using it as a base for a swarm of privateers that ravaged British commerce throughout the Caribbean. Mahan called this the clearest warning in the war against the folly of relying upon local support with troops inadequate to the task.

The British spent the next two years recovering: Sainte-Lucie was retaken in May 1796, St. Vincent and Grenada pacified, the Dutch mainland colonies of Demerara, Essequibo, and Berbice seized. But Guadeloupe itself remained French until 1810 — a thorn in the side of British trade for the entire war. Mahan’s judgment is precise: the recapture of Guadeloupe and its consequences were the result of a fatal miscalculation — sending ‘a boy to do a man’s work’ — and of deploying forces insufficient to hold what had been taken.

Saint-Domingue presented an entirely different problem. Its size — the French part alone was larger than any island in the Lesser Antilles — and its population made conquest impossible with the forces Britain could spare. The British occupied several coastal ports (Môle Saint-Nicolas, captured in 1793, was held until 1798) but never controlled the interior. Yellow fever consumed the garrisons. In 1798, the British commander concluded a treaty with Toussaint Louverture and evacuated the island, acknowledging that the colony could not be taken by force.

Mahan’s larger argument about the colonial theatre is that the Caribbean campaigns reveal the essential dependence of colonial possession upon sea power — but also the limits of that power. A navy could seize islands; it could deny harbours to an enemy’s cruisers; it could escort convoys and protect trade. What it could not do was govern an alien population against its will, or survive indefinitely in a climate that killed more soldiers than enemy action. The West Indies were a theatre where maritime supremacy was necessary but not sufficient — as the French would learn in their turn during the Leclerc expedition.

The Consulate’s Colonial Policy

When Bonaparte seized power in November 1799, the French colonial empire was a shadow of its former self. Saint-Domingue was effectively independent under Toussaint Louverture. Martinique remained in British hands. Guadeloupe was French but governed by a regime of revolutionary commissioners whose methods — the guillotine and forced labour under military supervision — were a world away from the stable plantation economy Bonaparte wanted to restore. Louisiana was still Spanish, though the retrocession was being negotiated. The Indian Ocean islands and the African comptoirs remained, but their commerce was a fraction of the pre-revolutionary volume.

The Peace of Amiens in March 1802 changed the picture entirely. The treaty restored to France all its captured colonies. Martinique, Sainte-Lucie, and Tobago were returned. The Indian comptoirs — Pondichéry, Karikal, Chandernagor, Mahé — were to be handed back. The retrocession of Louisiana from Spain, arranged by the Treaty of San Ildefonso in 1800, was confirmed. The Statistique Générale of 1803 was in a very real sense a document of imperial restoration — its preface traced the losses of 1713, 1763, and the Revolutionary wars, then noted how Amiens had returned France to a position from which she might again rival the other European powers in colonial commerce.

The Consulate moved quickly to establish a new administrative framework for the restored empire. The arrêté of 6 Prairial an X (26 May 1802) established a tripartite colonial government for Martinique, Sainte-Lucie, and Tobago, intended as a model for the rest. A captain-general held command of land and sea forces, directed defence, controlled passports and fortifications; a colonial prefect managed civil administration, agriculture, commerce, and finance; and a grand judge oversaw the judiciary and the enforcement of law. The three magistrates communicated with each other through defined protocols and reported to the minister of the navy and colonies in Paris. The old regime of governor and intendant — with its ambiguities and jurisdictional conflicts — was replaced by a structure designed for unified command and clear responsibility.

The Leclerc Expedition and the Loss of Saint-Domingue

The centrepiece of Bonaparte’s colonial policy was the recovery of Saint-Domingue. The colony was too valuable to lose — its pre-revolutionary exports had exceeded those of all the other French colonies combined — and Toussaint Louverture’s autonomous regime, while nominally loyal to France, was in practice an independent state with its own army, constitution, and foreign policy. Bonaparte resolved to restore direct metropolitan control.

In December 1801, an expedition of some 20,000 veterans under Bonaparte’s brother-in-law, General Charles Leclerc, sailed from Brest. It was the largest French force ever sent across the Atlantic. The plan was to land, disarm the black army, arrest Toussaint, and re-establish the plantation economy — with slavery implicitly restored, though this was not openly stated.

The expedition succeeded in its initial military objectives. Toussaint was captured in June 1802 and deported to France, where he died in prison at Fort de Joux in April 1803. But the underlying problem was the same one that had defeated the British: the climate and the resistance of the population. Yellow fever ravaged the French army with such ferocity that by the end of 1802, Leclerc himself was dead of the disease, and perhaps two-thirds of the original expeditionary force had perished. The black army, now under Jean-Jacques Dessalines, resumed hostilities in late 1802 when the news reached Saint-Domingue that slavery had been reinstated in Guadeloupe (by General Richepanse, acting on Bonaparte’s orders).

The French army in Saint-Domingue collapsed. By November 1803, the remnant of the expedition had evacuated the colony. On 1 January 1804, Dessalines declared the independence of Haiti — the first independent nation in Latin America and the first state born of a successful slave revolt.

The loss of Saint-Domingue was an economic catastrophe for France. The colony that had produced 218 million francs in colonial goods in 1788 was gone forever. The Leclerc expedition had cost tens of thousands of lives and enormous sums, and what little remained of French naval strength had been consumed in the operation — a fact of strategic consequence when war with Britain resumed in May 1803.

The Sale of Louisiana

The Louisiana territory had been returned to France by Spain in 1800 under the secret Treaty of San Ildefonso, but the transfer was not completed until 1802. The Statistique Générale of 1803 treats Louisiana as a French possession — ‘the voice of the public announces that it has been ceded to France, to whom it once belonged’ — and devotes an extended geographical description to the Mississippi basin, its navigation, the city of New Orleans, and its commercial prospects. But even as Herbin’s volume was being printed, the situation was changing.

The failure of the Leclerc expedition made Louisiana untenable. Without Saint-Domingue as the Caribbean anchor of a French American empire, the strategic logic of holding the Mississippi valley collapsed. The territory could not be defended against British naval power, and its retention would only invite capture in the war that was clearly about to resume. Bonaparte, who had dreamed of a revived French America, now saw Louisiana as a liability.

In April 1803 — the same year as the Statistique’s publication — the Consulate sold the entire Louisiana territory to the United States for 60 million francs (approximately $15 million). The sale doubled the size of the young American republic and removed France from the North American continent for the second time in four decades. It was the end of the French empire in America.

The Empire in 1803: Statistical Balance

The Statistique Générale records the colonial empire at the precise moment of its maximum extension under the Consulate — and on the eve of its dissolution. By the standards of 1803, France held more colonial territory than at any time since 1763: the American colonies (Martinique, Guadeloupe with its dependencies, Sainte-Lucie, Tobago, Saint-Pierre and Miquelon, Cayenne, the restored Louisiana); the African posts (Arguin, Senegal, Gorée, and the Gambia factories, with continuing relations with the Barbary states); the Indian Ocean islands (Île de France, Île Bourbon, Rodrigue, the Seychelles, Diego Garcia); and the five Indian comptoirs.

But the statistical reality was hollow. Saint-Domingue — which had accounted for perhaps two-thirds of the pre-revolutionary colonial trade — was lost in all but name. The 1788 export figures that Herbin reproduces in his volume could serve as a monument to a vanished prosperity, not as a guide to current revenue. The slave trade, whose 1789 merchandise table appears in the same pages, had been legally abolished in 1794; the Consulate had effectively permitted it to resume in the colonies that remained under French control, but the labour system on which the plantation economy rested had been fatally undermined by the Haitian Revolution and the British blockade.

What remained were the islands of the Lesser Antilles, whose combined output was a fraction of Saint-Domingue’s; the Indian Ocean sugar and coffee plantations, which had never approached the scale of the Caribbean production; the African trading posts, whose commercial significance declined as the slave trade came under increasing scrutiny; and the Indian comptoirs, whose trade was circumscribed by British dominance in the subcontinent.

The colonial empire that Herbin described in 1803 was a restoration only in the territorial sense. The economic structure that had made it the envy of Europe — the integrated system of slave plantations, Atlantic commerce, and metropolitan processing and re-export — was broken beyond repair. The Treaty of Amiens had given France back its colonies, but the Revolution had given France back its empire in name only. Within two years of the Statistique’s publication, Louisiana would be sold, Saint-Domingue would be independent, and the renewed war with Britain would strip France of most of what remained. The French colonial empire of the eighteenth century ended not in a single catastrophe but in a prolonged dissolution between 1789 and 1804, of which the Haitian Revolution was the decisive event.
