The Continental System

The Prelude: Commerce Raiding and the Road to Economic War, 1793–1806

The war against commerce during the French Revolution and Empire was marked from its opening by the same passionate vehemence that characterised the political and military enterprises of the period. Both France and Great Britain, in their effort to bring the commerce of the whole world under the yoke of their respective policies, swayed back and forth in a deadly grapple that trampled under foot the rights and interests of every weaker party. The question between Napoleon and the British people became simply one of endurance: like two infuriated dogs, they had locked jaws over commerce as the decisive element in the contest.

At the outbreak of war in 1793, both sides issued the customary instructions to commanders and privateers to seize enemy merchant vessels and neutrals violating international law. The sea swarmed with hastily equipped cruisers. But while the great British fleets gradually established a distinct preponderance over the masses of the enemy, France was quickly reduced to the ordinary operations of commerce-destroying — the guerre de course — a policy to which both national tradition and the opinion of many eminent naval officers particularly inclined. By 1795 the Directory formally abandoned the policy of keeping great fleets together and committed France wholly to the war against commerce.

To these raids upon their shipping the British opposed a twofold system. The first was the convoy system: merchant vessels bound to different quarters of the globe were gathered in specified ports and sailed together under the protection of ships of war. From two to three hundred ships was not an unusual gathering; five hundred or even a thousand were at times seen together in the Chops of the Channel or the entrance to the Baltic. The second was the system of patrol: fast frigates and sloops-of-war, with a host of smaller vessels, were disseminated over the ocean upon the tracks that commerce followed. The Convoy Act of 1798 compelled the taking of convoy and the payment of a sum for protection; in its first year this tax brought in £1,292,000 to the Treasury while resulting in a yet greater saving of insurance to owners.

The losses on both sides were substantial but never decisive. Over the twenty-one years of war approximately eleven thousand British vessels were captured — an annual average of about 524 ships, representing roughly two and a half per cent of British commerce. This loss, while painful, was partially offset by prizes taken by British cruisers and by the enormous expansion of British trade carried on under neutral flags. The British merchant marine, with its enormous tonnage and global reach, absorbed losses that would have shattered any smaller trading nation. Meanwhile French commerce was annihilated: in 1799 the Directory admitted that not a single merchant ship carrying the French flag remained on the sea. French exports to Asia, Africa, and America together amounted in 1800 to less than $56,000.

The neutral carriers — particularly the United States, whose merchant tonnage exploded from 355,000 tons in 1790 to 799,304 in 1801 — stepped into the gap. But this neutral carrying trade provoked escalating restrictions from both belligerents. Great Britain enforced the Rule of 1756, forbidding neutrals from carrying trade in peacetime that was closed to them; France, through the Directory’s law of January 18, 1798, subjected neutral vessels to seizure and condemnation. By 1806, both belligerents had moved far beyond the Law of Nations as it had been understood in 1793. The war was consuming the legal order as surely as it consumed men and treasure.

The Berlin Decree

On November 21, 1806, from the Prussian capital that he had entered after the battle of Jena, Napoleon issued the Berlin Decree. Its essential underlying idea was to crush the commerce of Great Britain by closing the entire European continent to her products of every kind. The pretext was found in the British Order in Council of May 16, 1806, issued by the ministry of Grenville and Fox, which had placed the coast of the Continent from Brest to the Elbe under blockade. Napoleon asserted — falsely — that the right to blockade applied only to fortified, not to commercial, ports; and argued, with some plausibility, that the united forces of Great Britain were unable to maintain so extensive an operation.

Retaliating an alleged abuse with a far greater excess, the Berlin Decree began by declaring the British Isles blockaded — at a time when the emperor could not keep a ship at sea except as a fugitive from the omnipresent fleets of his enemy. From this phantom blockade it followed that all commerce with the British Isles was forbidden; all merchandise exported from them became good prize; vessels from Great Britain could not be admitted into French ports. Not only was the property of individual Englishmen on the Continent to be seized, but also that of individual neutrals, if of British origin. The preamble ended with a clause defining the decree as a fundamental principle of the Empire, to remain in force until England acknowledged that the law of war was one and the same on land as on the sea — that it could not be extended to private property of whatever kind, nor to the person of individuals not in the military profession.

Having launched his missile, Napoleon immediately engaged in the campaign against Russia. The bloody and doubtful battle of Eylau was fought on February 8, 1807, and for the next several months the emperor was too busily engaged holding on by his teeth on the banks of the Vistula to superintend the working of his decree. The whole edict remained a dead letter until the struggle with Russia was decided. American vessels continued to trade freely with the Continent, and British merchants, at first desisting from sending goods, soon resumed shipments by neutral vessels as briskly as before. The decree would remain inoperative until Napoleon could enforce it by military control of the entire European coastline.

The British Response — Orders in Council (1807, 1809)

While the Berlin Decree lay dormant, negotiations were proceeding in London between the United States envoys and the British ministry concerning matters in dispute between the two countries. On December 31, 1806, a commercial treaty was signed, but it was not ratified by the United States government.

Meanwhile the British ministry determined to retort by a measure of retaliation. On January 7, 1807, an Order in Council was issued by the Whig ministers. Citing Napoleon’s decree, it avowed his Majesty’s unwillingness to carry retaliation to extremes and for the present went no further than to forbid all trade by neutral vessels from one hostile port to another — that is, the coastwise trade of France and her allies. This bore particularly hard upon American ships, which were in the habit of going from place to place in Europe seeking the best markets. Under this order American trade in the Mediterranean was swept away by seizures and condemnations.

A change of ministry in March 1807 brought back into power the party that had been in opposition since the death of Pitt. The Berlin Decree had remained inoperative through Napoleon’s absence in Poland, but the emperor’s return to Paris in the summer of 1807 presaged a new phase of the economic conflict. On November 11, 1807, were published the great retaliatory measures of Great Britain — the Orders in Council of that date — which for the moment filled the cup of neutrals to overflowing.

Setting forth the Berlin Decree as their justifying ground, the Orders proclaimed a paper blockade of the barest form and most extensive scope: all ports of France and her allies, and all other ports in Europe from which the British flag was excluded, were declared subject to the same restrictions as if actually blockaded in the most strict and rigorous manner. All trade in hostile colonial produce was likewise declared unlawful for neutrals.

The object of the Orders was twofold: to embarrass France by the prohibition of direct import and export trade, and at the same time to force into the Continent all British products and manufactures that it could take. “No trade except through England” was the formula: neutrals might not trade directly with any ports in Europe not open to British ships, but they might trade with them by first going to a British port, landing their cargo, reshipping it subject to certain duties, and then proceeding to a hostile port. The same process was to be observed on the return voyage. The commerce of the Continent thus paid toll going and coming.

Anything more humiliating and vexatious to neutrals could scarcely be conceived. The Orders trampled upon all previously received law, upon men’s inbred ideas of their rights, by sheer uncontrolled force. Yet they were a very real and severe measure of retaliation. The additional expense entailed upon every cargo that reached the Continent after passing through British ports — the expenses of delay, unloading and reloading, wharfage, licenses, maintenance — fell chiefly upon the continental consumer. And Great Britain, while unable to blockade every individual French port, was able to make the approach to the French coast exceedingly dangerous. In the English Channel she controlled the approaches from the Atlantic to all the northern continental ports; at Gibraltar she controlled those to the Mediterranean. The Orders were by no means an empty threat.

The Milan Decree

Napoleon was not the man to accept the Orders in Council silently. On October 27, 1807, he signed the treaty of Fontainebleau with Spain, arranging the partition of Portugal and taking the first step in the invasion of the Peninsula. On November 16 he left Fontainebleau to visit his kingdom of Italy. From the capital, Milan, on December 17, 1807, he issued the decree that bears its name.

Alleging the Orders as its motive, the Milan Decree declared that any ship that submitted to search by a British cruiser was thereby “denationalised” — outlawed — and liable to arrest as a vagrant. The decree further declared that all vessels going to or sailing from Great Britain were for that fact alone good prize, a point that under the Berlin Decree had been left open. French privateers were still sufficiently numerous to make these regulations a great additional danger to ships at sea; and the decree added that when vessels came under the previous provisions, they should be seized whenever they entered a French port.

The two belligerents had now laid down the general lines of policy on which they intended to act. Napoleon never revoked his Berlin and Milan Decrees, although by a trick he induced an over-eager President of the United States to believe that he had done so. The Orders in Council received various modifications over the following years, but the entire series was finally withdrawn only in June 1812 — too late to avert the war with the United States that was declared in the same month.

The System in Operation

The Continental System, once Napoleon had the military power to enforce it, extended over the entire coastline of Europe from the Baltic to the Adriatic. Its operation was shaped not merely by decrees but by the interaction of naval power, geography, and human ingenuity.

The Portuguese Court’s Flight to Brazil

In July 1807, while still at Dresden, Napoleon notified Portugal that she must choose between war with France or with Great Britain. A French corps under Junot assembled in the south of France to invade Portugal. The British government, which had received certain information of the secret articles agreed to at Tilsit, foresaw the danger. A squadron appeared off Lisbon at the critical moment, and its presence determined the vacillating Portuguese regent to withdraw to Brazil. When Junot’s army — which had made a march of extreme suffering with great rapidity, losing most of its numbers by privation, exposure, and straggling — entered Lisbon on November 30, 1807, it found the Portuguese fleet gone and the court and its treasure departed with it.

The Second Copenhagen Expedition

Simultaneously, Napoleon moved against Denmark. The British ministry had received intelligence of the secret articles of Tilsit and understood the danger of allowing the Danish and Portuguese navies to fall into the emperor’s hands. Early in August 1807, twenty-five sail-of-the-line entered the Baltic, convoying transports with twenty-seven thousand troops. The island on which Copenhagen stands was invested by the ships and the town by the army. The Danish government was summoned to surrender its fleet into British safe keeping, with a pledge that all maritime equipment delivered would be held as a deposit and restored at a general peace. The offer being refused, the city was bombarded from September 2 to September 5, after which the Danes yielded. The British took possession of eighteen sail-of-the-line and a number of frigates, stripped the dockyards of their stores, and returned to England.

Though foiled in his endeavours to seize the fleets, Napoleon had succeeded in formally closing the ports of both Denmark and Portugal to British goods. The bombardment of Copenhagen served as a pretext for Russia’s declaration of hostility against Great Britain on October 20, 1807. Prussia, under the compulsion of the two empires, closed her ports against British commerce by a proclamation on September 2. Austria acceded to the Continental System and excluded British goods from her borders. In Italy, the kingdom of Etruria showed little zeal in enforcing Napoleon’s commands; the British carried on commerce at Leghorn as freely as at any port in their own country. By the emperor’s orders the viceroy of Italy therefore took possession of the city, while French detachments entered the Papal States, occupied their coasts, and drove the British from them. With Joseph Bonaparte already king of Naples, Napoleon’s control extended over both coasts of Italy. Turkey, being involved in hostilities with Great Britain, completed the ring.

The System’s Economic Effects

The effects of this vast closure on the European economy were profound. Ports rotted idle. Colonial goods — sugar, coffee, cotton, tobacco — vanished from the continental markets or reached them only through channels of desperate ingenuity. Smuggling became a universal industry. The coasts of the North Sea, between the mouths of the Ems, the Weser, and the Elbe, and those of Danish Holstein — low-lying, difficult of approach for large vessels, favourable to small boats — fostered clandestine trade. The British seized Heligoland in September 1807 and converted it into a depot for goods waiting to be introduced into Germany and Holstein. A garrison of six hundred men defended the island while ships of war cruised continually in its neighbourhood; from there contraband traders obtained merchandise with which they supplied the Continent. Between five and six hundred women were employed by the merchants of Hamburg daily to convey into the city, each of them, fourteen pounds of coffee and other produce concealed beneath their garments.

In Holland, where Napoleon had placed his brother Louis on the throne in 1806, the king sought from the first to identify himself with his new subjects and constantly withstood Napoleon’s commands in favour of their maritime commerce. Evasions were frequent; the decrees were openly disregarded on various pretences. From Holland goods found their way without great difficulty into France, though on two occasions Napoleon punished Holland for her waywardness by closing the frontier against her.

In the Baltic the effectiveness of the system depended upon the heartiness of the czar in the cause. Decrees of great severity were issued, and the British flag was honestly excluded; but the quick mercantile intelligence of the era soon detected that no ill-timed curiosity as to ships’ papers would be exercised. British commerce was carried on in Russian ports by neutrals armed with British licenses. The czar had never promised to exclude neutrals or to forbid all export and import; it was none of his business to pry behind the papers that covered transactions essential to his people.

American Neutrality and the Embargo

The United States was caught between the two belligerents. Napoleon’s decisions of September 18, 1807, communicated to Congress on December 18, together with a British proclamation directing the impressment of British seamen found serving on foreign merchant ships, moved President Jefferson to recommend an embargo to keep American vessels in their own ports. An Act of Embargo was approved on December 22, 1807, forbidding all registered United States vessels to depart from the ports in which they were then lying.

The Embargo continued in force throughout 1808 and until March 1, 1809, when it was repealed and replaced by a Non-Intercourse Act that allowed American merchant ships to go abroad but forbade them to trade with Great Britain, France, or their colonies. This was the law governing American intercourse with the two great belligerents until May 1810.

Napoleon, meanwhile, had in April 1808 issued an order directing the sequestration of all American ships entering the ports of France, Italy, Holland, and the Hanse towns, on the ground that since the Embargo prevented them from having lawfully left their own country, their papers must be fabricated and they must really have come from England. Under this ruling sequestrations continued until March 23, 1810, when the Decree of Rambouillet confiscated the vessels and cargoes thus seized.

The Expansion and Cracking of the System

Tilsit: Russia Enters the System

The battle of Friedland, fought on June 14, 1807, resulted in the total defeat of the Russian army. An armistice was signed on June 22, and on June 25 Alexander and Napoleon had their first interview upon the raft in the Niemen. On July 8 was concluded the remarkable and threatening Treaty of Tilsit. The czar recognised all the new states created by the emperor and ceded to him the Ionian Islands and the mouths of the Cattaro in the Adriatic; in return, Napoleon acquiesced in Russia’s taking Finland from Sweden and, under certain conditions, the European provinces of the Turkish Empire as far as the Balkans. A further clause bound Russia to adopt the Continental System — to close her ports against British commerce and join the economic war against England.

The two empires had divided Europe between them. For Napoleon, this was the moment when the Continental System ceased to be a paper project and became the dominant economic fact of European life. From the Sound to the Hellespont, every port was nominally closed to British trade.

The Peninsular War Begins (1808)

In 1808 Napoleon’s purpose to overthrow the Spanish monarchy and place one of his own family upon the throne finally matured. He left Paris on April 2, reached Bayonne, and there engineered the resignation of the Spanish king. But on May 2 an insurrection in Madrid was followed by spontaneous popular risings throughout Spain. On July 21 an army corps under General Dupont was cut off by insurgents in Andalusia and surrendered at Baylen, eighteen thousand strong. On July 29 the new king of Spain, Joseph Bonaparte, fled from Madrid.

On August 1 a British fleet appeared off the coast of Portugal bearing the first division of troops destined to act in the Peninsula under Sir Arthur Wellesley. On August 21 the battle of Vimiero was fought, resulting in the defeat of Junot, who by the Convention of Cintra was permitted to evacuate Portugal and was conveyed to France in British transports. Portugal was wholly lost, and the French army in Spain fell back to the line of the Ebro.

Napoleon realised the necessity of suppressing the general uprising and determined to take the field in person. But before removing to this distant scene of action, he confirmed his understanding with the czar at a second meeting at Erfurt in September 1808. The alliance formed at Tilsit was renewed. The czar had already begun hostilities against Sweden; Denmark had declared war against Sweden as well; Prussia closed her ports against Swedish commerce. The vital importance of the Baltic to Great Britain — as the source of naval stores and a channel for commerce into the Continent remote from Napoleon’s vigilance — imposed upon her the necessity of supporting Sweden. A fleet of sixty-two sail, of which sixteen were of the line, was sent through the Sound under Sir James Saumarez.

The Peninsular War proved to be a catastrophe for the Continental System. The Spanish uprising gave Britain a foothold on the Continent and, for the first time, a continental army that she could supply and sustain entirely by sea. The French could not turn the British flank because they could not control the sea; they could not starve the British army because it was fed by sea. Wellington’s army in Portugal and Spain was supplied, reinforced, and sustained entirely by maritime power, and the Peninsular War became the final demonstration of what sea power meant: not a weapon of conquest but the condition that made endurance possible.

Austria’s Revival (1809)

Austria, since the Peace of Presburg in 1805, had been quietly engaged in restoring her military strength. The destruction of Prussia, the establishment of the Confederation of the Rhine, the foundation of the Duchy of Warsaw, and her own territorial losses had left Austria in a position she could not possibly accept as final. Napoleon undertook his Spanish enterprise with the full knowledge that his absence in the Peninsula must be short; he understood that a prolonged stay there would give Austria the opportunity she needed. This hope was frustrated by the action of Sir John Moore, whose daring move to the north in December 1808 turned Napoleon from his projected march on Lisbon and forced him into a winter campaign in northwestern Spain.

The year 1809 therefore opened with the prospect of war between France and Austria. Napoleon, at the head of eighty thousand men, was driving some twenty thousand British troops through the snows of northwestern Spain — but Austria was arming. The war that followed — the campaign of Essling and Wagram — absorbed Napoleon’s attention for the rest of the year, and in his absence trade with the North Sea ports went on almost as in time of peace.

The License System

Among the multiplied impediments to commerce there arose an extensive usage of licenses — individual permissions to carry on traffic forbidden by the existing laws of the issuing authority. The British government adopted the license system in 1808, immediately after the Orders in Council and the alliance of Russia with Napoleon. The numbers grew from 2,606 in 1807 to over 15,000 in 1809 and over 18,000 in 1810. The licenses, which protected against British cruisers, were primarily used for trade to the Baltic ports, though they also covered voyages to Holland, France, Spain, and other continental countries. After 1810 there was a rapid falling off — not from a change of system, but from the bitter discovery that the license that protected against a British cruiser did not save the ship upon arrival in a port under Napoleon’s control, when the emperor at last devoted his indomitable energy to the thorough enforcement of his decrees.

Growing Russian Tensions

In the Baltic, the czar was wearying of his engagements with France. He had adhered faithfully to the letter of his bargain and refused admission to British ships, but he would not open his eyes to the fact that British commerce was being carried on in his ports by neutrals with British licenses. The imports to Great Britain of naval stores, mainly from the Baltic, more than doubled from 1808 to 1809. The czar’s nobles disapproved of the French alliance; the distress of his subjects from the cessation of trade with Britain was palpable. Napoleon, by demanding ever stricter enforcement — by pressing for measures that would close Russian ports to neutral carriers entirely — was pushing Alexander toward the breaking point.

The Continental System, which had seemed Napoleon’s masterstroke, was creating the conditions for his eventual downfall. It alienated the populations and rulers he needed to keep the system closed. It forced the Spanish people into a national insurrection that Britain could support. It strained the alliance with Russia beyond endurance. And it gave Britain — which commanded the sea and had access to world markets — the strategic advantage of time. The system could not defeat a nation with command of the sea; it could only impoverish the continent that had to enforce it.

By 1810 the cracks were spreading. The Peninsular War bled French resources. Austria had risen and been beaten but was gathering strength. The czar’s compliance was visibly flagging. Napoleon’s own letters from this period reveal the strain: the emperor wrote to his brother Louis of Holland that he could close the Rhine, the Weser, the Scheldt, and the Meuse to Holland and place her in a position more critical than by declaring war. He threatened to reunite Holland to France. The system was devouring its own children.

The Balance of the System

The Continental System inflicted serious hardship on Britain. The exclusion from continental markets was real and painful; British exports to Europe fell sharply in certain years; the distress among manufacturing districts was at times acute. The financial strain was severe, and the years 1810–1811 witnessed a protracted commercial crisis in which British merchants groaned under the weight of accumulated inventories and restricted markets.

But the system did not bring Britain to terms — nor, on the evidence, could it have done so. The British merchant marine, protected by convoy and by the commanding position of the Royal Navy, carried on a global commerce that sustained British credit, taxation, and subsidy capacity. British exports found their way into Europe through every chink in Napoleon’s wall: through Heligoland, through the Baltic neutrals, through the license system, through the corruption of customs officials, through the smuggling networks that implicated entire populations. The continental peoples, with few exceptions, resented the French blockade and co-operated with the evaders. The British instinctively understood that the hearts of the continental peoples were with them rather than with Napoleon — for much the same reason that the United States, contrary to the general interests of mankind, sided upon the whole against Great Britain: the immediate oppressor was the object of hatred.

For Napoleon himself, the system was a trap. It demanded the absolute closure of the entire European coastline — a task that required military control of every port from the Baltic to the Adriatic, the co-operation or subjugation of every continental power, and the suppression of a universal instinct for commerce that no decree could eradicate. To maintain the system he had to extend his dominion further and further — into Spain, into Portugal, into the Papal States, into Holland, into the Hanseatic cities — each extension provoking new resistance, each conquest demanding new garrisons, each enforcement breeding new resentment. When Russia, the indispensable linchpin of the system in the east, finally defied the blockade by reopening her ports to neutral shipping in 1810, Napoleon had only one answer: war. The invasion of Russia in 1812 was not merely a punishment for Russian defiance; it was the logical consequence of a system that could tolerate no exception and no neutrality.

The Continental System thus accomplished what no British fleet could have achieved by itself: it created the conditions for Napoleon’s destruction. The Peninsular War bled France of men and prestige; the Russian campaign consumed the Grand Army; the coalitions that finally defeated Napoleon in 1813–1814 were sustained by British subsidies drawn from the very commerce the system had tried to destroy. The system that was meant to starve Britain instead starved the continent of everything except the will to resist the emperor who had impoverished it.
