Sea Power as a Concept

Mahan’s Retrospective Thesis

The argument that sea power determined the outcome of the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars belongs, in its fully developed form, not to the eighteenth century but to the 1890s. It is the creation of Rear Admiral Alfred Thayer Mahan, an American naval officer writing at the United States Naval War College a century after the events he analysed. Mahan’s great work, The Influence of Sea Power upon the French Revolution and Empire, 1793–1812 (1892), is not a naval history in the ordinary sense. It is a sustained theoretical argument, using the Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars as a case study to demonstrate a universal proposition: that control of the sea is the decisive factor in the outcome of great conflicts.

It is essential to understand what this means — and what it does not mean. The men who fought the Revolutionary Wars did not think in terms of ‘sea power’ as Mahan defined it. Nelson, Pitt, Bonaparte, and the statesmen of the era understood that the navy was important; they did not possess a systematic theory of maritime supremacy as a unified force encompassing commerce, colonies, naval strength, and national character. Mahan’s framework is a retrospective construct, imposed upon the events of 1793–1815 to reveal a pattern that contemporaries saw only dimly and in fragments. This page presents Mahan’s framework as he himself presented it: not as ‘how the revolutionaries understood the sea’ but as ‘how sea power, rightly understood, determined what happened to the revolutionaries.’

The central proposition, in Mahan’s own account, is this: a nation that controls the sea controls the terms of the conflict. It can strike where it chooses, supply its allies, starve its enemies, and prevent invasion of its own shores. Its commerce flourishes while that of its adversary withers; its credit remains sound while the enemy’s treasury empties; its people are sustained by the resources of the whole world while the enemy’s are confined to what lies within reach of his armies. The nation that loses control of the sea may still win battles, may still conquer territory, may still compel the submission of continental states — but it does so at an ever-mounting cost, and in the end it cannot prevent the maritime power from assembling against it a coalition that it cannot defeat.

This was the fate that overcame France. The Revolution produced the most formidable army Europe had seen since the Roman legions; it conquered Belgium, Holland, the Rhineland, Italy, Switzerland, and Egypt; it overthrew every coalition that the powers of the Continent could mount against it; and under Napoleon it dominated Europe from the Atlantic to the Niemen. But it never succeeded in challenging British control of the sea after the first years of the war, and that failure proved ultimately fatal. The French army could not cross the Channel; the French treasury could not draw upon the colonies once the navy was destroyed; the French people could not obtain the colonial products and raw materials that their economy required; and the French government could not prevent British subsidies from keeping continental armies in the field against it. Sea power, in Mahan’s reading, was the foundation upon which the entire edifice of resistance to the Revolution and the Empire was built.

The Definition of Sea Power

Mahan’s first move is to define his central term. ‘Sea power’ in his usage is not synonymous with naval strength. It is a broader concept encompassing three elements that together form a unified system: the merchant shipping that carries a nation’s trade, the colonies that produce the commodities that trade requires, and the navy that protects both. These three elements are interdependent. A nation without merchant shipping cannot develop a navy, because it has no pool of trained seamen; a nation without colonies cannot generate the wealth to sustain a navy in peace and war; a navy that cannot protect the merchant marine and the colonies is worthless. Sea power, in Mahan’s conception, is the capacity of a nation to deploy all three elements in a coherent strategy that serves its national interests.

This definition is retrospective. When Mahan applies it to the French Revolutionary Wars, he is not describing how the French or British governments understood their situation in 1793. He is measuring their actions against a theoretical standard that did not yet exist. The value of the definition lies not in its faithfulness to contemporary thinking but in its analytical power: it allows Mahan to explain why certain policies succeeded and others failed, in terms that the actors themselves could not have articulated.

The Six Elements Affecting Sea Power

Sea power does not arise spontaneously. It is not the product of a single decision or a single generation’s effort, but the result of a combination of conditions that together create the capacity for maritime strength. Mahan distinguishes six such elements, each contributing in its way to the whole.

Geographical Position

The first and most fundamental element is the position that a nation occupies upon the globe. An island or an archipelago is, other things being equal, better situated for sea power than a continental state — not because the islanders are more sea-minded, but because an island has no land frontier to defend and therefore can concentrate its military resources upon the sea. A continental state, by contrast, must maintain a standing army against its neighbours; its naval effort is always competing with the claims of its land frontiers for money, men, and attention.

From this alone, Mahan argues, it follows that Great Britain enjoyed a structural advantage over France in the struggle that opened in 1793. France was the most naturally powerful of the continental states, with long coastlines on three seas and a fine inheritance of harbours and seafaring populations. But France also had frontiers on the Rhine, the Alps, the Pyrenees, and the North Sea coast; it had to maintain the largest army in Europe; and its navy was therefore always the competitor of its army for resources, rather than the primary arm of the state. This was not a matter of choice but of geography: a state with a land frontier must guard it.

Geographical position also determines the strategic relationship between a nation and the great trade routes of the world. A nation placed athwart the principal lines of commerce can exert upon them an influence far out of proportion to its naval strength. The British Isles lie across the approaches to the Baltic, the Channel, and the Atlantic — the three great arteries of European maritime trade. Every vessel trading between northern and southern Europe, between Europe and America, between the Baltic and the Mediterranean, passes within reach of the British coast. This position gave Great Britain, even before she possessed the world’s largest navy, an enormous advantage in the control of commerce.

Physical Conformation

The physical shape of a country, and particularly the character of its coastline, constitutes the second element. A coast that provides good harbours, deep-water anchorages, and sheltered inlets offers facilities for both commerce and naval operations that a coast lacking these cannot match. The British Isles, with their numerous deep estuaries, their natural harbours at Portsmouth, Plymouth, and the Medway, and their sheltered anchorages along the Channel coast, were ideally suited to the maintenance of a great navy. The French coast, though not deficient in good harbours — Brest, Toulon, Rochefort, Cherbourg — was less favourably disposed in two critical respects: the Atlantic ports faced the prevailing westerly winds, making it difficult to sail out against a blockading force, and the Mediterranean and Atlantic fleets were separated by the Spanish peninsula and the Strait of Gibraltar, so that they could not easily combine.

Physical conformation also determines the interior communications of a country. A nation with good internal waterways can move goods and men more cheaply than one dependent upon roads. The British coal trade, carried by sea from Newcastle to London, was the foundation of British industrial strength; the French road system, though the best in Europe, could not match the cheapness of water carriage for heavy goods.

Extent of Territory

The third element is the extent of territory, but not in the sense that mere size confers sea power. The relevant measure is the length of coastline relative to the total land frontier and the productive interior. A country that is very large in area may be so largely continental in character that its maritime resources are stretched thin over a vast extent; its navy must defend a long coastline while its army guards an even longer land frontier. France, with its compact shape and its nearly equal land and sea frontiers, was in this respect better situated than Russia, with its enormous land mass and its limited access to the sea. But the comparison that matters is between France and Great Britain, and here the British advantage was clear: the whole of the British Isles was accessible to sea-borne commerce; there was no interior so remote that it could not be reached by water. France, by contrast, had large inland regions whose only contact with the outside world passed through ports that could be blockaded.

Population

The fourth element is the size of the population, but again with a qualification. The relevant population is not the total number of inhabitants but the number available for maritime employment — for the navy, the merchant marine, and the fishing fleets that together constitute the seafaring community. A large agricultural population is of little use for sea power if it has no maritime tradition and no access to the sea. The French population in 1793 was roughly twice that of Great Britain, but the proportion of Frenchmen who lived by the sea was much smaller; the British seafaring population, including the fishermen of the North Sea and the Channel, the crews of the merchant marine, and the Thames watermen, was probably at least as large as the French, and certainly better trained.

The relationship between the population of the seafaring class and the total population is also important for the mobilisation of naval reserves. In Great Britain, the merchant marine and the fisheries provided a constant stream of trained seamen who could be taken into the navy in time of war; the press-gang, brutal as it was, drew upon a deep reservoir of men who already knew how to handle a ship. In France, the system of maritime conscription — the inscription maritime — had been established by Colbert and maintained by the old monarchy, but it was disrupted by the Revolution and never fully restored. The French navy in the Revolutionary and Napoleonic period was chronically short of trained seamen, and the quality of those it had declined steadily as the war continued.

National Character

The fifth element is the character of the people, and especially their aptitude for commerce and their willingness to engage in maritime enterprise. A nation whose people are naturally inclined toward trade, toward risk-taking, and toward the kind of disciplined enterprise that seafaring requires will develop sea power almost unconsciously; a nation whose people prefer the security of the land and the settled life of the farmer will find sea power an unnatural growth that requires constant artificial support.

The British people, Mahan argues, possessed precisely the character that sea power requires. Commerce was not a mere occupation in Great Britain; it was the ruling passion of the nation, pursued by all classes from the aristocracy to the labourer. The British merchant marine was not a state creation but the spontaneous product of individual enterprise; the Navigation Acts might have shaped its course, but they did not create its impulse. The French, by contrast — while they had produced many of the finest navigators and naval officers of the eighteenth century — were on the whole a continental people whose genius turned toward the land. French commerce was more dependent upon state direction, French maritime enterprise more subject to bureaucratic regulation, and French seafaring less widely diffused through the population than in Great Britain.

National character also affects the quality of the navy in action. The British naval tradition, built up over centuries of maritime war, had produced an officer corps that combined professional skill with an extraordinary capacity for independent action in battle — a capacity that reached its highest expression in Nelson, but that was present in many lesser commanders as well. The French naval tradition, though it had produced brilliant officers in the age of Suffren, was destroyed by the Revolution, and the quality of French naval leadership never recovered.

Character of Government

The sixth element is the character of the government, and especially its capacity to pursue a consistent maritime policy over a long period. Sea power cannot be built in a single parliament or a single reign; it requires sustained investment in ships, dockyards, and training, and a strategic conception that outlasts the changing moods of public opinion and the turnover of ministries.

The British government, for all its faults, provided this consistency. The system of parliamentary government, however turbulent it appeared, produced a remarkable continuity of naval policy across the eighteenth century. The navy estimates, the dockyard programmes, the promotion system, and the strategic habits of the service were maintained through changes of ministry and fluctuations of public favour. In France, by contrast, the Revolution destroyed the continuity of naval administration along with the officer corps. The Committee of Public Safety, the Directory, the Consulate, and the Empire each reorganized the ministry of marine, each changed the system of promotion and training, and each began again from the beginning. The French navy never had the time — or the stable political environment — to develop the institutional memory and professional tradition that British sea power took for granted.

The Twofold Aspect of Sea Power

Mahan distinguishes two aspects of sea power in war, each requiring separate consideration. The first is the effect of sea power considered as a thing in itself — as a faculty that a nation possesses, a natural force that operates independently of the wisdom or folly of those who direct it. The second is the use made of sea power by statesmen and commanders — the strategy of the war, the direction given to naval operations, the decisions that determine whether the faculty is employed wisely or squandered.

The first aspect is the more fundamental. A nation that possesses sea power — that has the ships, the seamen, the commercial base, and the geographical position to control the sea — will produce certain effects simply by existing. The enemy’s commerce will wither, not because anyone in particular has decided to attack it, but because the ships that carry it cannot go where they need to go. The enemy’s colonies will be isolated and captured, not because a strategic decision has been made to take them, but because the sea power that protects them has disappeared. The enemy’s finances will be strained, not because of any particular financial operation, but because the revenues that flow from overseas trade have been cut off. These are the unconscious workings of the faculty, and they operate whether the government that possesses it is wise or foolish, active or passive.

The second aspect — the intelligent use of sea power — is what distinguishes a successful war policy from an unsuccessful one. A nation may possess overwhelming sea power and yet fail to use it effectively, as France failed under Napoleon to use what remained of its navy, or as Spain failed for a century and more. The question of how sea power should be directed is the question of naval strategy: where to concentrate force, when to seek battle, when to blockade, when to convoy, when to attack colonies, when to support allies.

The Seven Functions of Sea Power in the Revolutionary Wars

In the concluding chapter of his two-volume work, Mahan synthesises his argument by identifying seven distinct functions that British sea power discharged in the war against Revolutionary and Napoleonic France. These functions are not presented as a description of British strategic planning — Mahan is clear that the British ministry often acted without a coherent overall conception — but as an analytical framework for understanding how sea power actually contributed to the defeat of France.

Strategic Mobility

The first function of sea power was the capacity to shift military force from one theatre to another faster than any continental power could march its armies. The British army was small by continental standards, but it could be landed anywhere the navy could reach: the West Indies, the Mediterranean, Portugal, Holland, the Peninsula. This mobility gave Britain a flexibility that the French, bound to the roads and rivers of the Continent, could not match. A British expeditionary force could appear off the coast of Holland one season and in the Tagus the next, forcing the French to disperse their forces to guard against threats that might materialise anywhere along thousands of miles of coastline.

Economic Pressure on France

The second function was the economic strangulation of the enemy. British control of the sea cut France off from its colonial revenues, destroyed its overseas trade, and forced the French treasury to rely on continental exactions and plunder — a method that worked in the short term but exhausted the territories subjected to it. By 1799 the French Directory was forced to admit that not a single merchant vessel remained on the sea carrying the French flag. French exports to Asia, Africa, and America together amounted in the year 1800 to less than $56,000. The French merchant marine was annihilated, not by any particular campaign or battle, but by the simple fact that a French ship could not sail without encountering a British cruiser.

Sustaining the Coalitions

The third function was the financial and material support of continental allies. The British government, its own commerce flourishing under the protection of the navy, was able to raise enormous sums by taxation and borrowing. These sums were distributed as subsidies to Austria, Russia, Prussia, and the lesser European states, enabling them to maintain armies in the field against France. Without British gold, Mahan argues, no continental coalition could have sustained a war against the Revolution or the Empire for more than a single campaign. The navy thus functioned not only as a fighting force but as the guarantor of the financial system that made the coalitions possible: because British commerce was safe, British credit was good; because British credit was good, British subsidies could flow; because British subsidies flowed, the continental powers could fight.

The Blockade of French Naval Ports

The fourth function was the continuous watching of the French naval arsenals. The close blockade of Brest, Toulon, Rochefort, and later Cadiz was the most persistent and grinding operation of the war. It prevented the French fleet from concentrating, exercising, or intervening decisively in any theatre. The blockades were not passive but aggressive: they forced the French to sea at disadvantage, or kept them in port where they could not influence events. The strain on the blockading squadrons was immense — winter gales, lee shores, endless patrolling — but the effect on the French navy was far worse: its ships rotted at the quays, its crews never trained at sea, its officers never learned to handle a squadron under sail in all weathers. By the time the French fleet did venture out, as at the First of June (1794) or Trafalgar (1805), its seamanship and gunnery were so far inferior to the British that the outcome was all but foreordained.

Direct Naval Action

The fifth function was the destruction of the enemy’s organised naval forces in fleet actions. The three great battles of the war — the Nile (1798), Copenhagen (1801), and Trafalgar (1805) — each destroyed an enemy fleet and each had immediate strategic consequences far out of proportion to the numbers engaged. The Nile trapped Bonaparte’s army in Egypt and made possible the formation of the Second Coalition. Copenhagen broke the Armed Neutrality of 1800, restoring British access to the Baltic in a single morning. Trafalgar destroyed the Franco-Spanish combined fleet and made the invasion of England impossible forever.

But Mahan is careful not to overstate the importance of fleet actions. The blockades, he notes, were as destructive in their cumulative effect as any single battle. The French navy was destroyed not primarily by Trafalgar — though that was the culminating blow — but by the cumulative pressure of blockade, demoralisation, and the inability to maintain its personnel in a state of readiness. By 1805 the French navy was a shadow of what it had been in 1793, and after Trafalgar it never again attempted to contest the control of the sea in a line-of-battle engagement.

Protection of British Commerce

The sixth function was the preservation of the trade that underlay the entire British war effort. The convoy system, enforced by the Convoy Act of 1798, gathered merchant vessels in specified ports and sailed them under the protection of warships. Flotillas of two to three hundred ships were not unusual; five hundred or even a thousand were at times seen together in the approaches to the Channel or the Baltic. Fast frigates and sloops-of-war patrolled the trade routes, and a network of smaller vessels watched the approaches to enemy ports. The system was not perfect — approximately eleven thousand British vessels were captured over the twenty-one years of war, an annual average of about 524 ships — but the losses were absorbable. The British merchant marine, with its enormous tonnage and global reach, weathered a level of attrition that would have shattered any smaller trading nation. And because British commerce survived, British credit survived, and with it the capacity to finance the coalitions.

The Peninsular War as Culminating Demonstration

The seventh function, and for Mahan the culminating demonstration of what sea power meant, was the Peninsular War. The British army under Wellington in Portugal and Spain was supplied, reinforced, and sustained entirely by sea. The French could not turn the British flank because they could not control the sea; they could not starve the British army because it was fed by sea; they could not cut its communications because its communications were the ocean. The Peninsular War thus became, in Mahan’s reading, the most complete demonstration of sea power’s essential character: not a weapon of conquest but the condition that made endurance possible against a superior land power. The French army in Spain was steadily consumed by a foe it could not reach; the British army grew stronger as it was fed by the sea; and the French Empire was drained of men and resources in a theatre that should have been a sideshow but became, because of sea power, the wound that never healed.

Retrospective Framework versus Contemporary Understanding

It is worth pausing to emphasise the distance between Mahan’s framework and the thinking of the men who actually fought the war. The British government in 1793 did not possess a systematic theory of sea power. Pitt the Younger had reduced naval estimates in the years before the war, expecting a long peace; the mobilisation of 1793 was improvised, not the execution of a coherent maritime strategy. The great blockades of Brest and Toulon were not planned by a central strategist but developed piecemeal as local commanders did what seemed necessary. The convoy system was adopted in response to losses, not as the application of a pre-existing doctrine. Even Nelson, the greatest practitioner of sea power the age produced, thought in tactical rather than strategic terms; his genius lay in the handling of a fleet in action, not in the theoretical elaboration of maritime supremacy.

Mahan’s achievement, in his own view, was not to discover something that the actors had known but to make explicit what had been implicit — to extract from the events of 1793–1815 a pattern that explained why things had happened as they did, and to present that pattern as a general theory applicable to other conflicts and other nations. The six elements, the twofold aspect, and the seven functions are all analytical devices, retrospective constructs that Mahan imposes on the historical material to reveal its underlying logic. They are not descriptions of how the war was planned or understood by its participants; they are explanations of why it turned out as it did.

The Limits of the Thesis

Mahan himself acknowledged the limits of his argument. Sea power could not win battles on land; it could not prevent the French army from conquering most of Europe; it could not, by itself, bring Napoleon to his knees. That required the armies of Russia, Prussia, Austria, and Sweden, and the leadership of statesmen who could hold the coalition together against the centrifugal forces of national interest and mutual suspicion. What sea power could do — and what Mahan argued it did — was to make possible the conditions under which the continental powers could eventually combine to defeat Napoleon. It provided the financial sinews of war; it protected the base from which the Peninsular campaign was launched; it kept open the Baltic and the Mediterranean to British trade and allied supplies; it prevented Napoleon from concentrating his whole force against any single enemy; and, above all, it gave Britain the capacity to endure. In the long grinding struggle of attrition between two forms of power, the form that could draw upon the resources of the whole world — the form whose commerce was protected, whose credit was sound, whose people were safe from invasion — was the form that in the end prevailed.

Conclusion

The French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars constitute, in Mahan’s analysis, the most complete demonstration ever given of the power of maritime supremacy. They demonstrated that a nation which controls the sea can, if it has the patience and the political wisdom to use its advantage correctly, defeat a power that dominates the land. They demonstrated that sea power is not merely a military force but an economic and political instrument of the first order — that it can, by its pressure on commerce, colonies, and credit, impose upon the enemy a constant drain of strength that no victory on land can permanently repair. And they demonstrated that the elements from which sea power is built — geographical position, national character, governmental policy, commercial enterprise, and professional naval tradition — are not the products of any single moment of decision but the slow accumulation of centuries.

This was Mahan’s thesis. It was not the thesis of the age he studied, and he never pretended otherwise. The value of his framework lies precisely in its retrospectivity: looking back over twenty-three years of war from the vantage of the 1890s, Mahan could see patterns that the participants could not, and could articulate a theory of sea power that would shape naval thinking for generations to come.
