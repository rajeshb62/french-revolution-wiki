The Armed Neutrality of 1800

The Rupture with Russia

The defection of Russia from the Second Coalition in the autumn of 1799 was the first crack in the alliance that had seemed, for a moment, capable of crushing the French Republic. By the end of that year the Czar Paul I, whose armies had fought alongside the Austrians under Suwarrow in Italy and Switzerland, had become bitterly disillusioned with his allies. He blamed Austria for the reverses suffered by his troops — particularly the disaster that befell Korsakoff at Zurich while Suwarrow struggled through the Alpine passes — and he saw in the policies of Vienna and London less a zeal for the restoration of legitimate thrones than a cold calculation of territorial advantage. The unbalanced temper of the Czar, at once chivalrous and reckless, turned rapidly from coolness to active hostility.

Into this widening breach Bonaparte inserted himself with characteristic adroitness. From the moment he became First Consul he had fixed his gaze upon Russia as the key to a continental combination against Great Britain. As early as June 1800, while marching toward Marengo, he wrote to Talleyrand that if Paul turned his attention toward the decaying Ottoman Empire, French and Russian interests would become common. By July he had dispatched a letter to the Czar offering to surrender Malta — which he knew he could not hold — into Russian hands, appealing directly to Paul’s vanity as the self-styled protector of the Knights of St. John. The offer was carefully worded to distinguish the Czar from the other enemies of the Republic, who fought, Bonaparte wrote, from “a vile love of gain.”

Paul was captivated. The prospect of becoming Grand Master of the Knights of Malta, of seeing his flag fly over that fortress-island, was precisely the kind of grand gesture that appealed to his romantic and autocratic nature. He designated a general to proceed to Paris, take command of French prisoners of war, and with them repair to Malta.

The Neutral Rights Question

While Bonaparte worked upon the Czar, a more immediate quarrel was brewing between Great Britain and the Baltic powers over the ancient and vexed question of neutral rights — the same question that had produced the original Armed Neutrality of 1780 under Catherine the Great. Denmark in 1799 had adopted the policy of convoying her merchant vessels by ships of war, and claimed that a statement from the senior naval officer — that the cargoes contained nothing forbidden by the law of nations — exempted the convoy from the belligerent right of search. British statesmen denied that a conceded belligerent right could be nullified by any regulation a neutral might choose to adopt. The dispute was sharpened by a fundamental divergence over the definition of contraband: Great Britain classed naval stores — timber, hemp, iron, tar — as contraband of war, since they were essential to the construction and maintenance of warships; the Baltic states, whose economies depended upon the export of these very articles, insisted they were innocent goods in peaceful trade.

Two hostile encounters followed. In December 1799 a Danish frigate resisted search, and in July 1800 a second clash left several dead on both sides, the Danish frigate being carried into the Downs as a prize. The British government, seeing the storm gathering, acted swiftly. An ambassador was sent to Copenhagen supported by nine sail-of-the-line and bomb vessels, and on August 29 a convention was signed: Denmark agreed to suspend her convoys pending future discussion, and the immediate crisis passed.

But the damage had been done. Paul I seized upon the affair as a pretext for sequestrating all British property in Russia, to be held as a guarantee against the future action of Great Britain. A second order of September 10 announced that “several political circumstances induced the emperor to think that a rupture of friendship with England may ensue,” and directed a concentration of Russian troops. The sequestration was briefly lifted on September 22, but the fall of Malta — which had surrendered to the British on the 5th of that month — brought matters to a head. The Czar, who had so eagerly accepted Bonaparte’s offer, learned in early November that the island was now in British hands and would not be yielded to him. In a fury he again decreed the sequestration of British property. Some three hundred British merchant ships were seized, their crews marched into the interior, and seals placed on all warehouses containing British goods. The Czar declared the embargo would not be removed until his rights to Malta, as Grand Master of the Order, were acknowledged.

The Northern League

The breach between Russia and Great Britain had now opened wide enough for Bonaparte to drive through. Prussia, which since 1795 had maintained a rigorous neutrality, felt encouraged to stir. The commerce of the Continent had poured through the North German states — by the Ems, the Weser, and the Elbe, through Hamburg and Bremen — and the tonnage clearing from Great Britain alone to North Germany had risen from 120,000 in 1792 to 389,000 in 1800. Prussia took the lion’s share of this traffic and was loath to see it disturbed. Yet her ambition to play a leading part in Europe, her jealousy of Austria, and the hope of one day annexing Hanover — the German possession of the British king — pulled her in the opposite direction.

The incident that decided her course occurred in October 1800. A British cruiser seized a Prussian merchantman attempting to enter the Texel with a cargo of naval stores. The captor, driven by weather, took his prize into Cuxhaven, a port at the mouth of the Elbe belonging to Hamburg. Prussia demanded its release; the Hamburg senate hesitated; Prussia ordered two thousand troops to take possession of the port. The senate bought the prize and delivered it to Prussia, and the British government — conciliatory in the face of the gathering storm — directed its restoration. It was, as Fox later taunted the ministry, a concession of principle dictated by the fact that Prussia, while capable of doing much harm to Great Britain, could not be reached by the British navy. The concession availed nothing. The Prussian minister informed the British ambassador that the occupation of Cuxhaven, having been ordered, must proceed.

On December 16, 1800, a treaty renewing the Armed Neutrality of 1780 was signed at St. Petersburg by Russia and Sweden. Denmark and Prussia immediately adhered. Its leading affirmations struck at the heart of British maritime practice: neutral ships were free to carry on the coasting and colonial trade of belligerents; enemy’s goods under the neutral flag were not subject to seizure; blockades, to be respected, must be supported by a force sufficient to make entry actually hazardous; naval stores were excluded from the definition of contraband; and vessels under convoy of a ship of war were not liable to belligerent search. Each of these propositions contested a claim upon which British sea power rested. The consenting states bound themselves to maintain their positions by force if necessary.

The Baltic was thus closed. The strategic danger to Great Britain was acute. From the Baltic states came the naval stores without which the British navy could not operate: the great masts of Riga, the hemp of Russia, the iron of Sweden, the tar and pitch that preserved the hulls of the fleet. Without these, the dockyards would fall silent and the ships would rot at their moorings. Bonaparte’s conception was realized: there was not only a naval combination against Great Britain but also an exclusion of her trade from one of its chief markets.

The British Response

Great Britain was left alone, and for the moment against all Europe. The ministry met the emergency with vigor. An armed negotiation with the Baltic powers was determined upon, and a fleet of eighteen sail-of-the-line with thirty-five smaller vessels was assembled at Yarmouth on the east coast of England. Rapidity of movement was essential: the Baltic ice was breaking up, and the British fleet had to strike before the hostile navies could concentrate. The chief command was given to Sir Hyde Parker, a gallant and respectable officer of the old school, with Nelson as his second-in-command. The choice of Parker over Nelson was a mistake — one that would not have been made by Lord St. Vincent, who succeeded Spencer as First Lord a few weeks later. Nelson was still among the junior flag-officers, and his erratic conduct in the Mediterranean under the influence of Lady Hamilton had perhaps excited doubts about his fitness for a command requiring delicacy of discretion as well as vigor. But withholding the chief command from him unquestionably weakened the expedition from the start.

The fleet sailed from Yarmouth on March 12, 1801. By the 19th, despite a heavy gale that scattered the ships, nearly all were collected off the Skaw, the northern point of Jutland. The wind was fair for Copenhagen, and Nelson, had he been in command, would have advanced at once with the ambassador on board. “While the negotiation is going on,” he said, “the Dane should see our flag waving every moment he lifted his head.” As it was, the envoy went forward with a frigate alone while the fleet waited.

On the 21st the fleet was off Elsinore, where the envoy rejoined: Denmark had rejected the British terms. This amounted to an acceptance of hostilities, and it only remained to act at once, for the wind was favorable — an advantage that at any moment might be lost. Nelson addressed a letter to Parker summing up the situation with luminous clarity. “Not a moment should be lost in attacking,” he wrote. “We shall never be so good a match for them as at this moment.” He pointed out that the fleet ought to have been off Copenhagen, not at Elsinore, when the negotiation failed — then the Danish fleet could have been destroyed and the capital made so hot that Denmark would listen to reason. “Almost the safety, certainly the honor, of England, is more entrusted to you than ever yet fell to the lot of any British officer,” he concluded.

The Plan of Attack

Copenhagen lies on the east coast of the island of Zealand, fronting Sweden across the Sound. Before the city, shallow flats extend for half a mile from the shore, and in the Sound itself, little more than a mile out, lies a long shoal called the Middle Ground. Between these two bodies of shallow water runs the King’s Channel, through which heavy ships could sail. At the northern end of this channel, a deep pocket stretches toward the harbor, defended by powerful works rising on piles out of the shoal water — the formidable Three-Crown Batteries. The Danes had moored a line of armed hulks and floating batteries along the inner edge of the King’s Channel, covering the front of the city.

The natural point of attack appeared to be from the north, but Nelson pointed out that not only was the head of the line exceedingly strong, but the wind that was fair to attack would be foul to return. A disabled ship would have no escape but by running the gauntlet of the entire Danish line, separated from her fleet. He dismissed this approach as “taking the bull by the horns.” More importantly, he argued that so long as the British fleet remained in the Sound without entering the Baltic, the way was left open for the Swedes and Russians to join the Danes. He therefore advised that a sufficiently strong force should attack the southern end of the Danish line, interposing between Copenhagen and the Baltic.

Nelson’s letter of March 24, 1801, is a masterpiece of military reasoning. He concluded with a suggestion worthy of Napoleon himself: “Supposing us through the Belt with the wind first westerly, would it not be possible to go with the fleet, or detach ten ships of three and two decks, with one bomb and two fire-ships, to Revel, to destroy the Russian squadron at that place? I do not see the great risk of such a detachment, and with the remainder to attempt the business at Copenhagen. The measure may be thought bold, but I am of opinion the boldest are the safest; and our country demands a most vigorous exertion of her force, directed with judgment.”

He saw the Northern League as a tree, with Paul the trunk and Sweden and Denmark the branches. “If I can get at the trunk and hew it down, the branches fall of course; but I may lop the branches and yet not be able to fell the tree, and my power must be weaker when its greatest strength is required.” At Revel lay twelve Russian ships-of-the-line — fully half their Baltic fleet. Their destruction would have paralyzed the naval power of the empire. But Parker was determined not to leave Denmark hostile in his rear — a reason whose technical accuracy under the circumstances was nothing short of pedantic. He adopted Nelson’s plan for the attack on Copenhagen but not the broader strategic stroke.

The Battle of Copenhagen

The nights of March 30 and 31 were employed sounding the King’s Channel. On April 1 the fleet moved up to the north end of the Middle Ground, about four miles from the city. That afternoon Nelson’s division — to which Parker had assigned twelve ships-of-the-line, two more than asked — got under way, passed through the outer channel, and anchored off the southeast end of the shoal, two miles from the head of the Danish line. The enterprise was perilous, not so much from the force to be engaged as from the extreme difficulties of navigation. The pilots were mostly mates of merchantmen trading with the Baltic, and their experience in vessels of three or four hundred tons did not fit them for the charge of heavy battleships. They betrayed throughout great indecision, and their imperfect knowledge contributed to the principal mishaps of the day.

On the morning of April 2 the wind came fair from the south-southeast. At half-past nine the signal was made to weigh. The ships were soon under sail, but the difficulties of pilotage were immediately apparent. The Agamemnon, of sixty-four guns, was unable to weather the point of the Middle Ground and had to anchor out of range, taking no share in the battle. The Bellona and Russell, seventy-fours, the fourth and fifth in the order, kept too far to the eastward and ran ashore on the farther side of the channel — upon the Middle Ground itself. They were not entirely out of action, but were beyond the range of efficient gunnery under the conditions of that day. Nelson’s flagship passed clear, as did the rest of the heavy ships, but the loss of these three vessels from the line exposed the northern part of the British order to a weight of fire disproportioned to its strength. A body of frigates very gallantly undertook to fill the gap.

The battle was at its height by half-past eleven. There was no more maneuvering — only the simple question of efficient gunnery and endurance. Around two in the afternoon, a great part of the Danish line had ceased to fire, and the flagship Dannebrog was in flames. During the action, Danish crews were frequently reinforced from the shore, and in several cases, new arrivals reaching ships that had already struck renewed the fight, either through ignorance or indifference. The land batteries fired on British boats trying to take possession.

Nelson seized on this circumstance to bring the affair to a conclusion. He wrote a letter addressed “To the brothers of Englishmen, the Danes,” and sent it under a flag of truce to the Crown Prince: “Lord Nelson has directions to spare Denmark when no longer resisting; but if the firing is continued on the part of Denmark, Lord Nelson will be obliged to set on fire all the floating batteries he has taken, without having the power of saving the brave Danes who have defended them.” The letter was sent ashore by a British officer who spoke Danish. The engagement continued until about three o’clock, when the whole line of floating defenses southeast of the Crown Batteries had either struck or been destroyed.

It was at some point during this action that the famous episode of the blind eye occurred. Parker, lying four miles off with the reserve division, saw the smoke and confusion of the battle and concluded that Nelson was being overwhelmed. He hoisted signal No. 39 — the signal of recall. Nelson, aboard the Elephant, was informed. He raised his telescope to his blind eye and, turning to his flag-captain, Thomas Foley, said: “I really do not see the signal.” The signal remained flying; Nelson remained in action. It was an act of deliberate disobedience that has become the emblem of his genius — the recognition that at a certain point in a battle, the commander on the spot, who sees the situation with his own eyes, must overrule the distant judgment of the commander-in-chief.

The Crown Prince demanded the precise purport of Nelson’s first message. Nelson took a high hand. He had destroyed the part of the enemy’s line he had attacked, but it was now important to withdraw his crippled ships — and with the existing wind, that could only be done by passing the Crown Batteries, which had been but little injured. He wrote a second letter, politicly ignoring this danger: “Lord Nelson, with humble duty to His Royal Highness, begs leave to say that he will ever esteem it the greatest victory he ever gained, if this flag of truce may be the happy forerunner of a lasting and happy union between my most gracious Sovereign and His Majesty the King of Denmark.” Having written the letter, he referred the bearer for definite action to Sir Hyde Parker, some four miles off — foreseeing that the long pull there and back would give time for the leading ships, much crippled, to clear the shoals, though their course lay close under the Crown Batteries. Thus the exposed part of the British fleet was successfully withdrawn. Three out of five ships-of-the-line that had taken the heaviest punishment grounded on the shoals during the retirement, and only the respite gained by the flag of truce saved them from destruction by the shore batteries.

The Armistice and the Death of Paul

The result of the battle was to uncover the front of Copenhagen and lay it, with its dockyards and arsenals, open to bombardment. The bomb vessels could now be placed safely in the King’s Channel. Denmark had to decide whether fear of her powerful allies and zeal for neutral claims should lead her to undergo further punishment, or whether the suffering already endured was excuse enough for abandoning the coalition.

Nelson conducted the negotiations himself. He insisted upon an armistice of fourteen weeks, after which hostilities could be resumed on fourteen days’ notice. This would assure Parker complete immunity for his communications and leave Nelson free to act against the Russian fleet. Fear of Russia long deterred the Danes from this concession, which Nelson frankly told them he must have, so as to be at liberty to act against the Russian fleet and return to them; and he made it the indispensable requisite to sparing the city. During the discussions, however, the Crown Prince received news of the Czar’s death. Paul I had been murdered by a body of conspirators on the night of March 23, 1801. The Danish government concealed the tidings, but the departure of the soul of the confederacy relieved their worst fears and encouraged them to yield to Nelson’s demands.

The armistice was signed. Denmark’s part in the Armed Neutrality was suspended.

The Dissolution

Among the first measures of the new Czar, Alexander I, was the release of the British seamen imprisoned by his father, an order dated April 7. On the 12th the British ships entered the Baltic — much to the surprise of the Northern Powers, who had thought their heavy draught would prevent it. The three-deckers had to remove their guns to pass some shoal ground ten miles above Copenhagen. Parker anchored in Kioge Bay, off the coast of Zealand, there awaiting further instructions; the Russian minister at Copenhagen having informed him that the new Czar would not go to war. Nelson entirely disapproved of this inactivity. Russia might yield, but she would be more likely to do so if the British fleet lay off Revel.

The British ministry, however, had at last taken the measure of Parker. On April 17, four days after news of the victory reached London, orders were issued modifying his instructions: if Alexander removed the embargo and released the seamen, all hostile movements were to be suspended; if not, the Revel division must be prevented from joining that at Cronstadt. Four days later, orders were issued relieving Parker and leaving Nelson in command. Taken only a week after a victory, this could scarcely be construed as anything but an implied censure. “They are not Sir Hyde Parker’s real friends who wish for an inquiry,” Nelson wrote. “His friends in the fleet wish everything of this fleet to be forgot, for we all respect and love Sir Hyde; but the dearer his friends, the more uneasy they have been at his idleness, for that is the truth — no criminality.”

Nelson’s first signal upon receiving command was to hoist the boats aboard and prepare to weigh. On May 7 the fleet left Kioge Bay, and on the 12th appeared off Revel. But the Russian division had sailed three days before and was now safe under the guns of Cronstadt. From Revel, Nelson dispatched very complimentary letters to the Russian minister of foreign affairs, but received in reply the message that “the only proof of the loyalty of his intentions that the czar could accept was the prompt withdrawal of his fleet; and that until then no negotiation could proceed.” “I do not believe he would have written such a letter,” said Nelson, “if the Russian fleet had been in Revel.” But the bird was flown, and with a civil explanation he withdrew from the port. He remained in the Baltic, awaiting the issue of negotiations. Russia meant peace: on May 17 the Czar ordered the release of the embargoed British ships. On June 4 Great Britain released the Danes and Swedes detained in her ports. Russia and Prussia had already agreed, on April 27, that hostile measures against England should cease, Hamburg and Hanover be evacuated, and the free navigation of the rivers restored.

On June 17, 1801, a convention was signed at St. Petersburg between Russia and Great Britain, settling the points in dispute. The question of Malta was tacitly dropped. On neutral claims, Russia conceded that the neutral flag should not cover enemy’s goods. While she obtained the formal admission that articles of hostile origin which had become bona fide neutral property were exempt from seizure, she yielded the critically important exception of colonial produce: this, no matter who the owner, could not be carried by a neutral direct from the colony to the mother country of a nation at war. Great Britain conceded the right of neutrals to carry on the coasting trade of a belligerent and agreed that naval stores should not be classed as contraband of war. As for the right of search under convoy, Russia yielded the principle, and Great Britain accepted methods that would make the process less offensive.

Sweden acceded to these terms on October 23, 1801, Denmark on March 30, 1802. The Baltic states renewed among themselves the principles they had relinquished in their convention with Great Britain — that neutral flags should cover enemy’s property and that convoy should exempt from search — but these were now merely declarations of principle, without force of arms to support them. The Armed Neutrality of 1800 was dead.

The Meaning of the League

Thus had fallen resultless, as far as the objects of the First Consul were concerned, the vast combination against Great Britain which Bonaparte had fostered in the North. The dissolution of the Armed Neutrality demonstrated a fundamental truth about sea power. The Northern League had seemed formidable on paper: the combined fleets of Russia, Denmark, Sweden, and Prussia outnumbered the British squadron sent to confront them. But the coalition was held together by no common interest of sufficient weight. The commerce of the Baltic states depended upon the British market; their carrying trade had flourished during the war precisely because of the protection of British naval supremacy. The interests of the Northern powers lay in yielding the points in dispute, while those of Great Britain lay in not yielding. The coalition required only a decisive demonstration of British naval force — or the death of Paul I — to dissolve.

The Czar was the only man wholly embarked in the quarrel, the only one deaf to the call of clear interest. Bonaparte, who had fostered the combination with such skill, underestimated the vitality of a state whose power rested not merely on ships but on a far-reaching commercial system. He had shown wonderful skill at this critical period in combining against his principal enemy an opposition for which Prussia afforded the body and the hot temper of the Czar the animating soul. But he never fully understood the maritime problems with which he had to deal. He underestimated the vitality of a state upon which centred a far-reaching commercial system, and in valuing naval power he did not appreciate that a mere mass of ships had not the weight he himself was able to impart to a mass of men.

The Baltic campaign was one of Nelson’s finest achievements — not for the battle alone, but for his clear reading of the political and military situation. He had lifted on his shoulders the dead weight of his superior, had seen that the trunk of the tree was Paul and Russia, and had never for one moment lost sight of the key to the whole. Had Nelson been given the command from the start, the twelve Russian ships at Revel would have been destroyed, the League would have been shattered at its source, and the whole course of the naval war might have been altered. As it was, the Armed Neutrality of 1800 remains a classic demonstration of the difficulty of combining continental and maritime powers against a state that commands the sea. British sea power had dispersed the Northern coalition, commanded the friendship of the great continental states, retained control of the Mediterranean, reduced Egypt to submission, and forced even the invincible Bonaparte to wish for a speedy cessation of hostilities.
