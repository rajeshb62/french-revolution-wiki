The Girondins

The Girondins — also called Brissotins by their enemies — were the leading republican faction in the Legislative Assembly (1791–1792) and the National Convention (1792–1793). They are known to history by the name of the department of the Gironde, from which several of their principal speakers were elected, though many of their leaders came from other parts of France: Brissot from Chartres, Roland from Lyon, Buzot from Évreux, Louvet from Orléans, Pétion from Chartres, Barbaroux from Marseilles. The name was given to them by their enemies in the Jacobin clubs — the deputies of the Mountain — and accepted by them reluctantly, as a designation that misrepresented the scope of their support.

The Girondins left an unusually rich documentary record: memoirs written in hiding or in prison after their proscription, speeches and published writings, and the records of the Convention itself. These are not the work of detached observers but the documents of a defeated party, written by men who expected to die, and they preserve the Girondin understanding of the French Revolution as it was happening.

The Circle and Its Formation

The Girondins never constituted a formal party with a programme, a whip, or a coordinated discipline. They were a network of deputies who met in one another’s houses — principally at the apartment of Jean-Marie Roland and his wife Madame Roland on the rue Guénégaud in Paris, and at the house of the deputy Pierre Vergniaud in Versailles. The connections that became the faction formed gradually through 1791 in the Jacobin Club of Paris, where Brissot, Buzot, Pétion, and others were active members before the schism.

The sociological core of the group was provincial, professional, and intellectual. Brissot was a journalist. Roland was a manufacturing inspector and administrator. Buzot was a lawyer from Évreux. Vergniaud was a lawyer from Bordeaux. Louvet was a novelist turned journalist. Guadet and Gensonné were lawyers from the Bordelais. Barbaroux was a lawyer from Marseilles. Pétion had been the mayor of Paris. They were not nobles; they were men of the Third Estate, risen through talent and ambition in the decade before the Revolution.

The War Policy

The Girondins’ signature policy was the war against Austria. From the autumn of 1791 through the spring of 1792, Brissot’s speeches in the Legislative Assembly argued that the Revolution could not be secure while Austria and Prussia were arming and threatening the frontiers, that war would rally the nation to the Revolution, and that the king’s court would be forced to show its hand. The war was declared on 20 April 1792 — a war that went badly for France at first, that produced the invasion crisis of the summer, and that ultimately brought down the monarchy on 10 August 1792.

Brissot’s reasoning on the war was not purely ideological. He argued that Austria’s position in the Netherlands (Belgium) was the strategic key to French security: as long as Austria held the Scheldt, France was vulnerable to invasion from the north. The war policy also served a domestic purpose: it forced the issue of the king’s loyalty, dividing the court from the Revolution.

The Girondins and the Convention

By the time the National Convention met in September 1792, the Girondins were the dominant faction. They held the ministry. They had led the call for the republic. Their orators dominated the Convention debates. The Mountain — the Jacobin deputies on the upper benches who had been allied with the Commune of Paris and with the insurrection of 10 August — was numerically weaker but organised, ruthless, and allied with the Paris sections.

The conflict between the Girondins and the Mountain was not about the form of government (both were republican) or about the war (both supported it) or about the trial of the king (most Girondins voted for death). It was about the revolutionary process: whether the Revolution could be consolidated through constitutional order or whether it required permanent popular insurrection.

The Conflict with the Mountain

The most systematic Girondin analysis of the struggle came from Buzot. The true causes of Girondin proscription, he insisted, were not the public charges — royalism, federalism, counter-revolution, corruption by foreign gold — but the Girondin insistence on order, legality, and constitutional procedure at a moment when the Mountain’s power depended on maintaining popular agitation. Since the monarchy had been annihilated, Buzot argued, the insurrectional movement had to cease: where there is no tyranny to overthrow, there should be no force in insurrection; where one is only building, one needs order and reason.

The Mountain, according to the Girondins, used the Jacobin Club as a parallel power structure. The affiliated clubs in the departments let the Mountain mobilise public opinion against the Convention itself. The Commune of Paris — controlled by the radical sections — provided armed street pressure at key moments. The press — Marat’s L’Ami du Peuple, Hébert’s Père Duchesne — destroyed individual reputations systematically.

The Girondins’ counter-weapons were speeches, decrees, and constitutional procedure. They commissioned the report on the March 10 conspiracy; they voted for the Commission of Twelve to investigate the Commune’s activities; they attempted to discipline Marat through the Revolutionary Tribunal. Each attempt was turned back by the Mountain.

The Character Studies

The Girondins identified four figures as the instruments of Montagnard domination:

Marat — the visible extremist, whose denunciations served as the instrument for destroying individual reputations. Madame Roland’s portrait, drawn from the testimony of a woman imprisoned with her who had visited Marat’s apartment, does not describe a disorganised lunatic but a deliberate operator who extracted personal favours through fear. His acquittal by the Revolutionary Tribunal, Buzot argued, made the Convention permanently subject to factional terror.

Robespierre — the tactician, who rose through the Jacobin Club while Marat was the visible extremist, and moved toward dictatorship once Marat’s assassination removed his cover. Among the Girondins, Louvet was the most explicit enemy of Robespierre, having delivered the formal accusation before the Convention in October 1792 — Je t’accuse, Robespierre! — in which he charged him with conspiring against the republic through his dominance of the Jacobin Club.

Danton — the most complex and contested figure in Girondin memoirs. Madame Roland wrote the most severe judgment: she had warned against placing Danton in the ministry. She describes his repulsive face, his violence of brutal passions half-veiled by an air of joviality, his affectation of frankness. His conduct during the September Massacres is the subject of her most sustained accusation: he was at the Commune’s surveillance committee from which arrest orders were flowing, and at the council table that night when informed of efforts to protect prisoners, he cried out “I don’t give a damn about the prisoners!”

Pache — the Tartuffe of politics, the object of the most sustained anatomisation of betrayal among the memoirs. Roland had recommended him as his own successor at the Interior. As War Minister, Pache destroyed the army’s capacity by purging competent officers. The false simplicity — arriving at seven in the morning with a piece of bread, refusing salary — was a mask for a systematic building of factional power within the ministry.

The Hour of Collapse: 31 May – 2 June 1793

The coup against the Girondins was a three-day operation: 31 May, 1 June, 2 June. The sections of Paris surrounded the Convention each day, the tocsin was rung, the Commune petitioned for the arrest of twenty-two (later thirty-one) deputies. On 2 June the Convention was surrounded by armed National Guards — eighty thousand men by Girondin estimates — and decreed the arrest of its own leading members.

The Girondin reaction was not uniform. Some — like Brissot — were arrested in Paris. Some — like Buzot, Barbaroux, Louvet — escaped to the provinces and attempted to organise resistance. Some — like Vergniaud, Guadet, Gensonné — stayed in Paris and went to prison. Some — like Roland — fled. The central weakness of the Girondins as a political force was evident in the response: they had no organisation for resistance, no armed force of their own, no plan for what to do when the Convention was no longer a free assembly. They were men who believed in the power of speeches, laws, and constitutional procedure against armed insurrection.

After the Proscription

The proscribed Girondins who escaped Paris gathered in Caen, Normandy, under the protection of the departmental administration. There they established an assemblée centrale de résistance à l’oppression and issued a proclamation drafted by Louvet calling on the departments to resist the Convention’s usurpation. A military force was assembled under General Wimpfen but was defeated at Pacy-sur-Eure in July 1793.

Inside the Caen group, tensions ran high. Genuine republicans who wanted to march on Paris sat uneasily alongside men suspected of royalist sympathies or secret intelligences with the enemy. The provincial resistance itself faltered — property-owning republicans unwilling to leave their homes could not sustain the cause. The survivors scattered as the Terror reached each hiding place.

Charlotte Corday came to Caen before Marat’s assassination, ostensibly on family business. She never spoke to any of the fugitive Girondins about her design. If she had consulted them, they later reflected, they would not have directed her toward Marat — whom they knew had at most two days left to live from his disease.

The Trial and Execution

The Girondin deputies who were arrested in Paris were tried by the Revolutionary Tribunal in October 1793, condemned, and guillotined on 31 October 1793 on the Place de la Révolution. The executed were: Brissot, Vergniaud, Gensonné, Ducos, Fonfrède, Lasource, Lehardi, Duprat, Gardien, Duchastel, Mainvielle, Sillery, Valazé (who stabbed himself in the courtroom), Boilleau, Lesterpt-Beauvais, and a number associated with them.

Buzot, Barbaroux, and Pétion escaped the Paris roundup and were discovered dead in a field near Saint-Émilion in June 1794 — shot, or by their own hands. Their bodies were found half-eaten by dogs.

Louvet survived the Terror in hiding, was rehabilitated after Thermidor, and returned to the Convention. He died in 1797 as consul-designate to Palermo. Buzot’s memoirs were found among his papers and published in 1823. Louvet’s were written in a cave in the Jura in April 1794.
