Madame Roland

Marie-Jeanne Roland de la Platière (1754–1793) was the intellectual centre of the Girondin circle. Her husband Jean-Marie Roland served twice as Minister of the Interior; their apartment and salon in the Hôtel Britannique on the rue Guénégaud was the meeting place of the leading Girondins throughout 1791–1793. She was not merely a political hostess: she drafted many of her husband’s ministerial letters, conducted her own correspondence with deputies and writers, and formed sharply independent judgments on every figure she encountered. In the Abbaye and Sainte-Pélagie prisons between August and November 1793, she wrote the memoirs that would become the definitive first-person account of the Girondin experience.

Early Life and Formation

She was born on 17 March 1754, the daughter of Gratien Phlipon, an engraver and goldsmith whose workshop on the Pont-Neuf employed a number of workers. Her father was active and vain about his appearance, fond of jewellery, ambitious in commerce beyond his craft, and ended by ruining himself. Her mother, Marguerite Bimont, was a patient and tactful woman who protected her daughter’s intellectual development without appearing to do so — permitting access to whatever books the girl could reach while maintaining the form of parental supervision.

Her access to books was early and intensive. At nine years old she carried Dacier’s translation of Plutarch’s Lives to church in place of a prayer book, and from that experience, she later wrote, dated the impressions and ideas that made her a republican without her knowing what the word meant. She read Fénelon’s Télémaque, Tasso’s Jerusalem Delivered, Voltaire’s Candide (reprimanded for it by a neighbour, unperturbed by her mother), Fénelon’s Treatise on the Education of Girls, and Locke on child education.

At eleven years and two months (May 1765) she entered the Convent of the Congrégation des dames on the rue Neuve-Saint-Étienne, faubourg Saint-Marcel. She had asked to go herself. The religious life was intense and genuinely felt; the convent was not oppressive but nourishing. She describes individual nuns with the same precision she later applied to political figures. The friendship with a nun called Sainte-Agathe outlasted the convent.

After the convent years her reading continued intensively: Saint Francis de Sales, Saint Augustine, Bossuet’s works of religious controversy — which, though written to defend Catholicism, acquainted her with objections to it and set her on the path of reasoning her belief. She passed successively through Jansenism, Cartesianism, Stoicism, deism, and finally to a scepticism tempered by her discovery of Rousseau. Of this progression she wrote wryly: “What roads, to end up with patriotism, which has put me in irons!”

Her mother’s death when Roland was twenty-one was the decisive emotional event of her young womanhood. An abscess in the head, unrecognised until the final effusion; Roland was away two hours on the afternoon of the day it happened and returned at five-thirty to find her mother struck. The grief was violent and physical: fainting, rigidity, loss of the faculty of attention, seeming derangement. The abbé Legrand, visiting, had the insight that she needed to talk about her mother rather than avoid the subject; he led her through conversations that gradually brought other thoughts alongside. When he judged her ready for a book, he brought her Rousseau’s Héloïse. “Its reading was truly my first distraction. I was twenty-one years old. Rousseau made on me then an impression comparable to what Plutarch had made at eight years old.”

Marriage and the Roland Household

She met Jean-Marie Roland through a correspondence that preceded their meeting. He was a manufacturing inspector in the Lyon generality, contributing to the Encyclopédie méthodique, whose brother came to see her one day bearing notes Roland was sending from his Italian travels. She read his travel journals and found his soul, his austere probity, his rigorous principles, his knowledge and taste appearing undisguised. She was thirty-two; he was forty-six. The marriage produced one daughter, Eudora.

The Salon and the Girondin Circle

The Rolands’ apartment in Paris became the nerve-centre of the Girondin network from 1791. Brissot came to visit them in February 1791 — the first meeting after a long correspondence. She describes his simple manners, his frankness, his natural negligence, in perfect harmony with the austerity of his principles — but found in him a kind of levity of spirit and character that did not suit the gravity of philosophy. “The best of men, good husband, tender father, faithful friend, virtuous citizen… confiding to the point of imprudence, gay, naïve, ingenuous as one is at fifteen, he was made to live with sages and to be the dupe of the wicked.”

Through Brissot’s circle the Rolands became part of the wider Girondin network. The apartment on the rue Guénégaud received Buzot, Vergniaud, Pétion, Barbaroux, Guadet, Gensonné, Louvet, and many others. The meetings were not formal salons in the ancien régime sense but political gatherings in a domestic space, at which Madame Roland was a full participant. It was in this setting that the political positions of the Girondin faction were formed: the war policy against Austria, the defence of constitutional legality against the Commune’s arbitrary power, and the conviction that the Revolution could be consolidated only through order.

The First Ministry (March–June 1792)

When the Girondins formed a cabinet in March 1792 under the constitutional monarchy — Dumouriez at Foreign Affairs, Servan at War, Clavière at Finance — Jean-Marie Roland was named Minister of the Interior. The appointment was of a genuine reformist administrator with deep expertise in French industry and commerce.

The ministry attempted to govern constitutionally under a king who accepted his ministers but did not support them. The most significant act of this period was Roland’s letter to the king in June 1792, which Madame Roland substantially drafted. The letter called on Louis XVI to promulgate the decrees he had vetoed — on the deportation of refractory priests and on the establishment of a camp of federal volunteers near Paris — and warned him that constitutional resistance was dangerous to the monarchy itself. The king’s response was immediate: dismissal of Roland and his Girondin colleagues.

The Second Ministry and Danton (August 1792 – January 1793)

After the insurrection of 10 August 1792, Roland was recalled to the Interior in the reconstituted council. The critical and contested element of this second ministry was the inclusion of Danton at the Ministry of Justice.

She had warned that placing Danton in the government was to inoculate the body politic with men who torment a government when excluded and degrade it when they participate in it. Danton came frequently to her apartment during this period, arriving a little before council time and staying after with Fabre d’Églantine. She watched him closely: his repulsive and atrocious face, the violence of brutal passions half-veiled by an air of joviality, the affectation of frankness and a kind of good nature. Her imagination placed him a dagger in hand, inciting assassins. His biographical arc, as she traced it: a miserable lawyer, burdened with debts, rising through his district section by force of his lungs, a great partisan of the Orléanists, acquiring ease without visible work, becoming substitute prosecutor of the Commune.

She recorded his conduct during the September Massacres without mitigation. While the prisons were being attacked, Danton was at the Commune’s surveillance committee, from which the arrest orders were flowing. He had just embraced Marat there after a feigned twenty-four-hour quarrel. The warrant for Roland’s arrest was issued; Danton took it, showed it to the mayor Pétion, and on Pétion’s instruction did not execute it. Roland records the scene at the council table late that night when a citizen came to Danton recounting his attempts to protect prisoners from the massacres. Danton, irritated, cried out: “I don’t give a damn about the prisoners! let them become whatever they will!”

The Portraits

In twenty-two days in August 1793, she wrote portraits of fifteen individuals she had known.

Buzot — the longest and most personal portrait. Elevated character, proud spirit, boiling courage, sensitive, ardent, melancholic, and lazy — prone to extremes. The gentlest man in the world with his friends and the most formidable adversary of scoundrels. His parliamentary works are praised in detail: his report on the departmental guard, his law against inciters to murder, his motion for the banishment of the Bourbons, his opinion on the trial of the king. Accused of royalism, calumniating Paris, aristocracy, federalism — all for insisting on legality in a revolution that had abandoned it. “Buzot professed the morals of Socrates and preserved the politeness of Scipio,” for which his house was razed and his goods confiscated.

Pétion — a true honest man and a good man, incapable of wounding probity. The serenity of a good conscience, mildness of an easy character, frankness and gaiety distinguished his face. A cold orator and slack in prose style. A prudent mayor and faithful representative, but too trusting and too peaceful to foresee storms. “Made to practice the virtues in a republic, and not to found such a government among a corrupt people who regarded him for a time as their idol, and rejoiced at his proscription as at an enemy’s.”

Marat — known through anecdote rather than direct meeting. A Toulouse woman detained at Sainte-Pélagie described Marat receiving her: boots without stockings, old leather breeches, filthy open shirt exposing a yellowing chest, long dirty nails — received in a salon of blue and white damask furniture, silk curtains, a brilliant chandelier, superb porcelain vases full of natural flowers. He led her to a voluptuous ottoman, listened, kissed her hand, squeezed her knees, promised to free her cousin — and within twenty-four hours wrote to the husband demanding a reciprocal service. Not a disorganised lunatic but a deliberate political operator who extracted personal favours through fear.

Vergniaud — the most double-edged assessment. Perhaps the most eloquent orator in the Assembly, whose prepared speeches were strong with logic, burning with warmth, full of substance. “However, I do not love Vergniaud; I find in him the egoism of philosophy. Disdaining men, certainly because he knows them well, he does not trouble himself for them.” The moral consequence she drew was strict: “What a pity that a talent such as his was not employed with the ardor of a soul devoured by the love of the public good.”

Pache — the sustained anatomisation of betrayal. The Tartuffe of politics. He arrived every morning at seven with a piece of bread in his pocket, refused all pay and title, seeming attentive, prudent, zealous. Roland recommended him as his own successor at the Interior. The unmasking: Pache at the War Ministry began receiving Fabre, Chabot, and Montagnards while refusing Roland’s dinner invitations. As War Minister: 130 million livres in accounts impossible to render; in the twenty-four hours after he was forced to resign, appointments to sixty positions including his son-in-law made an ordonnateur at 19,000 livres and his hairdresser made a war commissary.

Barbaroux — painters would not disdain his features for a head of Antinous. Active, hardworking, frank, brave, with the vivacity of a young Marseillais. Love of pleasure adjacent to love of glory. Paired with Buzot as Nisus and Euryalus: “may they have a better fate than those two friends.”

Louvet — small, slight, poor-sighted, with a neglected coat — appearing nothing to the vulgar who do not remark the nobility of his brow and the fire in his eyes. Known for his Faublas novels, he had proven his hand could alternately shake the bells of folly and launch the thunderbolts of eloquence. Impossible to unite more wit with less pretension and more good nature.

Guadet and Gensonné — a complementary pair. Guadet impetuous, Gensonné cold. Guadet dissipated in brilliant but short bursts; Gensonné lost through deliberation the time for action. Both were tender husbands, good fathers, excellent citizens. They “succumbed under the accusation of conspirators only for having failed to coalesce themselves in favor of the good cause.”

The Fall and the Flight to the Convention

The coup of 31 May 1793 caught the Rolands at home. Madame Roland was preparing to leave Paris with her daughter when a colic attack put her to bed for six days. On 31 May the tocsin sounded. At five-thirty in the evening six armed men arrived with a warrant from the revolutionary committee to arrest Roland. He refused to recognise the authority: “I know no law that constitutes the authority you cite, and I will not comply with orders emanating from it.”

Roland immediately understood he needed to denounce this publicly. She had a letter written to the Convention president, called a cab, and went alone. The Tuileries courtyard was full of armed men. She passed through them “jumping like a bird.” At the Convention doors she used a trick — adopting the tone of a devotee of Robespierre — and was admitted. Inside the petitioners’ hall she waited an hour for Vergniaud, who finally came: “In the state the Assembly is in, I cannot flatter you, and you can barely hope; the Convention can no longer do anything.”

Roland fled that night.

The Imprisonments

She was arrested on 1 June 1793 and taken to the Abbaye prison. The arrest order bore the signature of Dorat-Cubières, the verse-writer who had tried to ingratiate himself with her through flattery when Roland was minister. She examined the warrant, found it defective in form, and refused to accompany the commissioners until they returned with a properly-executed order. She then went without protest.

From the Abbaye she wrote to the Committee of General Security asking to know on what grounds she was held: “I have no offenses to reproach myself; those who speak worst of me have never seen me… I am a mother, this title imposed duties I cannot fulfil.”

After a brief release, she was re-arrested and transferred to Sainte-Pélagie — the second and final imprisonment.

In Sainte-Pélagie she observed the factional struggle beginning to devour the Montagnards themselves: “Robespierre jealous rises against Danton; the Cordeliers and Jacobins are ready to divide. Grand spectacle preparing for us victims: the tigers are going to tear each other apart; they will perhaps forget us, unless the fury of their last moments carries them to exterminate everything before their own defeat.”

The Last Writings and Execution

She wrote four texts in her final weeks at Sainte-Pélagie: Mes dernières pensées, the Observations rapides sur l’acte d’accusation contre les députés, notes on her interrogation, and a projected defence she was not permitted to deliver.

Mes dernières pensées opens with Shakespeare’s Hamlet: “To be or not to be: it is the question. It will soon be resolved for me.” She asks forgiveness of Roland, of her daughter, of her friends. She states directly why she will not wait for the Revolutionary Tribunal’s procedures: “this vain formula of judgment is only an insulting apparatus from which, for the victims, they take care to remove the faculty of expressing oneself.” Her harshest words are for the twenty-two Girondin deputies who did not act decisively on 2 June: “Why did they not do themselves justice by falling on the monsters to annihilate them, rather than receive their verdict from them?”

She was condemned by the Revolutionary Tribunal on 18 Brumaire, Year II (8 November 1793), sentenced to death, and executed the same day in the Place de la Révolution. Her last words, addressed to the statue of Liberty in the square: “Oh Liberty, what crimes are committed in thy name!”

Jean-Marie Roland, learning of her execution, left Paris and died by his own hand on a road near Rouen, a sword-cane beside him.

The Buzot Question

Madame Roland’s relationship with Buzot — whether it was a romantic attachment or a close intellectual friendship — was hinted at in a footnote by the editor Champagneux, who preserved her private confession that she had a personal reason for wanting to leave Paris that “the time to publish has not yet come.” The full nature of the relationship was never publicly acknowledged in her lifetime or in the 1823 edition of her memoirs. Buzot’s own account, written in hiding, treats the matter with a delicacy that leaves the question unresolved.

Sources

The principal sources for Madame Roland’s life are her own memoirs in two volumes, written in the Abbaye and Sainte-Pélagie prisons between August and November 1793 and published posthumously in 1823. Volume I covers her childhood, convent, intellectual formation, her marriage, and the first Roland ministry. Volume II covers the second ministry, the imprisonments, the portraits of her political contemporaries, the last writings, and a documentary appendix. Supplementary accounts appear in the memoirs of Buzot, Louvet, and Brissot.
