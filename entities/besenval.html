Pierre-Victor, baron de Besenval

He was born in 1721 in Soleure, the capital of the Swiss Catholic cantons, into a patrician family of Savoyard origin that had served the French crown for generations. His father had been sent by Louis XIV as an envoy to Charles XII of Sweden and Augustus of Poland, then returned to the military career natural to his station, dying as a lieutenant-general and colonel of the régiment des Gardes Suisses. The boy was destined for the same path. At nine years old, he entered the régiment des Gardes as a cadet, and at thirteen he made his first campaign.

The Switzerland of his childhood was not the neutral, banking Switzerland of a later century. It was a rugged confederation of cantons whose principal export to the great powers of Europe was soldiers — hard, reliable infantry who fought for whoever paid them and whose loyalty was proverbial. The Besenvals, as officers in the French service, belonged to the military aristocracy that bridged the two worlds: Swiss by birth and loyalty, French by profession and culture. Young Pierre-Victor learned early that he was expected to be fearless, faithful, and clever, and he managed to be all three.

The Soldier

He made his name at the attack on a redoubt during one of the campaigns of the 1740s. The position was critical; the French had lost many men trying to take it. The general resolved to make one more assault with the elite of the grenadiers. Besenval, still a young officer, obtained permission to join the force. The Swiss grenadiers advanced under terrible fire, approached the redoubt, plunged into the ditch, and tried to scale the parapet. Besenval reached the front rank, climbed the wall with his bare hands, his palms bleeding from the sharp rocks. At the crucial moment, he turned to the men behind him and called out, with the particular kind of gaiety for which he became famous: Morbleu! camarades, cette situation-ci n’est pas commode; savez-vous bien que s’il n’y avoit pas des coups de fusil à gagner, on n’y tiendroit pas! The grenadiers, who had been faltering, surged forward; the redoubt was taken; Besenval was the first man over the wall.

The story, which his biographers repeated and which he himself seems to have encouraged, defined his public character: the brave, witty Swiss officer who could make men laugh while they were dying, who combined the solidity of the mercenary tradition with the lightness of the French court. He was promoted rapidly — maréchal-de-camp by the time of the Seven Years’ War, in which he served as aide-de-camp to the duc d’Orléans, the grandfather of the future Philippe-Égalité. He fought at Hastenbeck (1757) and Villinghausen (1761), and served in the campaign of 1757 alongside the officer-academicians of Drévenich — that curious literary academy formed by French generals in the field, where Besenval first tried his hand at writing prose and verse.

He was never wounded. It became a point of pride, almost a superstition. In a lifetime of exposing himself to fire, he came through untouched; and those who knew him attributed it to something more than luck. There was a quality about Besenval that danger could not quite reach, as if the same sanguine temperament that made him incapable of sustained resentment also made him proof against lead.

The Courtier

The peace brought him to Versailles, where his name and his position in the Swiss Guards naturally drew him. He became a fixture at court in the last years of Louis XV — that strange, decadent, brilliant world of the 1760s and early 1770s when the monarchy was still absolute in form but already eroding in fact. Besenval had the right combination of qualities for the place: a fine figure, a quick wit, a talent for friendship, and enough violence in his character to keep people uncertain of what he might do next. His temper was legendary — he admitted it himself, saying that he had tried to master it in his youth, found it impossible, and resolved to give way to his passions freely, like attacks that could not be repressed and that it was better to discharge quickly. But the fury passed as fast as it came, leaving no trace of resentment, and the man who had been the object of his rage often found himself suddenly the object of his caresses and his generosity.

The story told of his old valet Blanchard captures the man exactly. Besenval had a servant who had been with his father before him, an old man of eighty who lived in the house more as a friend than a domestic. One day Besenval received a beautiful jasmine from the Cape, intended for the queen; he entrusted it to Blanchard with instructions to water it. The old man dropped the pot. Besenval returned, saw the wreck, and flew into a fury that lasted the rest of the day. By nightfall the anger had passed and he was anxious about Blanchard’s distress. The next morning the old servant asked permission to retire to his family. Besenval exploded again: You want to leave me? You will stay, monsieur; we must live and die together. The old man insisted he was becoming odious, that his slowness provoked too much violence. Besenval, tears in his eyes, replied: You were with my father, your wife nursed me, you are more ancient in this house than I am. It is I who must leave. He took his hat and cane and made for the door. Blanchard threw himself at his feet; Besenval lifted him up, embraced him, and they swore never to part.

His intelligence was of the practical, worldly kind — more brilliant than deep, more natural than cultivated. He had a subtle and fine tact that served him as well as any education, and he believed, perhaps too readily, that a man of the world could dispense with formal instruction. He applied the same instinct to the arts, which he loved passionately but without the patience to study them properly. Yet his taste was so sure that he formed a superb cabinet of paintings and sculpture, and as an honorary member of the Académie de Peinture, he was often consulted by artists who found his advice surprisingly valuable. Sculptors, painters, and architects admitted that they owed ideas and successes to his counsel.

The Kingmaker

The court of Louis XV and the early reign of Louis XVI were a labyrinth of factions, and Besenval navigated them with the skill of a man who understood power without being consumed by the desire for it. He was a friend of the duc de Choiseul during that minister’s ascendancy, and he watched Choiseul’s fall in 1770 with the clear-eyed disillusionment of a man who knew how the game was played. He was present at the deathbed of Louis XV, saw the smallpox that killed the king and the stunned reaction of the court, and lived through the transition to the reign of Louis XVI.

His greatest political achievement was the elevation of his friend the maréchal de Ségur to the Ministry of War in 1780. Besenval had worked for years to prepare the ground, persuading everyone who could influence the appointment that Ségur was the only man capable of rescuing the French military from the chaos into which it had fallen. Ségur proved him right: he reformed the army, introduced Prussian-style discipline and manoeuvres, established permanent camps for training, and overhauled the administration of supplies and hospitals. The two men corresponded incessantly, and Besenval’s long letters to Ségur — preserved in his memoirs — are among the most detailed contemporary analyses of the French military system in the 1780s, covering everything from the drill regulations and the officer corps to the corruption of the forage contractors.

He was also the intimate friend of Chrétien-François de Lamoignon, the président à mortier of the Parlement of Paris who became Keeper of the Seals in 1787. It was Besenval who urged Lamoignon, during a famous conversation in the magistrate’s study, to undertake the reform of judicial abuses in the Parlement. Looking at the portrait of the great Lamoignon (the seventeenth-century premier président), Besenval had asked: Does he never speak to you? Lamoignon replied that he often heard the portrait telling him to try to become worthy of him. Well then, answered Besenval, isn’t that telling you that it is not enough to be a good father, a good husband, a good friend, a good magistrate — that when you bear such a name and occupy a place where you can make yourself useful, you must not content yourself with the virtues of an obscure citizen? The speech worked. Lamoignon undertook the reform, and when he finally became garde-des-sceaux in 1787, Besenval was the man who had put him on the path.

The Crisis

By 1787, Besenval was in his mid-sixties, a lieutenant-general, a grand’croix of the Order of Saint-Louis, governor of Haguenau, and commander of the provinces of the interior — a vast jurisdiction covering the Île-de-France (excluding Paris itself), the Soissonnais, the Berry, the Bourbonnais, the Orléanais, the Touraine, and the Maine. He had also been lieutenant-colonel of the Swiss Guards since 1764, second-in-command of the Swiss regiments in French service. He had reached the summit of a career that had begun with a nine-year-old cadet in Soleure.

The crisis of 1787-1789 unfolded before him with the terrible clarity of a tragedy whose every act he could foresee and whose outcome he could not prevent. He watched the Assembly of Notables fail to solve the financial crisis. He saw Calonne dismissed, Brienne appointed, Lamoignon made Keeper of the Seals, the Parlement exiled, the May Edicts imposed, the Cour plénière established, the provinces of Brittany and Dauphiné in revolt, the treasury empty, Brienne dismissed, Necker recalled. He wrote in his memoirs that he had predicted the catastrophe years before — that the morality of the philosophes, which tended only to destroy every principle of religion and subordination, could only end in ruin. But what he could not have foreseen was the point to which things actually came: the unheard-of, innumerable faults of the ministers, the weakness of the king, the decay of the nobility, the hypocrisy and clumsiness of the clergy, the insolence and cupidity of the faction that seized control of the National Assembly.

In April 1789, the comte d’Affry, colonel of the Swiss Guards, suffered a grave accident that nearly killed him. As lieutenant-colonel, Besenval was obliged to replace him, taking on the detail of Paris — the command of the troops responsible for maintaining order in the capital — in addition to all his other responsibilities. It was a burden that would have crushed a younger man. Besenval, at sixty-eight, bore it without complaint. The grain shortages that spring had set off riots across his command; the Réveillon uprising in the faubourg Saint-Antoine in April had already shown how fragile order was. He spent his days managing the distribution of troops to protect markets and grain convoys, and his nights at the assembly of the lieutenant of police, trying to ensure the arrival of wheat for the subsistence of Paris.

And then July came.

The July Days

On 12 July 1789, the news reached Paris that Necker had been dismissed. The insurrection that followed took an alarming character from the first hour. Besenval, commanding the troops at the Champ de Mars and the various posts around the capital, faced an impossible situation. The regiments under his orders — the Swiss Guards, the hussards de Berchiny, the dragons de Choiseul, the regiment of Salis-Samade — were concentrated on the Place Louis XV (the modern Place de la Concorde) and in the Champs-Élysées. He had four pieces of cannon. As the crowds grew and the violence spread, his troops were assailed with insults, stones, and pistol shots. Several men were seriously wounded. Not a single soldier made a threatening gesture. Besenval had given strict orders: not a drop of the citizens’ blood was to be spilled.

It was the decision that defined his place in history, and the one for which he was later nearly hanged.

As the disorder increased hour by hour, Besenval debated his options. If he ordered the troops into Paris, he would start a civil war. Blood would flow on both sides, and nothing useful would be accomplished for the public tranquillity. The troops were being seduced before his eyes; he received warnings about their loyalty. Versailles — the court, the ministers, the king — sent him no orders. They persisted in regarding three hundred thousand mutinous men as a mere mob, and the Revolution as a riot. His couriers to the maréchal de Broglie went unanswered. A second courier was intercepted by spies of the popular army.

At one in the morning on 13 July, Besenval made his decision: he withdrew the troops from the capital and left Paris to itself.

The 13th and 14th of July were a nightmare. Besenval moved his headquarters to the École Militaire and then to the Invalides, where the governor, de Sombreuil, showed him the arsenal. Thirty-two thousand muskets were stored in the cellars. The Invalides themselves — the veteran soldiers who guarded the building — had been corrupted. For ten days, money had been filling their pockets. A cripple had been discovered smuggling in packets of licentious and mutinous songs. Sombreuil told him that if the cannoneers received the order to load their pieces, they would turn them on the governor’s own apartment. Besenval wrote to Broglie again, reporting these facts. He received no reply.

At five in the morning on 14 July, a man entered Besenval’s room. He had inflamed eyes, rapid and abrupt speech, an audacious bearing, and something eloquent about him that struck the old officer. Monsieur le baron, the man said, you must be warned, to prevent a useless resistance. Today the barrières of Paris will be burned. I am sure of it, and can do nothing to stop it, nor can you. Do not try to prevent it. You will sacrifice men, and not extinguish a single torch. Besenval did not have him arrested. He let him go.

Between nine and noon, the arsenal of the Invalides was pillaged. Thirty or forty thousand men entered through every door and armed themselves with whatever came to hand. The soldiers of the Hôtel not only failed to resist — they helped. By noon the Bastille had fallen. De Launay, the governor, was dead. Besenval had warned Broglie eight days earlier that de Launay should be replaced — the man’s nerve was gone, his head troubled by noise. The proposal was rejected on grounds of equity, and the Bastille was taken.

A council of general officers at the École Militaire concluded that the effervescence was impossible to repress. The troops were visibly wavering; a colonel assured Besenval, with tears in his eyes, that his regiment would not march. Besenval ordered a retreat to Sèvres. As the troops began to move, he finally received Broglie’s order: he was to make his retreat. It was not troubled by the people.

The Scapegoat

He went directly to Versailles and reported to the king. It was the first time Louis XVI had heard the full story of what had happened. For three days, Besenval was almost the only minister-level figure at the king’s side. Louis came to Paris on 17 July, submitted to the new municipal authority, wore the tricolour cockade, and confirmed Bailly as mayor. The monarchy had capitulated.

Besenval had become the object of the Revolution’s hatred. The faction in Paris believed — or claimed to believe — that he had planned to massacre the city. He was the visible symbol of the military repression that had failed, and the victors needed a scapegoat. The king, warned that his life was in danger, ordered him to leave for Switzerland. Besenval resisted; the king insisted.

He left Versailles at dusk on the night of 19 July, disguised in the uniform of the maréchaussée des chasses, with two cavalrymen as escort. The disguise, which he accepted only after his friends begged and wept, humiliated him. This precaution that smacks of fear, he wrote later, cost my character a great deal. It was perhaps the first time I let myself be led. He was right to be sceptical. The next morning, stopping at an inn in the village of Villegruis near Provins, he was recognised. He had made the mistake of consulting a map in public and asking about the road to the frontier. The tocsin sounded; the inn was surrounded by two or three hundred armed peasants. He was arrested.

The commissioners sent from Paris to collect him told him the story of Foulon — the old intendant of finances who had been hanged from a lamppost, his head paraded on a pike — and gave him to understand that a similar fate awaited him. Necker, learning of the arrest, intervened and saved his life. Besenval never forgot it. I do not know and see only one thing: M. Necker saved my life. Without changing my opinion on his political operations and his errors — let us say the word — I vow to him, until my last day, attachment and gratitude.

Mirabeau, vexed that Necker had scored a point, had the decision reversed. Besenval was sent to Brie-Comte-Robert, to a ruined medieval fortress that was hastily repaired to receive him. His gaoler was a procureur named Bourdon — the same Bourdon who would later sit in the Convention as Bourdon de l’Oise, the man who voted for the king’s death and who was eventually deported to Cayenne. Besenval, with the sangfroid that was his peculiar gift, treated the whole thing as a farce. Bourdon strutted about in epaulettes, convinced that Besenval had plotted to besiege Paris with red-hot shot and grills. Commandant, Besenval told him, I have never known anything but the ruses of war. I am ignorant of those of the law. But I know enough not to be taken in by them.

He spent three months at Brie-Comte-Robert, playing trictrac with a village curé who panicked every time a drum sounded, entertaining himself with the pranks of the Bazoche (the young clerks who guarded him), and watching Bourdon’s burlesque authority with the same detached amusement with which he had once watched the court at Versailles. In November, he was transferred to the Châtelet in Paris, the abominable prison where he spent the winter awaiting trial. Mobs gathered under his window shouting for his head. He received four anonymous notes: They will come; but I will be there. He never discovered who sent them.

The Trial and the End

His trial before the Châtelet, constituted as the tribunal for crimes of lèse-nation, began in the early months of 1790. The committee of research of the Commune of Paris had assembled a hundred and fifty witnesses. Among the prosecutors were Brissot — the future Girondin leader — and other figures who would dominate the Revolution’s first years. The accusation was as simple as it was absurd: that Besenval had planned to massacre Paris. His defence, equally simple, was unanswerable: he had been ordered to suppress the insurrection and had found it impossible to do so.

His advocate was a certain Desèze, the same man who would later defend Louis XVI before the Convention. Desèze’s eloquence was enough. On 1 March 1790, the tribunal acquitted him. His friends, who had filled the courtroom every day of the trial, surrounded him as he walked free.

There is a story that his editor tells, and it is worth recording for what it reveals of the man. During the preparation of his defence, Besenval’s lawyers discovered a written order from the king, dated 12 July 1789, bearing the words: The baron de Besenval will repel force by force. It was a complete defence — a royal order that justified everything he had done and could not have done. His lawyers brought it to him exultantly. Besenval read it, tore it up, and threw it into the fire. What! I would use such an order, which would be turned against this unhappy prince! No, messieurs, save me without that, if you can. He laughed at the consternation of his procureur, who understood nothing of heroism.

He returned to his house, to his friends, to the remnants of a life that the Revolution had shattered. He was not well. During one of the trial sessions, leaving a hearing of witnesses called in his defence, he had suffered the first attack of the illness that would kill him. He knew it. This is the disease that will kill me, he said. It did.

He died in 1791, at the age of seventy, at the very moment when the last things he had loved — the monarchy, the army he had spent his life serving, the world of ordered hierarchy and personal honour that had been the air he breathed — were being swept away beyond any hope of recovery. His memoirs, entrusted to his executor A. J. de Ségur, were nearly lost during the Terror, when Ségur was imprisoned and the manuscript narrowly saved from seizure. They were finally published in 1805, fourteen years after his death.

He left no heir. He left no great political legacy. He left nothing but the memoirs — four volumes of sharp, negligent, brilliant prose that captured the voice of the old regime’s military aristocracy in its last generation: brave, loyal, corrupt, witty, violent, generous, and utterly unable to understand the forces that were destroying it. It is the voice of a man who fought a revolution with nothing but a bad temper and a sense of honour, and who, when he could fight no longer, sat down to write about it.
