Marie-Antoinette

Marie-Antoinette (1755–1793), Archduchess of Austria and Queen of France as wife of Louis XVI, was the most controversial woman of the French Revolution. From her arrival in France in 1770 as a fourteen-year-old bride through her execution in October 1793, she was the subject of more sustained public fascination, rumour, and hostility than any other figure at court. The figure that emerges from the records of those who knew her — her first lady-in-waiting, the American diplomat Gouverneur Morris, and the Swiss officer the Baron de Besenval — is sharply at odds with the legend that grew around her.

The Arrival at Versailles

She was met on the river island of the Rhine between Kehl and Strasbourg. All of France seemed to be gathered: Bernese, Austrian, Alsatian officers mixed with the crowd; women of Strasbourg with uncovered hair and flowers in their hats; silk weavers bringing her their best; a crowd so dense that the carriages could barely push through. In Strasbourg she learned that she was grown too tall for the new style of hairdressing: the dresser found nothing ready. The court of Louis XV was not what she had imagined: Marie-Antoinette found the young king awkward and silent, the king’s daughters remote, the king’s mistress Madame du Barry — a woman who had risen from the streets to the king’s bed — at the centre of every political calculation. The dauphine refused to speak to the favourite, citing protocol. The Austrian ambassador Mercy d’Argenteau and her mother Maria Theresa in Vienna both counselled her to comply.

The Young Queen’s Circle

After her accession in 1774, Marie-Antoinette surrounded herself with a small group of intimates: the Princesse de Lamballe (superintendent of her household), the Comtesse de Polignac (governess of the children), the Duc de Coigny, her page the Comte d’Artois, and the Duc de Guiche. The king’s aunts (Mesdames Adélaïde, Victoire, and Sophie) resented the queen’s separation of her circle from theirs and the queen’s independence from the forms of the court. The king’s brothers showed ambivalence: the Comte de Provence silent and watchful, the Comte d’Artois openly affectionate.

The queen built the Petit Trianon as a private retreat — a separate household within the larger court — where she could live without the ceremonial of Versailles. Her intimacy with the Polignac circle and her private amusements at Trianon became the material for the libelles: pamphlets printed in English-controlled presses in Paris and the Netherlands, depicting the queen as sexually corrupt, conspiring against France in Austrian interests, and indifferent to the sufferings of the people.

The Diamond Necklace Affair (1785–1786)

The affair of the diamond necklace is the decisive event in the destruction of the queen’s public reputation. The cardinal de Rohan, ambitious and debt-ridden, was deceived by a woman calling herself the Comtesse de la Motte-Valois into believing the queen had secretly authorised him to purchase a diamond necklace worth 1,600,000 livres on her behalf. The cardinal, who had been estranged from the queen since his time as ambassador to Vienna, was eager to believe the queen’s favour was returning. He did not deal with the queen directly but with an intermediary who forged her signature and produced — for a single night — a woman impersonating her in the gardens of Versailles.

The fraud was discovered when the cardinal tried to negotiate the necklace with the jewellers Boehmer and Bassenge. The queen, on learning she was implicated, insisted on a prosecution. The Parlement of Paris tried the cardinal and acquitted him — a verdict that was also a judgment on the queen. She was innocent of the fraud but publicly implicated in a scandal that confirmed the worst assumptions of her enemies: that her circle trafficked in secret negotiations, that she was allied with men disloyal to the monarchy, and that the royal family’s reputation was so damaged that a cardinal could be acquitted for defrauding the crown. The Affair became a fixture of revolutionary rhetoric — invoked as proof of the depravity of the court.

The High Summer of the Monarchy (1786–1789)

The queen’s diaries show her absorbed in the life of the court and the children, at Trianon and at Versailles, during the years when the fiscal crisis of the monarchy was deepening. Her brother the Emperor Joseph II visited her at Versailles (the journey improvised after a stay with the Comte d’Artois), and the correspondence with Vienna shows that Maria Theresa’s death in 1780 had removed the one figure who could command the queen’s obedience. The king’s gradual physical decline from the illness that would kill him — a tubercular condition that affected his gait and his speech after an operation in 1786 — was recorded without editorial emphasis by those close to the court.

The king’s younger brother, the Comte d’Artois, was the queen’s most consistent confidant among the king’s family. He alone of the princes shared and sustained her circle’s attitudes. The king’s older brother, the Comte de Provence (the future Louis XVIII), was perceptive enough to see the danger of the royal response to the growing political crisis. The king’s aunts (Mesdames) maintained a residence at Belle-Vue and exercised influence through their confessors.

The October Days (5–6 October 1789)

The women’s march to Versailles and the royal family’s forced transfer to Paris is the critical turning point in Marie-Antoinette’s revolutionary history. The Flanders Regiment had arrived at Versailles on 23 September, and the officers were entertaining the royal bodyguards. The queen’s appearance at the window with the dauphin — holding him out to the regiment’s view — showed the soldiers a woman in distress, appealing to their loyalty.

On 5 October, the Parisian women arrived at Versailles in the afternoon. The queen was advised not to receive a delegation of the insurgents. She stood at the balcony of her apartment — the Hall of Mirrors — with the king and the royal children. The crowd demanded that the king remove to Paris. He agreed.

The queen could not sleep during the night of 5–6 October. She spent the hours moving between her apartment and the king’s, waiting for decisions, while the court descended into chaos. Through it all she maintained her composure and the forms of dignity. Then came the night attack on the queen’s apartment — the mob breaking through the guards — and her escape through the Œil-de-Bœuf to the king’s chambers, the most dramatic incident of the October Days.

The Tuileries (October 1789 – August 1792)

At the Tuileries the queen’s life changed fundamentally. The palace was cramped, cold, and overlooked by the Parisian crowd. The queen’s circle shrank as émigration pruned the court. She had lost the use of Trianon; her intimate society had scattered to the frontiers. She had become instinctively a resister.

The king’s acceptance of the Civil Constitution of the Clergy and the constitutional oath in July 1790 marked the moment when the queen ceased to hope. Her correspondence from this period with Mercy and with her brother Leopold II in Vienna shows her increasingly desperate and isolated. Gouverneur Morris, the American diplomat who moved in court circles, saw her as a figure of tragic dignity and felt sympathy for her impossible political position, though his judgment of the court’s conduct was often severe.

The Flight to Varennes (June 1791)

The royal family’s attempted escape from Paris was planned in the queen’s apartments at the Tuileries with the Comte de Fersen, a Swedish officer and one of the queen’s closest friends. The plan called for the family to leave separately, rendezvous at Bondy, and proceed by heavy carriage to Montmédy on the frontier, where royalist troops under Bouillé were to receive them. The queen spent the last weeks before the flight shipping her diamonds to Brussels through intermediaries.

The flight failed at Varennes: the carriage was recognised and stopped, the king identified from his portrait on an assignat, and the family returned to Paris under escort, silent and still dressed in the clothes of the servant’s family they had meant to use as disguises. The return journey through the villages — the crowds silent, the mayor of each commune receiving the procession — was the greatest single blow to the monarchy’s popular standing before August 1792.

The Fall of the Monarchy (10 August 1792)

The assault on the Tuileries by the sections of Paris, coordinated with the Jacobin club and the Commune, drove the royal family to seek refuge in the National Assembly. That morning the women of the court sewed uniforms, the Swiss Guards stood at their posts, and the king reviewed the troops. Then the royal family walked from the Tuileries through the gardens to the Riding School where the Assembly sat — the queen carrying the dauphin, the king quiet, the princesse de Lamballe and the duchesse d’Angoulême following.

The subsequent massacres of the Swiss Guards and of the queen’s servants — the Princesse de Lamballe killed in the September Massacres, her head carried on a pike to the Temple — marked the threshold between the queen as a political figure and the queen as a prisoner.

The Temple Prison (August 1792 – August 1793)

The family was transferred to the Temple, the medieval fortress of the Knights Templar, on 13 August 1792. They were given three floors of a tower: the king on one floor, the queen on another, the children and the king’s sister Madame Élisabeth on a third. The family was permitted to keep some servants and the freedom of the tower’s rooms and garden. The king spent his time reading and teaching the dauphin, bearing the accelerating isolation with quietness. In the autumn of 1792 the remaining servants, including the queen’s first lady-in-waiting, were dismissed.

The Conciergerie and the Trial (August – October 1793)

After the king’s execution in January 1793, the queen was separated from her son in July and transferred to the Conciergerie — the prison of the Revolutionary Tribunal — on 2 August. She was confined in a cell that was damp, narrow, and opened onto the women’s courtyard. The guards were instructed not to speak to her. Her health deteriorated.

The trial opened on 14 October 1793 before the Revolutionary Tribunal, presided by Herman, with the prosecutor Fouquier-Tinville conducting the case. The indictment charged her with treason, conspiracy with foreign powers against the French Republic, and corruption of public morals — including the specific accusation by Hébert that she had sexually abused her son. The queen’s response to this accusation was the most dramatic moment of the trial: asked to answer, she stood and said that if she had not answered the charge it was because nature itself refused to answer such an accusation against a mother — appealing to every mother in the hall. The audience was moved; the judges did not stop the trial.

She was condemned on 15 October and executed on 16 October 1793. Her last letter — to Madame Élisabeth, the king’s sister, written the morning of her death — was not delivered.
