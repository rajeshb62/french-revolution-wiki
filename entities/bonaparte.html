Napoleon Bonaparte

Napoleon Bonaparte (1769–18??), First Consul of the French Republic and Emperor of the French, dominated European affairs from 1796 through 1815. His career is the story of a man of immense military genius whose ambition consumed all other qualities, who dismantled the French Republic that had raised him, and who finally exhausted the nation that had sustained him.

1. Character

Bonaparte possessed a rich fund of positive science, a vast genius, and a character marked by cold resolution and enterprise. He had the intellectual equipment of a great commander — the scientific knowledge of his profession, the rapidity of judgment, the capacity to hold vast quantities of operational detail in his mind without confusion.

What he lacked was the moral equipment of a great statesman. He had no sensitive soul, no calmer head, no harmony between his passions and his reason. A single passion — ambition — absorbed all the others and gave his career its long brilliance, sustained by a succession of spectacular successes. But this same passion made him incapable of the retreat and consolidation that might have preserved his empire. The contrast with General Moreau is instructive: Moreau’s Rhine retreat of 1796 showed the art of the strategically wise withdrawal — an art Napoleon never possessed and never valued.

His birth had originally placed him among the subaltern officers of an artillery regiment. The Revolution gave him a stage; his genius gave him victories; but his character — ambition without limit, intelligence without moral counterweight — gave Europe twenty years of war and France a regime that exhausted everything it touched.

2. Italian Campaigns, 1796–1797

The Italian campaign of 1796–97 was the making of Bonaparte’s reputation. He took command of the Army of Italy, a force that had been neglected and undersupplied, and in a single year destroyed four Austrian armies, conquered Lombardy, and forced Austria to sue for terms.

The campaign opened with a series of rapid victories that separated the Austrian and Sardinian armies. The Sardinians were forced to a separate peace; the Austrians fell back on Mantua, the great fortress city that commanded the approaches to the Tyrol and the Austrian heartland. Field Marshal Wurmser descended from the Alps with a relieving army; Bonaparte abandoned the siege of Mantua, massed his forces against the Austrian columns, and defeated them at Castiglione in August 1796. The victory re-established the siege and sent captured Austrian flags to the Directory in Paris.

A second Austrian attempt at relief, under Field Marshal Alvinzi, culminated in the three-day battle of Arcola in November 1796 — a contest fought in the marshes and canals between the Adige and the Brenta, described by the marshal Trivulzio as a battle of giants. Bonaparte himself was knocked into a marsh by Austrian fire and had to be pulled from the water by his staff. The French flanked the Austrian position and Alvinzi withdrew.

A third Austrian effort, again under Alvinzi, was crushed at Rivoli in January 1797 in Bonaparte’s most complete Italian victory. The Austrian army was destroyed; the road to Vienna lay open. Mantua surrendered in February 1797, Wurmser capitulating with his garrison after a six-month siege. Bonaparte then drove through the Norican Alps to Klagenfurt and Leoben, within a hundred miles of Vienna. Austria signed the preliminaries of Leoben in April 1797.

The British Mediterranean position became untenable precisely because Bonaparte’s Italian victories cut off the Austro-Sardinian forces on which British strategy depended. The British fleet under Jervis withdrew from the Mediterranean in 1796, a strategic retreat made necessary by land victories that sea power could not prevent.

3. Egyptian Expedition, 1798–1799

Bonaparte’s Egyptian expedition was two things simultaneously: a genuine strategic ambition and a flight from the Directory’s accumulating failures. The Orient was conceived as the road to India; Egypt was to be the base from which France could threaten the richest of Britain’s possessions and sever the commerce of the East. Bonaparte presented it as the great blow against England on its most vulnerable flank.

The fleet that sailed from Toulon in May 1798 carried an army of some 35,000 men and a commission of savants and artists. But the ships were ill-supplied: almost without spare rigging or stores, three of thirteen ships-of-the-line not fit for sea. The entire operation depended on avoiding Nelson’s Mediterranean squadron long enough to land the army. Nelson twice misguessed the expedition’s destination, searching first at Alexandria and then ranging eastward, before returning to find the French fleet anchored in Aboukir Bay.

The Battle of the Nile, August 1, 1798, was the decisive event. Nelson attacked without waiting for tactical formation, sending ships inside the French line as well as outside. Thirteen of seventeen French ships-of-the-line were taken or destroyed; Admiral Brueys was killed. The army in Egypt was cut off absolutely from France. The Nile was the battle that made Waterloo possible — it demonstrated that sea power could isolate any expeditionary force, regardless of its land strength.

Bonaparte had to choose between watching France drift from Egypt or returning through a British-dominated sea. He chose to return, slipping through the blockade in a fast frigate — a personal gamble that succeeded and that he later represented as a strategic necessity. The Syrian campaign that preceded his departure had failed at Acre, where the British naval squadron under Sidney Smith supplied the garrison with naval artillery and intercepted Bonaparte’s siege train at sea.

4. 18 Brumaire 1799

The coup of 18 Brumaire (November 1799) was a personal political achievement of extraordinary craft, not a rescue of France from the Directory’s collapse. Bonaparte cultivated the Abbé Sieyès, the most influential intellectual of the Revolution’s constitutional phase, as his instrument. He attached himself to Sieyès to deceive him, and deceived him in order not to have to fear him. He regarded his future colleagues as coarse and inert mud, deprived of feeling, ideas, and will.

The coup itself succeeded because military glory was the avowed title of Bonaparte’s elevation. But the true causes were fear and venality. The deputies who abandoned the Republic did so not from conviction but from a calculation of personal advantage and a terror of the alternatives. The court of courtiers that immediately formed at Bonaparte’s headquarters and at the Luxembourg Palace was composed of those who saw in the general their path to favour.

The Constitution of Year VIII that emerged from the coup was a design in which nothing was lacking to guarantee the citizen’s liberty except everything that was lacking in the men who would execute it. The Senate’s anticipated devotion confirmed a servility already inherent in the structure. Everything seemed arranged, men and things, for the advantage of a single man.

The obstacles that had stopped or slowed great captains during several campaigns were finally overcome — the path was clear for the consolidation that followed.

5. The Consulate, 1799–1804

The Consulate was a period of genuine achievement and deepening corruption — each feeding the other. Bonaparte as First Consul undertook immense public works: canals, roads over the Mont-Blanc and the Simplon, the harnessing of mountain rivers for Paris’s water supply. The civil and criminal codes were framed under his direction, consolidating the legal reforms of the Revolution. The education law under Fourcroy established a system of state-controlled secondary and higher education. The Legion of Honor was created, merging military and civil service in a single order of merit — a gesture genuinely philosophical in its conception.

The Concordat with the Papacy (1801) restored Catholic worship to France after the dechristianisation of the Revolution. But the measure carried a darker edge: restoring the Church was necessary; making the clergy agents of the state’s social-control apparatus was something else. The Senate’s self-abasements revealed the mechanism by which the Republic was dismantled within constitutional forms. The Senate doubled the duration of the Consulship before it had fairly begun; it proclaimed the Life Consulship; it erected dictatorial authority by a single sénatus-consulte without the authentic concurrence of the people. The chiefs of intrigue pressed these accelerations on Bonaparte before he could demand them, assuring themselves thereby of the first dignities and the favour of the master.

The Saint-Domingue expedition of 1801–02 exhibited the pattern that would define the Empire: ambition disregarding consequences, announced with the imposing apparatus of dictatorial authority against a public opinion that clearly disapproved. The expedition was a disaster: the army melted in the tropical climate, the colony was lost, and the financial and military cost were enormous at the moment France most needed to prepare for the renewal of war with England.

The Peace of Amiens (1802–03), the fourteen-month interval of peace between Britain and France, was never a real peace. Bonaparte used the truce to consolidate French domination of the Continent: French troops remained in the Batavian Republic, the Italian Republic was established with Bonaparte as its president, and Piedmont was annexed outright. The treaty was an armed interlude in which both sides prepared for the next round.

6. The Empire, 1804–1812

The Empire was proclaimed in December 1804. The atmosphere was one of singular epidemic — not merely the vulgar who always create supernatural powers for their rulers, but the educated classes, senators, and men of letters who proclaimed Napoleon’s fortune a certain sign of divine vocation to universal empire. The coronation ceremony at Notre-Dame revealed the new emperor’s character with perfect clarity: Pope Pius VII consecrated the crown; Napoleon took it from the altar and placed it on his own head. The contrast was devastating: Charlemagne had received the crown from Rome as a favour from the Church; Bonaparte, recognizing no intermediary between himself and Providence, commanded.

The military achievements of the Empire’s first years were unparalleled. Austerlitz (December 1805), fought on the anniversary of Napoleon’s coronation, destroyed the combined Austro-Russian army and shook the throne of Emperor Francis. Napoleon harangued his army with the confidence that inspires certainty of victory, entered Vienna abandoned by its government, and reassembled the Austrian monarchy by signing peace at Pressburg. Austerlitz was made possible by the strategic failure of Napoleon’s naval combinations. The Trafalgar campaign — the invasion of England abandoned when Villeneuve’s fleet took refuge in Cadiz instead of proceeding to Brest — forced Napoleon to turn the Army of England eastward. Trafalgar, fought on October 21, 1805, destroyed the combined Franco-Spanish fleet and broke Napoleon’s naval arm for the rest of the war. Unable to fight Britain at sea, he turned to the economic warfare of the Continental System.

Jena-Auerstedt (October 1806) was the most complete and rapid of Napoleon’s victories: Berlin occupied, Prussian fortresses falling in sequence. The Treaty of Tilsit (July 1807) with Alexander I marked the height of Napoleon’s power. The Continental System against Britain — the Berlin Decree of November 1806 and the Milan Decree of 1807 — declared the British Isles in a state of blockade and prohibited all commerce with them. The system was a strategic conception that was not absurd but was impossible to execute without a continental compliance Napoleon could not maintain. The Spanish ulcer, the Austrian marriage of 1810 — the moment when Napoleon’s pride reached the point of delirium, manipulated by Vienna’s calculated humiliation — and the growing tension with Russia over the blockade’s enforcement all pointed toward the crisis of 1812.

7. The Decline, 1812–1815

The Russian campaign was defined by the political failure that preceded the military catastrophe. Napoleon entered Russia without having resolved the Polish question. If he had organised the government of Poland, fixed its borders, armed it, and made it capable of defending itself, Russia would have been forced to respect the barrier. The opportunity was lost because the glory of creating a people held few attractions for a man who found peoples to subjugate and states to conquer. The retreat from Moscow was less a meteorological catastrophe than the consequence of a campaign that had no exit: having sought a battle the Russians refused to give and an occupation the inhabitants burned rather than concede, Napoleon found himself deep in Russia with an army impossible to feed and a political situation impossible to manage.

The 1813 campaign in Germany opened with successes that proved a deceptive prelude to the reverses fortune was preparing. A peace was available on terms that, while not restoring the pre-1812 position, would have preserved France and its emperor. Austria offered itself as mediator between Napoleon its son-in-law and the allied powers. Napoleon could have concluded a treaty no less advantageous than honorable; but he claimed to dictate peace, not negotiate it.

The battle of Leipzig (October 1813) broke the Empire’s hold on Germany. The 1814 campaign in France — fought on French soil for the first time since 1792 — produced some of Napoleon’s most brilliant tactical improvisations. But the fundamental political decision to reject Austrian mediation and refuse the Frankfurt peace terms made the military brilliance irrelevant. The abdication at Fontainebleau in April 1814 was the deserved consequence of a politics in opposition to the lights of the time, the dispositions of the peoples, and local conditions.

The Elba episode and the Hundred Days followed. Napoleon returned to France in March 1815, landing at Cannes and marching through mountain roads to avoid the Rhône valley. At Grenoble, the battalion sent to arrest him was won over at pistol range; Lyon fell; Paris opened its gates as Louis XVIII fled. The men who rallied to Bonaparte did so for different reasons: some moved by genuine military devotion, some by hatred of the Bourbons, some by personal advantage. These three motives were not equivalent.

The Acte Additionnel of April 1815 — Napoleon’s proclaimed return to constitutional principles — was a clandestine work written by valets under the master’s dictation, in which despotism veiled itself with some modesty and pushed to its last terms the doctrine of Machiavelli. Waterloo (June 18, 1815) ended the Hundred Days. The rout of the French army, Napoleon’s return to Paris, and his second abdication — this time in favour of his son Napoleon II, a constitutional fiction the Chambers declined to accept — closed the military career.

8. Final Judgment

The final judgment on Napoleon is contained in a single sentence that serves as the epigraph to his entire story: Napoleon Bonaparte was a great error of nature that she cannot commit twice. He had no model; he will have no imitators. His system of politics, strongly conceived, was outside human proportions and almost ideal in its coherence — but ideal for whom? For the man who would rule without consent, without law, without the harmony of passion and reason that makes political life possible.

The ambition that consumed all other qualities, the cold resolution that operated without the check of a sensitive soul, the vast intelligence applied without moral direction — these made him the greatest commander of his age and the worst statesman. He destroyed the Republic that had raised him, exhausted France in wars that could not be won, and left Europe in ruins and his dynasty extinct. The history of Napoleon Bonaparte, as written by the men who lived through his rise and fall, is the history of a genius without character, a soldier without a statesman, and a revolution that devoured its own children.
