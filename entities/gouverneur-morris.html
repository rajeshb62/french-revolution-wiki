Gouverneur Morris

He arrived in Paris in February 1789 with the ink barely dry on the Constitution of the United States — a document he had written with his own hand as chairman of the Committee of Style, the man who gave the world the words “We the People.” He was thirty-six years old, tall, commanding, with a wooden leg below the left knee (lost when a runaway horse overturned his phaeton in a Philadelphia street in 1780), and a New York Federalist’s deep suspicion of enthusiasm in politics. He came on commercial business for Robert Morris, the financier of the American Revolution — no relation, but the man who had been his chief in the Superintendent of Finance office during the war. The business was the tobacco contract with the Farmers-General of France, a tangled inheritance from the old system of monopoly that was unraveling even as he stepped off the ship at Havre.

But within weeks of his landing, the world he had come to negotiate in began to dissolve.

The Green Diary

On his first days in Paris, Morris visited the shop of one Marquis, at À l’Espérance, rue Neuve des Petits Champs, and bought a blank-book bound in apple-green vellum. On March 1, 1789, he made the first entry. The diary that followed — written in a terse, precise hand, no paragraph breaks, business and politics and weather and women all in the same breath — became the most sustained American eyewitness account of the French Revolution ever written. He kept it for nearly four years, through the fall of the Bastille, the October Days, the flight to Varennes, the storming of the Tuileries, and the September Massacres. After August 10, 1792, when his house on the rue de la Planche became a refuge for royalists fleeing the insurrection, the diary fell silent. But by then the record was already extraordinary.

That first entry ran: “This Morning employed in Writing after my Return from M! Le Nd with whom I breakfasted and conferred on the Means of proceeding further with Respect to the Tobacco. I suggested to him a Plan for drawing Silver from Havanna which he did not seem to like… Dine with M! Du Molley. Madame wishes some American Plants and Seeds. Go to the french Theatre. The Misanthrope is acted with great and deserved Applause.” Already the pattern was set: business, society, observation — the three threads of his Paris life wound together on the same page, each given the same weight.

Business in Revolutionary Paris

The tobacco contract with the Farmers-General was the surface reason for Morris’s journey. Robert Morris, the Philadelphia merchant-prince whose wealth had helped finance the Revolution, had negotiated a monopoly on supplying the French tobacco market. The contract was now in dispute: the American end had been undercut by a competing agreement, the French end was tangled in the Farm’s labyrinthine bureaucracy, and the political crisis was making everyone reluctant to pay. Morris spent his first year in Paris shuttling between Le Couteulx et Cie (the bankers who acted as intermediaries), Le Normand (the Farm’s representative), and an endless series of dinners and conferences at which nothing was quite decided.

“Paris is if possible a worse place for Business than Virginia,” he wrote early on. “Every Body here has his Country Seat and comes to Town to do Business once in three or four Days and then works not to finish but to get Rid of Work that he may again get out of Town. Indeed Pleasure is the great Business.” He pressed, cajoled, drafted memoranda, wrote sharp letters, and slowly extracted concessions. The tobacco negotiations would run for years, interleaved with the Revolution’s convulsions; he was still trying to collect payment when the monarchy itself had ceased to exist.

The same commercial thread ran through every phase of his time in France. He worked the whale oil trade (a major American export), the tariff rates under debate in the Assembly committees, the Dutch banking houses for loans to develop the Delaware ironworks at Trenton, the London bankers for Robert Morris’s sprawling credit network. He lobbied ministers, drafted memorials, calculated exchange rates, and recorded the assignat’s accelerating depreciation with the precision of a man watching his own money evaporate. The diary in 1791 has a section the editor titled “Whales and Nicotine” — two weeks of concentrated lobbying on both commodities at once, the commercial and the diplomatic merging into a single daily round.

The Salons and the Louvre

No account of Morris’s Paris years can separate the business from the social, and no social thread ran deeper than his connection with Adèle de Flahaut. He met her in March 1789 at a dinner at the Comte d’Angivillier’s — the Superintendent of the King’s Buildings, who had given her rooms in the Louvre. “She speaks English and is a pleasing Woman. If I might judge from Appearances, not a sworn Enemy to Intrigue,” he wrote. She was twenty-eight, married at eighteen to the fifty-three-year-old Comte de Flahaut, and the acknowledged mistress of Charles-Maurice de Talleyrand-Périgord, then Bishop of Autun, by whom she had a young son. Her apartment in the Louvre — the blue room, as the diary calls it — became Morris’s fixed social coordinate in Paris, a place of refuge, conversation, and politics, where the news of the Revolution arrived before it reached the newspapers.

The triangle with Talleyrand gave the relationship its peculiar tension. The Bishop of Autun — lame, cynical, visionary, the man who would become the diplomat of diplomats — was Morris’s rival and his intellectual companion simultaneously. They dined together at Madame de Flahaut’s table, debated constitutions and finance, visited painters’ studios, and maintained a surface of civilized ease that did not quite conceal the competition beneath. “The eternal triangle was squared,” the editor observed: “there were pretty lady, antique husband, brilliant official lover, and triumphant newcomer.”

Morris’s other great social subject was Lafayette — the hero of two worlds, the man who had fought for American liberty and now commanded the National Guard of Paris. Morris studied him with the clinical attention of a portraitist who admires his subject and sees through him at the same time. Lafayette told him, in a moment of confidence, that he had “had the utmost Power his Heart could wish and is grown tired of it. That he has commanded absolutely an hundred thousand Men, has marched his Sovereign about the Streets as he pleased, prescribed the Degree of Applause which he should receive, and could have detained him Prisoner had he thought proper.” Morris’s private verdict: “He wishes therefore as soon as possible to return to private Life. In this last Expression he deceives himself or wishes to deceive me; a little of both perhaps. But in Fact he is the Lover of Freedom from Ambition, of which there are two Kinds, the one born of Pride, the other of Vanity and his partakes most of the latter.”

Jefferson was a regular presence — the American Minister, with whom Morris visited the National Assembly, exchanged political analyses, and compared notes on Parisian life. Their relationship was cordial but not warm; Jefferson’s enthusiasm for the Revolution was something Morris could not share, and the tobacco dispute that had brought Morris to France had originated in part from Jefferson’s role in dismantling the Morris monopoly. Still, they dined together, consulted on commercial questions, and maintained the mutual respect of two men who understood that they disagreed on fundamentals.

Observer of the Revolution

Morris watched the Revolution unfold with the same compressed precision he gave his business accounts. The entries for July 12–14, 1789 — the days the Bastille fell — are among the most vivid in the diary.

On July 12, Necker was dismissed. Morris was dining with the Maréchal de Castries when the Marshal took him aside and told him. The news broke Paris open. Morris rode through the city and witnessed a cavalry charge in the Place Louis Quinze — sabres drawn, stones thrown, pistols fired without effect. “The People are employed in breaking open the Armorers’ Shops,” he noted, “and presently a large Body of the Gardes Françoises appear with Bayonets fixed, in the Garden, mingled with the Mob.” He predicted what was coming: “If the Representatives of the Tiers have formed a just Estimate of their Constituents, in ten Days all France will be in Commotion.”

On July 13, he walked to the Louvre with a green bow in his hat — “the Fashion of the Day which every Body is obliged to comply with who means to march in Peace. It is somewhat whimsical that this Day of Violence and Tumult is the only one in which I have dared to walk the Streets, but as no Carriages are abroad but the Fiacres I do not hazard being crushed, and I apprehend nothing from the Populace.” He recorded the forced prisons, the seized grain from the Monastery of Saint-Lazare (“on every Cart a Friar”), the rumors from Versailles.

July 14 was in some ways the quietest of the three days in his telling — he was detained by business with Nesbitt at the Temple, stopped twice for weapons searches — but the entry for July 22 is the one that stops the reader. Walking under the arcade of the Palais-Royal, he saw the head and body of Foulon, the old minister, carried through the streets on a pike, the body dragged naked on the earth, and then Bertier his son-in-law, also torn to pieces. “Gracious God what a People!” he wrote.

Lafayette gave him a passport to visit the Bastille on July 21. Morris went with Madame de Flahaut, the Abbé Bertrand, and Capellis. The stench was terrible — “more than I wish to see,” he wrote. The same day, visiting a painter’s studio, he saw a canvas titled Love Escaped from His Cage and proposed a different subject: the guardsman who, seizing the Bastille gate and unable to bring it down, cried to his comrades to pull on his legs, and held while a dozen men pulled him like a rope, racking his joints, until the gate gave way. “To represent him drawn out of Joint with his Head turned round, encouraging them to draw still harder, must I think have a fine Effect.” The Bishop of Autun — Talleyrand — agreed entirely.

The Secret Royalist

Throughout 1791 and 1792, as the Revolution radicalized, Morris’s position shifted from observer to participant in a clandestine and ultimately tragic royalist operation. His sympathies had always been with constitutional monarchy — he had helped write an American constitution with a strong executive, and he watched the French fumble toward a unicameral assembly with a powerless king and predicted failure from the start. “I preach incessantly Respect for the Constitution under which we live and for that which is forming here,” he wrote. But by 1792, preaching was no longer relevant. The king’s flight to Varennes in June 1791 had changed everything. Morris’s analysis was cold and clear: “He changed every Thing and now the general wish seems to be for a Republic, which is quite in the natural Order of Things.”

The royalist counter-conspiracy of 1792 drew him in through a chain of contacts: the Comte de La Marck, the former minister Montmorin, and the Provençal agent Brémond. The scheme was to organize armed men inside Paris who would rally to the king’s defense at designated points in the event of an insurrection. Morris held 5,000 louis d’or in his house — gold coins earmarked for paying reliable men when the moment came. The money had been paid out of royal funds through Brémond; the purses of twenty louis were divided and kept at Morris’s house pending activation of the network.

The moment never came. On August 10, 1792, the insurrectionary sections stormed the Tuileries before the network could be activated. The Swiss Guard was massacred. The king took refuge with the Assembly. The monarchy fell. Morris’s house on the rue de la Planche became a shelter for former minister Monciel and his family, along with others implicated in the royalist resistance. The diary was suppressed — it would have been a death warrant for everyone under his roof if discovered.

The 5,000 louis became a prolonged ordeal. Brémond had removed the gold to Swiss bankers and Morris spent years afterward pursuing its recovery, finally rendering an account to Madame Royale — the Princess Marie-Thérèse, Louis XVI’s daughter — after her release from the Temple prison. The accounting survives in the diary’s appendix: a document of remarkable clarity and moral seriousness, tracing what had become of every coin.

The American Minister

In May 1792, despite French republican protests — the Girondins knew his monarchist sympathies — Washington appointed Morris as United States Minister to France. The appointment was controversial. Morris had been too candid about his views; he had written to Washington that the Revolution was devouring itself; the French foreign ministry had lobbied against his accreditation. But Washington trusted him, and Jefferson as Secretary of State supported the appointment for reasons of policy if not personal sympathy.

Morris presented his credentials and entered the diplomatic corps at the worst possible moment. Within weeks, the June 20 invasion of the Tuileries forced the king to put on a red cap and drink to the nation from a bottle handed to him by a rioter. Within two months, the monarchy was gone. Within three, the September Massacres had killed Montmorin — the minister with whom Morris had dined and corresponded — and filled the streets of Paris with bodies.

After August 10, Morris’s dispatches to Jefferson became the primary American channel for information about the collapsing French state. He reported on the abolition of the monarchy, the proclamation of the Republic, the formation of the National Convention, the trial of the king. His position required diplomatic restraint, but his private assessments can be read between the lines of his official correspondence. He watched Robespierre survive Louvet’s accusation in the Convention — “Robespierre has got thro his Affair with Éclat” — and noted the rise of the Jacobins with the same cold eye he had turned on Lafayette’s vanity.

The Diarist’s Voice

What makes Morris’s diary extraordinary is not merely the events it records but the voice that records them. He writes in short, declarative sentences, each fact standing alone, business and atrocity and the weather given the same flat emphasis. He is laconic about violence: the heads on pikes are recorded in the same paragraph as the price of dinner. He is ironic about human weakness, including his own: “I have never yet mentioned to you that M! Jefferson… commands very much Respect in this Country and which is merited by good Sense and good Intentions,” he wrote to Robert Morris. “The french, who pique themselves on possessing the Graces, very readily excuse in others the Want of them; and to be an Étranger (like Charity) covers a Multitude of Sins.”

He is precise about business: the tobacco prices, the exchange rates, the discount on bills, the calculations of profit and loss that run like a double-entry ledger through the revolutionary turmoil. He is tender with Madame de Flahaut and dry with Lafayette and sharp with the politicians he sees making a constitution that cannot work. He is, above all, a man who refuses to be swept along by the emotional currents of his time. “I apprehend nothing from the Populace” — the sentence could stand as his motto through the whole four years.

The diary was published in 1939, edited by Beatrix Cary Davenport, who wove editorial commentary and connecting letters into the text. The original manuscript — written in a blank book bought at À l’Espérance — is the record of a man who went to Paris to negotiate a tobacco contract and ended up holding royal gold in his house while the monarchy fell around him, writing it all down in a voice so distinct that two centuries later it still sounds like a man speaking directly across a dinner table.

He left France in 1794, recalled by Washington, the United States having recognized the Republic. He returned to Morrisania — the manor on the Harlem River where he had been born in 1752 — and spent his remaining years as a farmer, a Federalist senator, and a determined opponent of Jefferson’s embargo. But the French years stayed with him. The green diary, the Louvre salon, the heads on pikes on the rue Saint-Honoré, the 5,000 louis that never bought what it was meant to buy, the king he had tried to save — they formed the great subject of his life, and he knew it.
