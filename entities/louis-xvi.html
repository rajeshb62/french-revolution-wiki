Louis XVI

Character and Personality

Louis-Auguste de France, who ascended the throne as Louis XVI on 10 May 1774 at the age of nineteen, carried into kingship a temperament singularly ill-suited to the times that awaited him. He was a man of genuine goodness — a quality on which virtually every observer agreed — but his virtues were those of a private gentleman rather than a sovereign. His reign would become a tragedy of character meeting circumstance at the worst possible hour.

He was a big man, tall and heavily built, with a thick neck and a phlegmatic constitution that gave him an appearance of dullness. In his youth he had been awkward and shy, uncomfortable in the elaborate rituals of Versailles, preferring the company of a few trusted servants to the ceaseless performance of court life. An operation performed in 1786 — a fistula or some related complaint, kept largely secret — left its mark on him. His gait became irregular, his speech slightly thickened, and those who saw him in the years that followed often remarked on a certain heaviness of movement and hesitation of utterance that compounded the impression of sluggishness. By 1789, American minister Gouverneur Morris, who observed the king at close quarters during the critical months, noted frankly that the king was already in physical decline — a man whose body seemed to be failing him even as his throne began to shake.

The king’s piety was deep and sincere. He attended mass daily, observed the full liturgical calendar of the Church, and confessed regularly. This religious devotion, however, was of a simple, almost peasant-like quality — it was not the intellectual faith of a theologian but the unquestioning belief of a man who had been raised to see the altar and the throne as twin pillars of the same divine order. It gave him steadiness of conscience but not flexibility of mind. When the Revolution eventually demanded that he choose between his crown and his salvation, his piety would paralyse him rather than guide him.

His private tastes ran to the mechanical and the physical. He was an enthusiastic hunter — perhaps the only arena of life in which he displayed genuine vigour and decisiveness — and he spent as many days as possible in the saddle pursuing stag and boar through the forests around Versailles. The hunt was his escape, his one reliable pleasure, and he resented any interruption of it with a petulance that surprised those who saw only his usual placidity. More unusually for a king, he had a passion for locksmithing. He maintained a private forge and workshop at Versailles, where he spent hours working with iron and steel, fashioning locks and mechanical devices under the tutelage of a skilled locksmith named Gamin. The Comte de Saint-Germain is said to have remarked that the king would have been happier as a locksmith than as a monarch — an observation that captured something essential about the man’s misfit with his destiny.

Those who saw him daily at court, like the queen’s first lady-in-waiting, noted his reserve and his difficulty with emotional expression. He was not unfeeling — his correspondence with his ministers shows genuine distress at the suffering of his people — but he lacked the easy warmth that inspires loyalty. His marriage to Marie Antoinette, initially cold and unconsummated for seven years due to a physical condition that was eventually corrected by a minor surgical procedure, had settled into a relationship of mutual if undemonstrative affection by the 1780s. The king was devoted to his children, especially the Dauphin, and his family life at Versailles was the one domain where he seemed genuinely at ease.

To the Swiss commander the Baron de Besenval, who had observed the court for decades, the king appeared as a man constitutionally incapable of the kind of decisive action that monarchy required. Besenval, who commanded the royal troops in Paris and saw the king’s hesitation during the early crises, concluded that Louis possessed every private virtue — honesty, frugality, kindness, modesty — but lacked entirely the public qualities of a ruler: the capacity to command, to threaten, to punish, and, when necessary, to shed blood for the preservation of order. The king shrank from violence with a physical revulsion that Besenval found admirable in a man but disastrous in a king.

Jean-François Marmontel, the historian and moralist who watched the Revolution unfold from a constitutional-monarchist perspective, drew much the same portrait. In his memoirs he described a king whose intentions were uniformly good, whose heart was just, but whose understanding of power was so rudimentary that he could not conceive of men acting from base motives. Louis believed, Marmontel wrote with a mixture of affection and despair, that if he simply explained his difficulties honestly to his people, they would respond with the same goodwill he felt toward them. This naivety — this assumption that the world was as honest as himself — was the fatal flaw that his enemies calculated with precision.

The Crisis 1787–1789

The fiscal crisis that forced the king’s hand was of long gestation. The American War of Independence, in which France had played a decisive role in humbling Britain, had left the treasury exhausted. Year after year, expenditure outstripped revenue, and by 1786 the deficit had reached a point where even the most optimistic ministers could see no way forward without fundamental reform of the tax system. The problem was not merely arithmetic: it was political. The privileged orders — the clergy and the nobility — were exempt from the principal taxes, and any attempt to make them pay provoked immediate resistance from the Parlements, the sovereign courts that registered royal edicts and claimed a constitutional voice in the nation’s affairs.

The king’s first response, characteristically, was to seek counsel. He summoned an Assembly of Notables in February 1787 — a hand-picked body of 144 princes, dukes, magistrates, and clergymen — to approve a package of reforms proposed by his controller-general, Calonne. The centrepiece was a universal land tax that would fall on all landowners regardless of status. The Notables, many of whom were themselves the principal beneficiaries of the tax exemptions, refused. Calonne was dismissed. His successor, Brienne, attempted to force the reforms through the Parlement of Paris, but the Parlement declared that only the Estates-General of the realm — the ancient representative assembly that had not met since 1614 — could consent to new taxes.

The king’s position throughout this struggle was curiously passive. He alternated between yielding to the Parlement and attempting to coerce it, never fully committing to either course. He exiled the Parlement to Troyes, then recalled it; he attempted a lit de justice to browbeat the magistrates, then gave way; he dismissed one minister after another — Turgot, Necker, Calonne, Brienne — each time hoping that a new face would resolve what was fundamentally a crisis of authority rather than of personnel. The court watched his vacillations with growing alarm. Besenval, from his command post in Paris, saw the king’s authority eroding month by month and wrote despairingly of a sovereign who could neither impose his will nor choose a course and stick to it.

Finally, in the summer of 1788, the king capitulated to the logic of events. He agreed to summon the Estates-General, hoping that this ancient body would somehow restore the harmony that his ministers had failed to achieve. The decision was taken with the same good faith that characterised all his public acts. He genuinely believed that the representatives of the three orders — clergy, nobility, and commoners — would join with him in a common effort to restore the kingdom’s finances and address the grievances of the people. What he did not understand — what no one at Versailles fully understood — was that the act of summoning the Estates-General after 175 years of disuse had unleashed forces that no king could control.

The question of how the Estates-General should be constituted became the explosive issue of the winter of 1788–1789. The Third Estate — the commoners — demanded that their representation be doubled to match that of the two privileged orders combined, and that voting should be by head rather than by order. The Parlement of Paris, in a moment of disastrous misjudgement, ruled that the Estates-General must follow the forms of 1614, which would have given the clergy and nobility an automatic majority. The resulting outcry swept through France. Pamphlets, petitions, and demonstrations multiplied. The king, after weeks of indecision, accepted the principle of doubling the Third Estate but left the question of voting method unresolved — a compromise that satisfied no one and merely postponed the confrontation.

In the streets of Paris, the agitation grew steadily through the spring of 1789. The winter had been the harshest in decades; bread prices had risen to levels that the urban poor could not afford. Hungry crowds gathered in the Palais-Royal, the great arcaded square owned by the king’s cousin, the Duke of Orléans, where orators harangued them daily against the aristocracy, the clergy, and the court. The king, still at Versailles, received reports of these disturbances with genuine distress but without any corresponding sense of urgency. He continued to hunt, to work at his forge, to attend mass, to preside over the endless ceremonies of the court, as though the crisis were a temporary misunderstanding that would resolve itself in time.

March–July 1789

The Estates-General opened on 5 May 1789 in a vast hall at Versailles, with all the pomp that the court could muster. The king appeared before the assembled deputies in full regalia, accompanied by the queen, his brothers, and the princes of the blood. To the historian Marmontel, who was present, he seemed at that moment every inch a sovereign — dignified, earnest, visibly moved by the occasion. His speech, which he delivered in a simple, unornamented style that was entirely his own, spoke of his love for his people and his hope that the Estates would bring about a new era of happiness and prosperity. He promised reform, economy, and justice. Many of those present wept.

But within weeks the atmosphere had soured irrevocably. The Third Estate, having waited in vain for the privileged orders to join them in a common assembly, declared itself on 17 June to be the National Assembly — the sole legitimate representative of the French people. The king, pressed by the court party that surrounded the queen, responded by closing the hall where the Third Estate met. The deputies, led by Jean-Sylvain Bailly and the Comte de Mirabeau, reconvened in a nearby indoor tennis court and swore the famous oath: they would not disperse until they had given France a constitution.

The king hesitated. He announced a Royal Session for 23 June at which he would present a reform programme of his own, but his plans were undermined by the growing radicalism of the Third Estate and by the incompetence of his advisers. When the Royal Session was held, the king declared the acts of the Third Estate null and void and ordered the deputies to separate by order. The Third Estate refused to budge. When the master of ceremonies repeated the royal command, Mirabeau uttered his thunderous reply: “Go tell those who sent you that we are here by the will of the people, and that we will not leave except at the point of the bayonet.”

The king, faced with open defiance, did nothing. He could have ordered the troops to clear the hall; he could have dissolved the Estates-General by royal decree; he could have retreated to a provincial stronghold and raised the standard of civil war. Instead, he yielded. On 27 June, he ordered the clergy and nobility to join the National Assembly. The Revolution had its first victory, and the king had revealed the full extent of his weakness.

Through July, the crisis deepened. The king dismissed his popular minister Necker on 11 July — a move dictated by the queen’s circle, which blamed Necker for encouraging the Third Estate. The news of Necker’s dismissal reached Paris on 12 July and ignited an explosion of popular fury. Crowds gathered, orators inflamed them, troops under the Prince de Lambesc charged a gathering in the Tuileries Gardens, and by the morning of 14 July the city was in open insurrection. The mob seized arms from the Invalides and marched on the Bastille, the ancient fortress-prison that symbolised royal authority in the heart of Paris.

The Bastille fell after a brief and confused fight. Its governor, the Marquis de Launay, was murdered along with several of his officers. The heads of the dead were paraded through the streets on pikes. When the news reached Versailles, the king is said to have asked: “Is it a revolt?” The Duke of La Rochefoucauld replied: “No, Sire, it is a revolution.”

The king’s response was characteristic. He appeared before the National Assembly on 15 July, accompanied only by his brothers, and announced that he had ordered the troops to withdraw from Paris and Versailles. The following day, he went to Paris in person — a journey that had been arranged by the revolutionaries to demonstrate his submission. At the Hôtel de Ville, the new mayor, Bailly, presented him with the tricolour cockade — the blue, white, and red badge that had been adopted by the revolutionary militia. The king accepted it and pinned it to his hat. The crowd cheered. He returned to Versailles as a constitutional monarch in name, but the power had already shifted.

In the weeks that followed, the National Assembly worked through the nights of August to dismantle the entire structure of the old regime. On 4 August, the deputies voted to abolish feudalism, seigneurial rights, tithes, and all the privileges of the nobility and clergy. On 26 August, they published the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, which proclaimed the sovereignty of the nation and the natural rights of every man. The king, who had accepted the tricolour under duress, delayed ratifying these decrees. He could not bring himself to approve the abolition of a system that had consecrated the throne for a thousand years. His hesitation would cost him dearly.

October 1789

By the autumn of 1789, Paris was hungry and suspicious. The bread shortages that had plagued the spring had not been resolved; the harvest of 1789 was poor, and grain did not flow freely into the capital. Rumours spread that the court at Versailles was stockpiling flour, that the queen was plotting a counter-revolution, that foreign regiments were being summoned to crush the National Assembly. On 1 October, a banquet was held in the opera house at Versailles for the king’s bodyguards. When the royal family appeared, the officers tore off the tricolour cockades and trampled them underfoot, replacing them with white cockades — the colour of the Bourbon monarchy. News of this incident reached Paris and detonated the smouldering tension.

On the morning of 5 October, a crowd of women — fishwives, market women, working-class mothers — gathered at the Hôtel de Ville, demanding bread. They were joined by men armed with pikes and muskets, and by detachments of the National Guard under the Marquis de La Fayette. The mob, thousands strong, began the twelve-mile march from Paris to Versailles in the rain.

The king was hunting at Meudon when word reached him. He returned to Versailles to find the palace grounds filling with the ragged, angry crowd. Throughout the night, deputations of women came before the king and the Assembly, demanding bread and the royal family’s return to Paris. The king, exhausted and overwhelmed, promised to ratify the August decrees and to provide food for the capital. The crowd was not satisfied.

At dawn on 6 October, a group of the more determined rioters forced their way into the palace. They surged through the marble corridors, killing two bodyguards whose heads they mounted on pikes. The queen, awakened by the uproar, fled barefoot through a secret passage to the king’s apartments. La Fayette finally restored order, but the mob had achieved its aim. The king appeared on the balcony with the queen and the Dauphin. The crowd shouted for them to come to Paris.

Louis XVI agreed. That afternoon, the royal family left Versailles — the great palace that had been the seat of French monarchy since Louis XIV — never to return. The procession wound its way slowly to Paris, the king’s carriage surrounded by a shouting, singing multitude carrying the heads of the murdered guards on pikes. Behind the carriage followed wagons laden with flour from the palace stores. The king and his family were installed in the Tuileries Palace, a dilapidated royal residence that had stood empty for years. They were, to all intents, prisoners.

The Constitutional Monarchy 1789–1791

The two years that followed the October Days were a strange interregnum in which the forms of monarchy were preserved while the substance of power drained away. The king lived at the Tuileries, venturing out occasionally for state ceremonies or for walks in the Tuileries Gardens, where the Parisians could see him and — for a time — still cheer him. He continued to perform the routine duties of a sovereign: he reviewed the National Guard, received ambassadors, affixed his signature to laws passed by the Assembly. But his heart was no longer in the enterprise.

The National Assembly, meanwhile, was engaged in the vast project of remaking France. It reorganized the country into eighty-three departments, abolished the ancient provinces, suppressed the religious orders, nationalized church lands, and — most controversially — imposed the Civil Constitution of the Clergy, which required all priests and bishops to swear allegiance to the state rather than to the Pope. The king’s conscience revolted. He was a devout Catholic, and he could not accept a law that he believed would divide the Church and damn the souls of those who obeyed it. He delayed his assent for months, and when he finally gave it in December 1790, he did so with a heavy heart — a gesture of surrender that satisfied no one and alienated the pious clergy who had remained loyal to him.

Throughout this period, the king maintained a secret correspondence with foreign monarchs — his brother-in-law, the Emperor Leopold II of Austria; the King of Prussia; the King of Sweden — begging them to intervene on his behalf. He had become, in effect, the prisoner of a revolution he could neither accept nor resist, and his only hope was that foreign armies would restore him to his rightful authority.

This double game — public acceptance of the Revolution, private plotting against it — culminated in the disastrous flight to Varennes.

Flight to Varennes

The plan was simple in conception: the royal family would escape from the Tuileries in disguise, travel by carriage to the eastern frontier, and place themselves under the protection of the royalist army commanded by the Marquis de Bouillé. From there, the king would issue a proclamation rejecting the Revolution and calling for the restoration of the monarchy on his own terms. The queen, who had been the driving force behind the plan, organized the details with the help of the Swedish Count Axel von Fersen.

On the night of 20 June 1791, the plan was put into action. The king, disguised as a valet, the queen as a governess, and the Dauphin as a girl — the elaborate disguises that were meant to deceive the sentries — slipped out of the Tuileries one by one and made their way to a waiting carriage on the boulevard. By midnight, the royal family was on the road east, heading for the fortress of Montmédy.

The escape went smoothly for the first twelve hours. The great berlin carriage, drawn by six horses, lumbered through the summer night. The king, who had brought his maps and travel guides, was in good spirits — perhaps the happiest he had been since the fall of the Bastille. But delays accumulated. The carriage lost time at almost every relay station. By the time they reached the small town of Sainte-Menehould, they were hours behind schedule.

There, the postmaster, Jean-Baptiste Drouet, recognized the king from his portrait on an assignat — a revolutionary banknote — and raised the alarm. The carriage rolled on toward Varennes, a walled town on the River Aisne, where the horses were to be changed for the last stage of the journey. But the relay had been sabotaged; the horses were not ready. When the king’s party arrived at Varennes after midnight, they found the bridge blocked and the town in arms. The local officials confronted the king. He identified himself, produced his passport — which identified him as the Baron de Korff, a Russian nobleman — and tried to brazen it out. But the game was up. By morning, the National Guard had arrived from the surrounding towns, and the royal family was escorted back to Paris, a slow, humiliating procession through crowds that had transformed overnight from subjects into captors.

The king’s attempt at escape destroyed what remained of the popular trust in the monarchy. The Jacobin clubs, led by Robespierre and Desmoulins, called for the abolition of the monarchy and the establishment of a republic. The Assembly, still dominated by moderates, temporized: it suspended the king from his functions, declared him temporarily incapacitated, and began to consider the future of the monarchy. For six weeks, Louis and his family remained virtual prisoners in the Tuileries while the Assembly debated.

In the end, the Assembly produced an elaborate fiction: the king had not fled; he had been abducted. He was restored to the throne on 14 September 1791, having sworn an oath to uphold the new Constitution that the Assembly had finally completed. The Constitution of 1791 made France a constitutional monarchy. The king retained the title of King of the French — no longer King of France — and a suspensive veto over legislation, but his powers were sharply circumscribed. He could not dissolve the Assembly, could not declare war or make peace without its consent, could not command the armed forces directly. The experiment of limited monarchy had begun.

The Fall 1792

The constitutional monarchy lasted less than a year. The sources of tension that had produced the Revolution did not disappear with the adoption of the Constitution. The émigrés — the nobles and clergy who had fled France — gathered in the German states and agitated for foreign intervention. The revolutionary factions in Paris, led by the Jacobins and the more radical Cordeliers, denounced the king as a traitor and demanded a republic. The European monarchies, Austria and Prussia foremost, viewed the Revolution with growing alarm and issued increasingly bellicose declarations.

The king was caught between forces he could neither control nor conciliate. In March 1792, under pressure from the Assembly, he accepted a ministry dominated by the Girondins, the faction that favoured war with Austria. The king himself favoured war, hoping that a French defeat would lead to the collapse of the Revolution and his restoration by foreign arms. On 20 April 1792, he appeared before the Assembly and proposed a declaration of war against Austria. The Assembly voted overwhelmingly in favour.

The war went badly from the start. French armies fled before the Austrian advance. The king exercised his veto to block two key measures: the decree that would have summoned a provincial army of twenty thousand National Guards to defend Paris, and the decree that would have exiled the refractory priests who had refused to swear allegiance to the Civil Constitution. His vetoes were constitutionally valid, but they confirmed the popular suspicion that he was in league with France’s enemies. The sections of Paris — the popular assemblies of the city’s forty-eight districts — began to prepare for insurrection.

On 20 June, a crowd of several thousand armed Parisians invaded the Tuileries Palace, demanding that the king recall the decrees he had vetoed. The king received them with what many observers considered remarkable composure. He submitted to their shouts and threats for several hours, drinking to the health of the nation, accepting a red cap of liberty, and allowing himself to be harangued by a succession of popular orators. He refused, however, to revoke his vetoes. The crowd eventually withdrew, having accomplished nothing except to demonstrate the impotence of the monarchy.

The decisive blow came on 10 August 1792. The Paris Commune — the revolutionary municipal government that had formed in the aftermath of the 20 June demonstration — ordered the tocsin to be rung. The sections of Paris mobilized their National Guard battalions. A vast armed column, estimated at twenty thousand men, marched on the Tuileries. The king, at the last moment, abandoned the palace and sought refuge with the Legislative Assembly, which was sitting in the nearby Riding School. He brought his family with him. As they crossed the garden, they could hear the firing begin.

The Swiss Guard, left to defend the palace without clear orders from the king, fought bravely but was overwhelmed. Hundreds of Swiss were massacred in the palace courtyards and the gardens — shot down as they tried to surrender, their bodies stripped and mutilated. The Assembly, sitting in continuous session, heard the roar of the crowd and the crackle of musket fire. Finally, the president announced that the royal family would be lodged in the reporters’ box, where they could hear the debate. The king sat in silence, the Dauphin on his knee, as the Assembly voted to suspend him from his functions. The monarchy was over.

The royal family was transferred to the Temple, a medieval fortress in the eastern quarter of Paris that the revolutionaries had converted into a prison. Here, in the tower where the Knights Templar had once been held, Louis XVI — no longer king, now simply Louis Capet — began his final confinement.

The Trial and Execution

The National Convention, which succeeded the Legislative Assembly in September 1792, proclaimed the Republic on 21 September. The question of what to do with the former king hung over its proceedings from the very first sessions. The Jacobins, led by Robespierre and Saint-Just, demanded his immediate trial and execution. The Girondins, more cautious, argued that the inviolability of the king’s person was guaranteed by the Constitution of 1791 and that he should be deposed and exiled rather than tried. The debate raged for weeks.

In November, a remarkable discovery was made: a secret iron chest in the Tuileries, which the king had built for his locksmithing work — the very hobby that had marked him as a man of the people — was found to contain a cache of incriminating documents. The correspondence revealed the king’s secret dealings with foreign powers, his denunciations of the Revolution in his private letters, and his plans to restore his absolute authority by force of arms. For the Jacobins, this was the proof they needed. The king was a traitor who had conspired against the nation while pretending to accept its laws.

The trial began on 11 December 1792, when Louis was brought before the Convention for the first time. He was questioned for hours by Bertrand Barère, the president of the Convention, in a proceeding that was as much political theatre as judicial process. The king answered with composure. He denied the authenticity of some of the documents, explained others as expressions of his true feelings at a time when he was not free, and refused to recognize the Convention’s authority to try him. “I was your king,” he told them, “and you have no right to judge me.”

The king’s defence was conducted by three lawyers: Chrétien de Malesherbes, a former minister who had once been the king’s censor — and had, ironically, helped to disseminate the Enlightenment writings that had undermined the monarchy — along with Guillaume-Chrétien de Lamoignon de Malesherbes and Raymond Desèze, the latter a renowned orator from Bordeaux. Desèze delivered the principal defence on 26 December. It was a long, eloquent, and ultimately futile speech. He argued that the king could not be tried under the Constitution of 1791, which had declared him inviolable; that the Convention had no power to act as both prosecutor and judge; and that Louis had never knowingly betrayed a nation that he had always loved.

The Convention was not moved. On 15 January 1793, the deputies voted on three questions. First, was Louis Capet guilty of conspiracy against the public liberty and of attacks against the general security of the state? The vote was overwhelming: 693 in favour, none against (with some abstentions). Second, should the verdict be submitted to the people for ratification? The Girondins had proposed a referendum, hoping to save the king through popular mercy. The Convention voted it down, 424 to 287. Third, what punishment should be inflicted? This was the roll-call vote that would determine the king’s fate.

For three days, from 16 to 18 January, each of the 721 deputies mounted the tribune in alphabetical order and pronounced his verdict. It was a harrowing spectacle. Some deputies wept as they voted. Others, notably the Duke of Orléans — the king’s own cousin, who had taken the revolutionary name Philippe Égalité — voted for death. In the end, 361 deputies voted for death without conditions, 334 for death with a stay or for imprisonment, and 26 for various other penalties. The majority was a single vote — 361 to 360 — when the ambiguous votes were counted. The king of France was condemned to die.

On Sunday, 20 January, the Convention’s delegation came to the Temple to inform the king of his sentence. He received the news with greater calm than many of those who brought it. He asked for a three-day reprieve to prepare his soul; it was denied. He requested permission to see his family; this was granted. That evening, he met with his wife, his sister, and his two children for the last time. The interview lasted an hour and three-quarters. When it was over, the queen and the children were led away. The king spent the night in prayer and confession with the Abbé Edgeworth de Firmont, an Irish priest who had been smuggled into the Temple to administer the last rites.

The morning of 21 January 1793 was cold and grey. The king was awakened at five o’clock. He heard mass, received communion, and prepared himself for death. At around eight o’clock, the guards arrived. The king handed them his will, a packet of personal effects for his family, and his wedding ring. He was taken to a closed green carriage, which rolled slowly through the silent, heavily guarded streets to the Place de la Révolution — today’s Place de la Concorde — where the guillotine had been erected.

The carriage halted at the foot of the scaffold. The king emerged, pale but composed. He attempted to address the crowd, shouting in a voice that carried across the vast square: “I die innocent of all the crimes of which I am accused. I pardon the authors of my death, and I pray to God that the blood you are about to shed may never fall upon France…” The drummers, under orders to drown out any speech, began to beat. The king was seized by the executioner, Charles-Henri Sanson, and his assistants. His coat was removed, his hands were tied behind his back, his hair was cut. For a moment, he resisted — a brief, instinctive struggle — but then he submitted. He was strapped to the plank and thrust under the blade.

The guillotine fell. Sanson lifted the severed head by the hair and displayed it to the crowd. The onlookers — many of whom had been kept at a distance behind a line of soldiers — erupted in shouts of “Vive la République!” The head of Louis XVI, the seventy-seventh king of France in the direct Capetian line, rolled into the basket.

Gouverneur Morris, who had remained in Paris through the darkest months of the Revolution, recorded the event in his diary with characteristic bluntness. He had seen the king often, had watched him decline from a good-natured if inept sovereign to a prisoner who could not save himself. He had warned of the coming catastrophe and had been ignored. Now, he noted simply the fact of the execution — a single line in his journal for 21 January 1793 — and recorded that the news had reached the American legation with the speed of a spreading stain. The monarchy had ended not with a battle but with a single swing of the blade, and the king who had been too weak for his throne had faced the scaffold with a dignity that had eluded him in life.
