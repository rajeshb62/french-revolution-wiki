Jean-François Marmontel

He was born in 1723, in the last years of the Regency, in a small town in the Massif Central called Bort-les-Orgues, where his father was a poor draper with thirteen children. The landscape of his childhood — the black volcanic cliffs of the Dordogne valley, the organ-pipe basalt formations that gave the town its name — stayed with him for the rest of his life, and he wrote about it in the opening pages of his memoirs with the precision of a man who had spent sixty years away from it and had never stopped seeing it in his mind. He was the eleventh child, and the family was too poor to feed him; his mother, who emerges from his account as the decisive figure of his early life, managed to get him into the Jesuit college of Mauriac on a charity placement, because the Jesuits ran a system of free education for promising boys from the provincial poor. He was eleven years old and he walked there barefoot.

The Jesuit Formation

The college at Mauriac was a rough school by the standards of the time: the boys slept in dormitories with broken windows, ate soup made from boiled chestnuts in winter, and were beaten regularly by the régents. But the education was real. Marmontel learned Latin verse composition, rhetoric, and philosophy in the classical Jesuit curriculum, and he discovered that he had a facility for writing that set him apart from the other boys. He was not a naturally gifted student — he admits in his memoirs that his memory was poor and that he had to compensate with sheer stubbornness — but he had a talent for imitation and adaptation that made his verses admired by his teachers.

His father died when Marmontel was still an adolescent. He describes the scene in the memoirs: the boy, seventeen years old, returning from a walk to find his father dead in the house, and collapsing into a fever that nearly killed him. The death left the family destitute. Marmontel was taken out of school and placed with a merchant in Clermont-Ferrand — a commercial apprenticeship that lasted exactly as long as it took him to realise he would rather starve than spend his life counting bolts of cloth. He ran away back to college, announced that he had a religious vocation, and took minor orders.

For several years he drifted through a series of positions typical of the provincial cleric-intellectual: preceptor in the château of Linars, where he taught the children of a noble family and wrote Latin verses in praise of the local seigneur; a brief stay at the seminary of Limoges, where he discovered that the directors were more interested in his literary abilities than in his piety; and a return to the Jesuits in Toulouse, where he was offered the chance to enter the novitiate. He consulted his mother by letter. She wrote back: do not become a Jesuit, you are not made for it. It was the first of several moments in his life when he followed her judgment and was saved by it.

In Toulouse he began to make his way as a man of letters. He competed in the Floral Games — the ancient academy of Toulouse that awarded prizes for poetry — and won. Voltaire heard of him through a mutual acquaintance and wrote him a letter of encouragement. The letter, which Marmontel kept all his life, was the turning point. A young man from the provinces, with no family connections and no money, had been noticed by the most famous writer in Europe. In 1745, at the age of twenty-two, he took the road to Paris.

Paris and Voltaire

He arrived in Paris with almost nothing — a few coins, a letter of introduction to Voltaire, and the manuscript of a tragedy. Voltaire received him in the manner for which he was famous to promising young writers: with immediate warmth, practical advice, and the offer of a room in his own house. Marmontel became part of Voltaire’s circle at a time when Voltaire was at the height of his fame but not yet at the height of his fortune — the years between the success of Mahomet and the move to Ferney. Voltaire read his tragedy, told him it was not good enough, and set him to work writing verse epistles and literary criticism.

The mentorship was the making of him. Voltaire introduced him to everyone who mattered in Paris: the salons of Madame de Tencin, the theatre world of the Comédie-Française, the philosophical circle of d’Alembert and Diderot that was then forming around the Encyclopédie. Marmontel absorbed the intellectual culture of the French Enlightenment at its source. He was not a revolutionary thinker — his mind was too moderate, too classical, too attached to order to embrace the radical implications of the philosophy he was learning — but he was the perfect literary craftsman of the movement: capable of turning any idea into elegant prose, any philosophical argument into a readable story.

His first major success came in 1748 with Denys le tyran, a tragedy that was performed at the Comédie-Française and ran for several months. The play was conventional — classical verse, moral theme, the rehabilitation of a tyrant through the power of clemency — but it hit the public taste at exactly the right moment. Marmontel followed it with Aristomène (1749) and Cléopâtre (1750), both successful, and for a few years he was the most performed tragic playwright in Paris after Voltaire himself.

The theatre brought him into the world that would define his life: the actresses, the patrons, the courtiers, and the salonnières who controlled literary reputation in France. He had an affair with Marie Verrière, an actress who was the mistress of the Maréchal de Saxe; the Maréchal discovered it and threatened to have him killed, an episode that left Marmontel terrified and discreet for the rest of his life. He became a regular at the salon of Madame de Tencin, the former nun turned political fixer whose Thursday dinners were the crossroads of literary and ministerial France. It was at Madame de Tencin’s that he discovered the secret fireplace through which she listened to the conversations of her guests — a discovery that did not endear him to her.

The Salon Years

Through the 1750s and 1760s, Marmontel moved from the theatre into the broader world of letters. He contributed articles on literary subjects to the Encyclopédie — not the great philosophical articles of Diderot or d’Alembert, but the competent, clear, readable entries on poetry, tragedy, and rhetoric that helped make the work accessible. He was appointed secretary of the Bâtiments du Roi under the Marquis de Marigny, Madame de Pompadour’s brother, which gave him a position at Versailles and a comfortable income. He moved between worlds with an ease that few of his contemporaries managed: the court at Versailles in the morning, the philosophe salons in the evening, the theatre at night.

Madame de Pompadour liked him. She found him useful, polite, and without the prickly vanity that afflicted so many of the literary men she patronised. He wrote libretti for the court opera, occasional verses for the king’s entertainments, and a steady stream of literary criticism. In 1758, when the Mercure de France — the official literary journal of the kingdom — needed a new editor, the position was offered to him. He transformed it from a dry collection of official announcements into a real literary review, commissioning work from the best young writers he could find and building it into the most widely read periodical in France.

His Contes moraux, published in the Mercure and then collected in book form, made him famous across Europe. These were short, elegant tales in the classical French tradition — La Fontaine updated for the eighteenth century — each with a moral point lightly carried by the narrative. They were translated into every European language, read by Catherine the Great in Russia, Frederick the Great in Prussia, and every literate person between them. Marmontel became the representative French man of letters of his generation: not a genius like Voltaire or Diderot, but the kind of writer who defined the standard of good taste for an entire age.

In 1763, he published Bélisaire, a philosophical novel set in the Byzantine Empire that used the story of the blinded general Belisarius to advance arguments for religious toleration and enlightened monarchy. The Sorbonne condemned it for its heretical propositions — fifteen specific propositions were extracted and denounced — and the Archbishop of Paris, Christophe de Beaumont, threatened him with excommunication. Marmontel defended himself with a mixture of deference and firmness that was characteristic of his entire career: he would not retract the book, but he would not attack his persecutors either. The affair made him a hero to the philosophe party without making him a martyr; he had the rare ability to be both principled and prudent.

The Académie and the Historian

In 1763, the same year Bélisaire appeared, Marmontel was elected to the Académie Française. The election was contested — there were factions who thought him too much a philosophe and others who thought him too much a courtier — but he was admitted, and the Académie became the centre of his institutional life for the next thirty years. He was also appointed Historiographer of France, a position that gave him access to the state archives and a mandate to write the history of the reign of Louis XV.

The history was never written — the material was too vast, the Revolution too disruptive, the task too daunting — but the preparation for it gave Marmontel an intimate knowledge of French political life at the highest level. He read the memoirs of Saint-Simon in manuscript, consulted the ministers who had served under Louis XV, and accumulated the documentary base that would later make his account of the Revolution so well-informed.

In 1783, he was elected Secrétaire Perpétuel of the Académie Française — the permanent secretary, the most senior administrative position in the institution, responsible for its records, its correspondence, and its public face. It was the summit of a literary career in the ancien régime. Marmontel delivered the éloges of deceased academicians in the annual public sessions, and his speeches became literary events in their own right — the synthesis of the classical French style at its most polished, celebrating the lives of writers and statesmen whose world was about to end.

The Revolution: Electoral Politics

When the Estates-General were convoked in 1788, Marmontel was sixty-five years old. He had spent his entire life in the service of the monarchy and the institutions of the ancien régime, but he was not a reactionary. He believed in constitutional reform, in the principles of the Enlightenment, in the possibility of a rational and ordered liberty that would preserve the monarchy while transforming the state. In the spring of 1789 he became a member of the primary assembly of the district of the Feuillants — the ancient monastery in Paris where the electors of the Third Estate met — and then of the electoral assembly that chose the deputies for Paris.

The electoral assembly of Paris in 1789 was one of the most extraordinary political gatherings in French history: six hundred men from the Third Estate of the capital, meeting not in a royal building but in a former monastery, electing deputies who would go to Versailles to remake the kingdom. Marmontel sat among lawyers, merchants, academics, and men of property, many of whom had never participated in politics before. He describes the scene in his memoirs with a mixture of hope and unease: the eloquence of the young advocates, the energy of the debates, the growing influence of men like Adrien Duport who were already thinking beyond constitutional monarchy to something more radical.

He stood for election himself. The assembly needed to fill forty seats for the Paris deputation. On the first ballot, he placed high enough to be in contention. On the second, he was defeated — the seat went to Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès, the man who had just written What is the Third Estate? and whose name was on every tongue in France. Marmontel withdrew from the contest with dignity. He was too old, too moderate, too identified with the institutions of the old world to command the confidence of a body that was inventing a new one.

The Revolution: Witness

From the summer of 1789 onward, Marmontel watched the Revolution unfold from the best possible vantage point — and from the worst. He was a witness to the great events: the fall of the Bastille reached him through the streets of Paris; the October Days he saw from his house near the Tuileries; the king’s flight to Varennes, the storming of the Tuileries on 10 August 1792, the September Massacres — each event registered in his mind with the accuracy of a trained observer. But he was also a victim of the Revolution, and the two perspectives are interwoven in his account in a way that gives his memoirs their peculiar tension.

He had been a man of the ancien régime — a pensioner of the crown, a member of the Académie, a beneficiary of the system of patronage and privilege that the Revolution was dismantling. The loss of the Mercure de France deprived him of his principal income. The suppression of the academies in 1793 deprived him of his institutional identity. The law of suspects, the proscription of ex-nobles, and the attacks on ex-priests touched his family directly: his brother-in-law was arrested, his friends were executed, his world was systematically destroyed.

He did not flee France. He stayed in Paris through the first years of the Revolution, moving between his house in the city and a small property at Saint-Brice, north of Paris, where he tried to keep his head down and his family safe. In 1793, as the Terror deepened, he withdrew more completely. He had been proscribed by name as a former noble — not by blood, but by association with the Académie and the court — and he lived in daily expectation of arrest.

The Memoirist in Retirement

The memoirs were written in this period of hiding and waiting. Marmontel conceived them as a testament for his children — Mémoires d’un père pour servir à l’instruction de ses enfans — a record of a world that had vanished and a life that had crossed the entire span of the Enlightenment, from the provincial poverty of Bort to the heights of the Académie Française, from the reign of Louis XV to the Terror. He wrote in the measured, classical prose that had been his trademark for fifty years, but the tone is different from his earlier work: more reflective, more melancholy, more aware of the ironies of a life that had been lived at the centre of events and had ended in impotent observation.

The memoir is not a political tract. Marmontel was a constitutional monarchist to the end, but his account of the Revolution is less concerned with justifying his politics than with recording what he saw. He gives extended portraits of the figures he knew: Louis XVI, whom he describes with a sympathy that is all the more striking for being unillusioned; Necker, whose miscalculations he analyses without rancour; Mirabeau, whose genius he recognised and whose corruption he deplored; the deputies, the ministers, the king’s councillors, the revolutionaries, all drawn from a lifetime of observation in the salons and the corridors of power.

The most remarkable section of the memoirs is the account of the fall of the Bastille as told to him by Élie, one of the vainqueurs de la Bastille — the insurgents who stormed the fortress on 14 July 1789. Marmontel met Élie in the days after the event and took down his narrative directly. It is one of the very few eyewitness accounts from the insurgent side, preserved within the memoirs of a moderate constitutionalist who could not have been more different from the men who took the Bastille, but who had the historian’s instinct to record what he heard.

The Last Years

In 1795, with the Terror over and the Thermidorian Reaction in progress, Marmontel moved his family to Abbeville, in Picardy, where he had inherited a small property from his wife’s family. He lived there in retirement, completing the memoirs and writing educational works for his children: a course in grammar, a course in logic, a course in metaphysics and morality — the curriculum of the Enlightenment reduced to its essentials for the private instruction of one family.

He died on 31 December 1799, the last day of the eighteenth century, at his house in Abbeville. The coincidence was not lost on those who knew him. He had been born in 1723, in the France of Louis XV, when the philosophes were still a scattered group of writers without influence, when the Encyclopédie did not exist, when the monarchy seemed as eternal as the seasons. He died in 1799, on the eve of the Consulate, having seen the monarchy fall, the republic rise and fall, the Terror consume his friends, and the institutions of his life — the Académie, the Mercure, the salons, the system of royal patronage — swept away by forces that he had helped to set in motion without knowing what they were.

The memoirs were published posthumously in 1804. They became one of the most-read accounts of the Revolution from the moderate constitutionalist perspective, valued as much for their portrait of the eighteenth-century literary world as for their account of the revolutionary years. Marmontel had been the perfect representative of his age — not its greatest writer, not its boldest thinker, but the man whose life trajectory traced the curve of the French Enlightenment from its origins to its collapse, and who had the sense, in his final years, to write it all down.
