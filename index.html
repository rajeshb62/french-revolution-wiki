The Narrative

The French Revolution of 1789 broke upon a monarchy already in crisis. The fiscal exhaustion following the American War of Independence forced louis-xvi to summon the Estates-General for the first time since 1614, setting in motion a collision between the privileged orders of the Ancien Régime, the liberal aristocracy of the philosophe salons, and the Third Estate — the commoners who carried the weight of the kingdom’s taxation. When the frustrated deputies declared themselves a National Assembly and swore the Tennis Court Oath, and when the people of Paris answered by storming the Bastille on 14 July 1789, the Revolution had begun not as a single event but as a process that would overturn every institution of French society.

The first phase — the Revolution of 1789 — swept away feudal privileges, proclaimed the Rights of Man, and attempted to reconcile a constitutional monarchy with popular sovereignty. The October Days brought the king and the Assembly to Paris; the Civil Constitution of the Clergy divided the nation; and the Flight to Varennes in June 1791 destroyed the illusion of a reconciled king and revolution. The Legislative Assembly that followed gave way in August 1792 to the insurrection that overthrew the monarchy itself.

The Republic proclaimed in September 1792 was born in crisis — war with Austria and Prussia, the September Massacres in the prisons of Paris, and the trial and execution of the king in January 1793. The Convention, divided between the Girondins and the Mountain, lurched toward the Terror: a year of revolutionary tribunals, the Committee of Public Safety, and the execution of 16,000 presumed enemies of the state. The proscription of the Girondins in June 1793, the assassination of Marat, the coup of 9 Thermidor that brought down Robespierre in July 1794 — each turn consumed its predecessors.

The Directory that followed attempted a middle course between royalism and Jacobinism, but the five years from 1795 to 1799 were a chronicle of coups, financial collapse, and the steady rise of a young general whose Italian campaigns of 1796–97 had already made him the most famous soldier in Europe. The émigrés plotted in exile, the Chouans rose in the West, and the British navy — under Nelson at the Nile and Copenhagen — destroyed the fleets of France and her allies.

On 18 Brumaire 1799, Bonaparte seized power. The Consulate gave way to the Empire in 1804; the Continental System attempted to starve Britain into submission; the Trafalgar campaign of 1805 destroyed the Franco-Spanish fleet and confirmed British command of the seas — a command that Mahan argued forced Napoleon to fight Austerlitz before winter closed the campaigning season. From the Peace of Amiens (1802) to the invasion of Russia (1812), Napoleon remade the map of Europe and then watched it unravel: the Spanish ulcer, the disaster in Russia, the War of the Sixth Coalition, and finally Waterloo in 1815.
