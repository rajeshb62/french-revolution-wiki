The Trial of Louis XVI

I. The Iron Cabinet

The King had been a prisoner in the Temple since the tenth of August. For four months, the National Convention — the new assembly elected by universal manhood suffrage after the fall of the monarchy — had governed France in his name without him. The question of what to do with Louis XVI hung over every debate, every decree, every act of the revolutionary government. The Girondins, who dominated the Convention in its early months, wished to postpone the decision; the Mountain demanded that it be taken at once. And then, in November of 1792, the question was decided for them.

On the twentieth of November, Roland, the Minister of the Interior, received word that a locksmith named François Gamain had a secret to reveal. Gamain had been employed by the King for years — he had installed the famous iron lock on the door of the King’s private cabinet at the Tuileries, the cabinet where Louis worked on his maps and his books, the room where he kept his private papers. Gamain now led the commissioners of the Convention to the King’s chamber in the Tuileries, and there, behind a panel of wainscoting, behind a sliding iron plate worked by a secret spring, he revealed an iron cabinet — the armoire de fer — built into the wall. Within it were stacked bundles of letters, sealed and unsealed, the King’s secret correspondence with Mirabeau, with the émigré princes, with the courts of Europe, with the generals who had commanded his armies.

The discovery was a thunderbolt. The papers proved what the Jacobins had always alleged and what the Girondins had hesitated to affirm — that the King had been in secret communication with the enemies of France from the very beginning of the Revolution. There were letters to the Marquis de Bouillé, the commander who had arranged the flight to Varennes. There were letters to the émigré princes urging them to hasten the invasion of France. There were letters to the courts of Austria and Prussia, letters that begged for armed intervention, letters that promised to make common cause with the invaders against his own people. The mask of the constitutional monarch, of the King who had accepted the Revolution, fell away at a stroke. Louis XVI stood revealed not as a reluctant prisoner of events but as a conspirator at the heart of the counter-revolution.

Roland, who had supervised the seizure of the papers, sent an account of the discovery to the Convention, and the effect was immediate. The Convention ordered that all the papers be examined, that the King be interrogated, that the proofs of his treachery be laid before the nation. From that moment, the trial of Louis XVI was not a question of whether but of how.

II. The Question of Jurisdiction

The discovery of the iron cabinet transformed the political landscape. Until then, the Girondins had hoped to avoid a trial altogether, or at least to postpone it until the war was won and passions had cooled. The Mountain, led by Robespierre, Saint-Just, and Danton, had demanded that the King be judged immediately and without appeal. But the iron cabinet gave the Mountain the evidence it needed to force the issue.

The first debate was over jurisdiction. Could the Convention, which was both the legislative body and the sovereign power of the nation, sit in judgment over the King? The Constitution of 1791 had declared the person of the King inviolable and sacred; by its terms, Louis could not be tried for any act committed during his constitutional reign. But the Mountain argued that the Constitution was dead — the King had destroyed it by his flight to Varennes, by his secret correspondence with foreign powers, by his conspiracy against the nation. The insurrection of the tenth of August had annulled the Constitution and established a new order based on the sovereignty of the people. The King could no longer claim the protection of a compact he himself had broken.

Saint-Just, speaking for the Mountain, laid out the revolutionary logic with cold precision. The King was not a citizen who could be tried under the laws; he was the enemy of the people, a stranger to the social compact. The Revolution had been founded on the insurrection of the people against the tyrant; to judge the King by the ordinary forms of justice was to deny the revolutionary character of the act that had overthrown him. “Louis was not a citizen,” Saint-Just declared from the tribune. “He was a king. The very act of judging him implies that he may be condemned or acquitted. But Louis must be judged as an enemy; he must be judged not by the laws of the civil order but by the law of the people, which is the law of necessity. A king who has conspired against his people has already condemned himself. The people have already judged him in their hearts by the insurrection of the tenth of August. It remains only for the Convention to pronounce the sentence that the people have already passed.”

The Girondins recoiled from this argument. Vergniaud, the greatest orator of the Gironde, rose to defend the forms of justice. If the Revolution was to establish a republic founded on law, he argued, then it must act according to law. The King could not be condemned without a trial; he could not be executed without a judgment. The Girondins proposed that the Convention should hear the King’s defence, that the King should be allowed counsel, that the due forms of procedure should be observed. And on one point they insisted above all: the sentence of the Convention should be submitted to the people — to the primary assemblies of France — for ratification. The appeal to the people, the appel au peuple, was the Girondin solution to the dilemma. It would give the sentence legitimacy; it would remove the responsibility from the shoulders of the deputies; it would, perhaps, save the King’s life.

But the Mountain saw in the appeal to the people a trap. It would delay the trial, divide the nation, and give the royalists time to rally. It would, as Robespierre argued, substitute the uncertain voice of the provinces for the certain voice of the Revolution. The Revolution had been made in Paris, by the people of Paris, for the people of France. To appeal to the primary assemblies was to invite civil war. The debate raged for weeks, and the Mountain prevailed. The Convention declared itself competent to judge the King; it declared that the King was not inviolable; it declared that the trial would proceed before the Convention alone, with no appeal to the people.

III. The King Before the Convention

On the eleventh of December, Louis Capet — for so he was now called, stripped of his royal titles — was brought from the Temple to the hall of the Convention. The carriage passed through the silent streets of Paris, lined with troops. The King was calm. He had chosen his defenders: the Count de Malesherbes, the venerable magistrate who had once been his minister; François-Denis Tronchet, the jurist who had defended the Parlements; and Raymond de Sèze, the eloquent young advocate who had made his reputation at the bar of Bordeaux. They had worked through the night preparing the defence, reading the evidence, composing the arguments that would save the King’s life.

Louis entered the hall with a firm step. He wore a brown coat, a white waistcoat, black breeches. He stood before the bar of the Convention, between his guards, and listened while the President, Bertrand Barère, read the act of accusation — a catalogue of the crimes alleged against him: the veto of the decrees, the dismissal of the Girondin ministers, the calling of foreign troops to French soil, the massacre of the Champ de Mars, the flight to Varennes, the secret correspondence with the enemies of France. The charges were read by sections, and after each section, the King was asked to answer.

He answered with the same calm that had marked his bearing throughout. He denied the secret correspondence with foreign powers; he denied that he had called foreign armies into France; he denied that he had conspired against the nation. To the charge that he had allowed the blood of the tenth of August to be shed, he replied that he had done everything in his power to prevent it. His voice was low but clear; he did not falter; he did not show fear. When the President pressed him on the iron cabinet, on the letters found within it, on the proof of his correspondence with the émigrés, the King answered that he had forgotten the details, that he did not recall the letters, that the papers might have been forged or planted. It was a weak defence against the mountain of evidence, but the King maintained it with unbroken composure.

The hearing lasted for hours. The King was then taken back to the Temple, and the Convention adjourned. The trial had begun.

On the twenty-sixth of December, the King appeared before the Convention for the second and last time. His counsel, de Sèze, rose to speak for him. The young advocate delivered a defence that was at once legal and emotional, a plea for the life of a man whose only crime, he argued, was the misfortune of having been a king. He argued the inviolability of the King under the Constitution of 1791; he argued that the King had never voluntarily conspired against the nation; he argued that the Revolution itself had been the work of the King, who had summoned the Estates-General, who had surrendered his absolute power, who had accepted the Constitution. He concluded with a passage that moved even his enemies to silence: “I speak for a man who cannot speak for himself, a man who is accused before you, a man whose life is in your hands. I ask you not to judge him as a king, but as a man — a man who has been your King, who has loved his people, who has done nothing but what he believed to be right. I ask you to remember that you are men, that you are Frenchmen, that you are the representatives of a nation that has always been generous. I ask you to spare the life of Louis XVI.”

The King then spoke for himself. He said that he had no reproaches to make; he had done only what he believed to be right; he had never conspired against his people; he died — if he must die — innocent of the charges laid against him. He asked the Convention to consider the fate of his family, to protect his wife and children, to show them the mercy that the Revolution claimed for itself. His voice was steady; his eyes were dry. When he had finished, he was led back to the Temple, and the Convention prepared to judge him.

IV. The Debate

The debate on the King’s fate began on the first of January, 1793, and continued without interruption for fifteen days. It was the greatest debate of the Revolution — a conflict between the two conceptions of justice, the two visions of the Republic, the two irreconcilable factions that would soon tear each other apart.

The Girondins argued for mercy. Vergniaud, whose eloquence had overthrown the monarchy, now pleaded for the life of the fallen King. He was torn, as many of the Girondins were, between their revolutionary principles and their horror at the act they were about to commit. He argued that the Republic could afford to be generous; that the execution of the King would make him a martyr; that it would unite Europe against France; that it would close the door to any reconciliation with the other Powers. “You have condemned the King,” Vergniaud said. “Do not condemn the Republic. The execution of the King will be a crime that will stain the Revolution forever. It will be an act of vengeance, not of justice. It will be the death of the Revolution itself.”

The Mountain answered with fury. Robespierre, who had never forgiven the King for the massacre of the Champ de Mars, who had always believed that the Revolution could not be safe while a Bourbon lived, mounted the tribune to deliver the speech that would seal the King’s fate. “Louis must die so that the Republic may live,” he declared. The words were carried across the hall, across the galleries, across the city, and across the world. “If the King is acquitted, if the King is spared, then the Revolution is condemned. The King is not a man; he is a principle. He is the principle of monarchy, the principle of tyranny, the principle of the oppression of the people. To spare him is to betray the Revolution. To spare him is to confess that the people were wrong to rise on the tenth of August. To spare him is to admit that the monarchy might return, that the Bourbons might reclaim their throne, that the Revolution was nothing but a passing madness. Louis must die so that the Republic may live.”

Saint-Just, who had been the first to demand the King’s death, followed Robespierre with an even sterner speech. He argued that the King’s trial was not a trial at all, but a political act. The forms of justice had been observed; the King had been heard; his defence had been made. Now the Convention must act. Delay was weakness; mercy was treason. “There is no middle path,” Saint-Just said. “The King must be condemned, or the Republic must perish.”

The debate grew more bitter as the days passed. The Girondins proposed a series of amendments — that the sentence should be ratified by the primary assemblies, that the King should be imprisoned until the end of the war, that the sentence should be commuted to banishment. Each proposal was voted down by the Mountain, which held the support of the Parisian deputies and the pressure of the galleries. The Commune of Paris, the Jacobin clubs, the sections — all demanded the King’s death. The Girondins, who had once been the masters of the Assembly, now found themselves isolated, outnumbered, and afraid.

V. The Roll-Call Vote

On the fifteenth of January, the Convention voted on three questions. The first: Is Louis Capet guilty of conspiracy against the public liberty and the general safety of the state? The vote was unanimous: six hundred and ninety-three deputies answered yes. The second: Shall the sentence of the Convention be submitted to the primary assemblies for ratification? The Girondins had staked everything on this question; its defeat was their defeat. By four hundred and twenty-four votes against two hundred and eighty-three, the appeal to the people was rejected. The third question remained: What sentence shall be pronounced against Louis Capet?

The vote on the sentence began on the sixteenth of January and continued through the night and into the following day. The roll was called by alphabetical order, and each deputy, as his name was called, mounted the tribune, faced the Convention, and pronounced his sentence aloud, for the galleries, for the city, for history, to hear. It was the most terrible moment of the Revolution: seven hundred and twenty-one men, one by one, answering the question that would decide whether a king would live or die.

The voting was agonizing. Some deputies spoke for hours, explaining their reasons, justifying their choice, trying to reconcile their conscience with their politics. Others passed in silence, too moved to speak. The galleries were packed with spectators, with Jacobins, with the desperate agents of the Commune, who watched each deputy as he voted and noted whose voice faltered, whose hand trembled, whose eye met the gaze of the crowd. The Mountain voted for death without conditions — “Death,” they said, “pure and simple.” The Girondins voted for imprisonment, for banishment, for death with a reprieve. Some voted for death but with an appeal to the people. Some, like the Duke of Orléans — now called Philippe-Égalité — voted for the death of his cousin with a coldness that would later be remembered against him. “Regicide,” the galleries cried as he descended from the tribune, and Philippe-Égalité — whose own son, the future Louis-Philippe, sat in the hall — returned to his seat without a word.

The voting continued through the night. The candles burned low; the deputies grew hoarse; the tension was unbearable. Morris, the American Minister, waited in his house for word of the result. He had done what he could for the King — had spoken to his ministers, had written to his government, had urged the French to spare the life of a man who was, whatever his faults, the legitimate sovereign of France. But he knew that his influence was limited, and that the forces of revolution were stronger than any diplomacy.

At last, at two in the morning of the seventeenth of January, the President rose to announce the result. The sentence was death. By three hundred and sixty-one votes against three hundred and sixty — a majority of one — the Convention had condemned Louis Capet to die.

The margin was so narrow that a single deputy, by changing his vote, could have saved the King. But no deputy changed his vote. The Revolution had spoken.

VI. The King’s Farewell

The King received the news of his sentence with the same calm he had shown throughout the trial. He asked for a respite of three days; the Convention granted him one. He asked to see his family; the Convention granted that too. He asked for a priest; the Convention sent him the Abbé Edgeworth de Firmont, an Irish priest who had been recommended to him by his uncle, the Count de Provence. Edgeworth was a royalist, a man of courage and faith, and he stayed with the King to the end.

On the evening of the twentieth of January, the King was brought down from his tower to meet his family for the last time. The Queen, the Princess Elisabeth, the Dauphin, and Madame Royale were waiting for him. The King held his son in his arms; he embraced his wife; he spoke to them of the future, of the need for forgiveness, of the hope that they would one day be reunited. The Queen, who had been brave for months, broke down. She clung to the King’s hand; she begged to be allowed to die with him. The King gently refused. He told her that she must live for the children, that she must be strong, that she must bear whatever fate the Revolution had in store for her. He gave his son his blessing. He promised his daughter that he would not forget her. And then he was led back to his tower, and the door was closed, and the royal family of France was separated forever.

The King passed the night in prayer. Edgeworth remained with him, confessed him, gave him the last communion. The King wrote a letter to his wife and children — a letter that would not reach them for years, that would be intercepted by the Commune and kept in the archives of the Revolution. He wrote of his love for them, of his innocence, of his faith in God. He forgave his enemies. He asked his wife to forgive him for his faults and his failings. He commended them to the mercy of Heaven.

At dawn, he slept for a few hours. He woke at five, heard mass, and prepared himself for what was to come. The execution had been announced for nine o’clock.

VII. The Twenty-First of January

The morning of the twenty-first of January, 1793, was cold and grey. A thin frost covered the streets of Paris. The drums beat the générale before dawn; the troops were under arms; the forty thousand men of the National Guard lined the route from the Temple to the Place de la Révolution. The cannons were in place at the bridges and the squares. The sections were in arms. The city was a city under siege.

At eight o’clock, the King’s carriage arrived at the Temple. It was a green carriage — the carriage of the mayor of Paris — with two postilions and four horses. The King was brought down from the tower, dressed in a brown coat, a white waistcoat, grey breeches, and black shoes. His hair was unpowdered; he wore a simple cravat. He was calm, almost serene. Edgeworth accompanied him. The carriage set out through the silent streets, through the crowds that lined the boulevards, through the forest of bayonets and the silence of a people who had come to watch a king die.

The crowd was vast but quiet. Some called out curses; some wept; most watched in silence. The King sat in the carriage, reading the Office of the Dead from the priest’s breviary. He looked out at the people he had ruled, at the city he had loved, at the world he was leaving, and he said nothing. Edgeworth, who sat beside him, asked him if he was afraid. The King smiled and shook his head.

The carriage reached the Place de la Révolution at ten o’clock. The guillotine had been erected at the foot of the statue of Liberty, on the spot where the statue of Louis XV had once stood. The scaffold was surrounded by troops, by cannon, by the silent crowd that stretched to the horizon. The King stepped down from the carriage. He removed his coat and his cravat. He looked at the guillotine — at the blade that would end his life — and he did not flinch.

The executioner, Sanson, approached to bind his hands. The King resisted. “Do you think I will attempt to escape?” he said. Sanson insisted; the King submitted. The priests of the Commune had tried to make the King recant, to make him confess his crimes before the people, but he refused. Edgeworth whispered to him: “Go, son of Saint Louis, ascend to Heaven.” The King climbed the steps of the scaffold without help. He turned to face the crowd. He tried to speak.

“I die innocent of all the crimes laid to my charge,” he said. His voice was clear and strong. “I forgive those who have brought about my death. I pray that my blood may bring happiness to France. And I pray that—”

At that moment, the drums rolled. The order had been given. The King’s words were drowned by the thunder of the drums. The executioners seized him and forced him onto the plank. The blade fell. The head of Louis XVI fell into the basket, and the executioner lifted it by the hair and showed it to the crowd. A great cry rose from the people — “Vive la République!” — and the drums rolled again, and the cannons fired, and the King of France was dead.

VIII. The Effect in Europe

The news of the King’s execution reached the capitals of Europe within days. The reaction was universal horror. The courts of Europe had watched the Revolution with unease; they had seen the fall of the Bastille, the abolition of feudalism, the civil constitution of the clergy, the flight to Varennes, the insurrection of the tenth of August — and they had done nothing. But the execution of the King was different. It was not a political change; it was an act of war against the principle of monarchy itself. Every sovereign in Europe understood that what had happened to Louis XVI could happen to him.

The Emperor of Austria, Francis II — the nephew of Marie Antoinette — declared that the execution of the King was an outrage against the law of nations. The King of Prussia, Frederick William II, who had already been at war with France for eight months, renewed his oath to restore the monarchy. The Empress Catherine of Russia broke off diplomatic relations and expelled the French ambassador. The King of Spain declared war. The King of England, George III, whose minister Pitt had been watching the Revolution with cautious neutrality, was moved to break off negotiations. On the first of February, the Convention declared war on England and Holland; within weeks, the war that had been a conflict between France and Austria had become a war between the Revolution and Europe.

The émigré princes, led by the Count de Provence — who now proclaimed himself Louis XVIII, Regent of France — prepared for the invasion that would restore the monarchy. The armies of the Coalition swelled; the frontiers of France were threatened from the Pyrenees to the Rhine. The Revolution was isolated, surrounded, and at war with the world.

IX. The Effect in France

In France, the execution of the King produced a profound and lasting change. The Republic, which had been a theory and a hope, was now a fact sealed in blood. The death of the King cut the last thread that connected the new order to the old. There could be no return; the monarchy was not merely abolished but destroyed, and every man who had voted for the King’s death was now bound to the Revolution by a bond that could never be broken.

The Mountain emerged from the trial stronger than ever. The Girondins, who had been unable either to save the King or to prevent the terror that was to come, were fatally weakened. Their opposition to the King’s execution had made them enemies of the Mountain; their failure to save him had made them contemptible to the people. The struggle between the Girondins and the Mountain, which had been the central drama of the Convention, now entered its final phase. Within months, the Girondins would be proscribed, arrested, and sent to the guillotine. The Revolution would devour its own.

The sections of Paris, the Jacobin clubs, the Commune — these were the true victors of the trial. They had demanded the King’s death; they had forced the Convention to deliver it; they now held the power that the Convention had once held. The execution of the King was not the end of the Revolution but the beginning of its most terrible phase. The Committee of Public Safety, the Revolutionary Tribunal, the Law of Suspects, the Reign of Terror — these were the consequences of the twenty-first of January.

As for the King himself, he passed into legend. The royalist writers who would later recount his trial and execution painted him as a martyr, a saint, the innocent victim of a bloodthirsty rebellion. The revolutionary writers painted him as a traitor, a conspirator, a tyrant who had received the justice he deserved. Both portraits were simplifications. Louis XVI was neither a saint nor a monster. He was a man of ordinary intelligence and decent intentions, placed by birth at the centre of events he could not control, and destroyed by forces he could not understand. His death was not the death of a king but the death of an idea — the idea that sovereignty could be embodied in a single man, that the nation could be represented by a dynasty, that the Revolution could be reconciled with the monarchy. The trial of Louis XVI was the trial of that idea, and the verdict was death.
