The October Days

The October Days of 5 and 6 October 1789 were the second great popular insurrection of the French Revolution, and in many respects the more consequential of the two. The storming of the Bastille on 14 July had saved the National Assembly from dissolution and given Paris its armed militia; but the October Days altered the very geography of power. They brought the king, the queen, and the royal family from Versailles to Paris as prisoners of the people. They transferred the National Assembly to the capital. And they ended, in the space of thirty-six hours, the long history of Versailles as the seat of the French monarchy — a seat it had occupied since Louis XIV moved the court there in 1682.

The Bread Shortage and the Banquet

The immediate cause of the October Days was the scarcity of bread in Paris. Throughout September 1789, the grain supply had been irregular, prices had risen, and the bakers’ shops of the capital could not meet demand. Hunger, always the sharpest goad of the Paris crowd, had been aggravated by the political crisis of the summer and by the stagnation of trade that followed the July insurrection. But what turned hunger into insurrection was the news from Versailles of what the people called the orgy of 1 October.

The Flanders Regiment had been ordered to Versailles in mid-September as part of what the court intended as a show of force. The king’s military household, the bodyguards, and the Swiss regiments had long formed the armed core of the court’s security; but the arrival of an additional regiment of the line — regular troops — was interpreted by Paris as a preparation for the dissolution of the National Assembly and a counter-revolutionary stroke. The Comte d’Estaing, commanding the National Guard of Versailles, had received the regiment with all the forms of military honour, and the court resolved to cement the loyalty of the new troops by a gesture of hospitality.

On the evening of 1 October, the bodyguards invited the officers of the Flanders Regiment to a banquet in the great theatre of the château. The hall was decked for a fête. The music of the two regiments played through the meal. At the second service, four toasts were drunk — to the king, the queen, the Dauphin, and the royal family — but the health of the nation was expressly refused. Then the king and queen entered, the queen leading the Dauphin by the hand. She made the tour of the tables, the child in her arms, moving through waves of applause and acclamation. The visit had the appearance of spontaneity; it had been arranged in advance.

The fête dissolved into an orgy. Wine flowed without restraint. The musicians played “O Richard, O mon roi, l’univers t’abandonne” — a song whose perfidious allusion to the king’s abandonment could not, in that moment, have been lost on any hearer — and the march of the howling winds from Iphigénie. The national cockade was trampled. White cockades, the symbol of the monarchy and of resistance to the Revolution, were distributed in the galleries. An officer of d’Estaing’s staff scaled the balcony of the king’s apartment and displayed the white cockade to applause. The banquet was repeated the following night in the riding school, with a greater crowd of guests and circumstances still more offensive to the nation.

The news of these events reached Paris two days later. The effect was explosive. The scarcity of bread, the arrogance of the courtiers who wore the black cockade in the public promenades of Paris as a symbol of the mourning they were preparing to plunge all France into, the insult to the tricolour, the rumour that the king would soon flee to Metz and dissolve the Assembly — all these converged into a single cry: vengeance.

Monday 5 October: The Women March

The morning of Monday 5 October saw the outbreak. Troops of women — the fishwives and market-women of Les Halles, the poorest and most formidable of the Parisian populace — spread through the city like a torrent, crying for bread. A baker was nearly seized and hanged from the street lantern; only the intervention of M. de Gouvion, who threw himself between the man and his pursuers, saved him.

The women gathered before the Hôtel de Ville. Some six or seven thousand of them, by the most moderate estimate, crowded the square. They broke into the municipal building, took what arms they could find, and threatened to burn the whole edifice. At this point a man named Maillard — one of the heroes of the Bastille, known for his composure under fire — imposed himself as their leader. It was Maillard who, seeing that the Hôtel de Ville could not be saved unless the crowd was diverted, proposed to lead the women to Versailles. “While they are walking four leagues,” he said to the authorities, “we can prevent the misfortunes that threaten us.” He persuaded the women to lay down their weapons, telling them they would move the National Assembly far more by appearing as suppliants asking for bread than by presenting themselves as an armed force.

They set out in the rain. A great multitude of armed men had joined them. Companies of volunteers from the Bastille accompanied the march; a troop of the Parisian guard followed with cannon. The tocsin had been ringing all morning; the générale had been beaten; the whole city was in motion.

At Versailles, the National Assembly was debating the king’s response to the constitutional articles. Mirabeau, who had received word of the march before anyone else in the chamber — a circumstance that has never been satisfactorily explained — approached the president, Mounier, and said in a low voice: “Mounier, Paris marches on us. Go to the château; give them this warning. Tell them, if you wish, that you have it from me; I consent. But end this scandalous debate. Time presses; there is not a minute to lose.”

At about three in the afternoon, the women appeared at the entrance of the Avenue de Paris. Maillard was at their head. The bodyguards, three hundred and twenty strong, mounted their horses and formed in squadrons on the Place d’Armes, their backs to the château gates. The Flanders Regiment and the dragoons took up positions. Maillard presented himself at the bar of the National Assembly: “We have come to ask for bread and to demand the punishment of the bodyguards who insulted the national cockade.” At that moment a national cockade was brought to Maillard from the bodyguards; he showed it to the women as a token of peaceable intentions. They cried: “Vive le roi! Vive les gardes-du-corps!”

The president went to the château to lay before the king the picture of the famine that afflicted Paris. But while Maillard was still at the bar of the Assembly, a detachment of women had advanced towards the château to demand bread. The bodyguards moved to bar their way. Lieutenant de Savonnières, sabre in hand, pursued a soldier of the Paris guard whom the women had forced to march at their head. A musket-shot from a citizen-soldier of Versailles shattered Savonnières’ arm and saved the man’s life. This first act of hostility inflamed both sides. Stones were thrown; carbines were fired from the king’s guard; two or three of the women fell. The crowd answered with a volley; two or three bodyguards were thrown from their horses. Three pieces of cannon loaded with grapeshot, brought by the Faubourg Saint-Antoine, were trained on the bodyguards. The match was brought to the touch-hole several times, but the rain had dampened the powder, and voices were heard crying: “Stop. It is not time yet.”

It was the rain and the lack of concert that saved the bodyguards. The women of Paris broke away from the main body of combatants, attacked the Flanders Regiment from the front and took the dragoons in flank. They penetrated the ranks, embraced the soldiers, and made the weapons fall from their hands. The order not to fire, not to irritate the people, was repeated and carried to the bodyguards. They withdrew. The grilles of the château closed behind them.

The Night

The Parisian army, exhausted by the march and the rain, arrived at nightfall. Lafayette, who had been compelled to lead his troops — he paled when the order was given, and cast a sorrowful look over the battalions that surrounded him — took command. The king had accepted the constitutional articles in a pure and simple form. The National Assembly decreed that the king and the Constituent body were inseparable during the current session. Peace seemed to have been restored. Lafayette, assured that all was quiet, took some rest.

But the women and the brigands mingled among them had passed the night in the Assembly hall and in the great guardhouse of the Place d’Armes. They drank; they whipped each other’s rage; they went out at daybreak famished for vengeance and thirsting for blood.

It was about five in the morning of Tuesday 6 October. The day was just breaking. The crowd surged towards the château. The ordinary precautions of security had been forgotten in the confusion of the previous day. The horde penetrated as far as the Cour Royale. The bodyguards seized their arms. A musket-shot from a window of the château, whether by imprudence or blind zeal, struck down a citizen. The crowd, excited by the spectacle, rushed to vengeance with terrible cries directed above all against the queen.

The crowd mounted the great staircase. The bodyguards presented themselves. Miomandre de Sainte-Marie, a bodyguard, descended four steps and was immediately set upon; he managed to disengage himself and rejoin his comrades. The guards fell back, some into the king’s chamber, some into the great hall. The brigands turned towards the queen’s apartment. Tardivet du Repaire, a bodyguard, threw himself before the door to prevent the howling mass from entering. He was assailed and fell under the blows that rained upon him from all sides. A man armed with a pike tried to pierce his heart; he managed to disarm the man and rise again, parrying the bayonet-strokes of a soldier with the very pike. The door of the king’s chamber opened a little, and two of his comrades pulled him inside.

During this time, Miomandre de Sainte-Marie saw a bodyguard of the queen’s antechamber being dragged on the staircase. Terrified only for the princess’s danger, he leaped through the waves of the furious multitude, saw Repaire struggling in the hands of assassins, heard a band of cannibals howling the death-cry. Nothing stopped him. He flew to the queen’s apartment, opened the door: “Madame,” he cried to a woman he perceived at the extremity of the next room, “save the queen; they want her life; I am alone here against two thousand tigers.” He closed the door behind him, and after some minutes of combat was struck down by a pike; another assailant struck him on the head with a musket-butt. He lay senseless and bathed in his blood. The brigands, believing him dead, stripped him and returned to the great hall.

The queen had been asleep. Her women — who had disobeyed her order to go to bed — heard the horrible cries and the gunshots at about half-past four. One of them entered the queen’s chamber and woke her. “Get out of bed, Madame,” she cried; “do not dress; save yourself to the king.” The queen, terrified, threw herself out of bed; a petticoat was passed over her without being tied; her two ladies led her towards the Œil-de-Bœuf. The door of her dressing-cabinet, which communicated with that room, was never locked except on her side. In that terrible moment it was found locked on the other side. They knocked repeatedly; a domestic of the king’s valet de chambre opened it. The queen entered the king’s chamber and did not find him. Alarmed for the queen’s life, he had descended by the staircases and corridors that ran beneath the Œil-de-Bœuf and that led habitually to the queen’s apartment without needing to cross that room. He entered her chamber and found only the bodyguards who had taken refuge there.

The queen, meanwhile, as she crossed the Œil-de-Bœuf, heard threatening voices crying out: “It is a messenger; she must be hanged; we must eat her liver.” A pistol-shot and a musket-shot redoubled her terror. She reached the king at last — he had entered by one door as she entered by another — and threw herself into his arms. Madame de Tourzel, the governess of the royal children, brought the Dauphin and Madame Royale. The family was reunited.

The Parisian National Guard had now arrived in force. The grenadiers mounted to the apartments, and in an instant the brigands were driven out. But a great number of the bodyguards had fallen into their hands. Messieurs Desuttes and de Varicourt, surprised in the first moment of the attack, had been massacred, and their bloody heads carried, each at the end of a pike, from Versailles to Paris. Bands of madmen dragged the surviving prisoners from side to side; some condemned them to the lantern; others summoned an assassin of their band, known as Coupe-Tête, remarkable for his long beard and fierce aspect, his arms naked to the elbow, his hands still bloody from the first executions, brandishing a hatchet and inviting new murders.

Lafayette exerted himself to obtain mercy for the surviving bodyguards. The Paris soldiers placed them under their own banners as under a sacred shield. The bodyguards who had retreated into the interior apartments of the château reappeared suddenly at the windows, raising their hats, displaying the national cockade, and crying: “Vive la nation!” The people answered: “Vive le roi! Vive les gardes-du-corps!” All was reconciliation. The two troops mingled and embraced.

The Balcony and the Return to Paris

The king appeared on the balcony, accompanied by the queen and her children. A voice cried: “The king to Paris!” The people and the army repeated it. Lafayette announced that the monarch consented to the wish of the capital.

Before the departure, there was the moment on the balcony that has become one of the most famous images of the Revolution. The crowd demanded that the queen appear alone. She stepped forward with the Dauphin and Madame Royale. The cry arose: “No children!” Perhaps the faction hoped that some desperate hand would dare to strike her; perhaps they wished to strip her of the protection her children gave her. The queen sent the children back. She advanced alone to the edge of the balcony, her eyes and hands raised to heaven, like a victim offering herself. Lafayette, who had followed her, knelt and kissed her hand. The crowd, which a moment before had been howling for her blood, fell silent, and then broke into acclamations.

The departure from Versailles took place at about one in the afternoon. The cortege was extraordinary beyond any that France had ever seen — part triumphal procession, part funeral march, part saturnalia. The vanguard was composed of a large detachment of the army, trains of artillery, and a great part of the women and the pikemen, some on foot, others in fiacres or hired carriages, others in carts, still others mounted on the cannon. They were followed by fifty to sixty wagons of flour and wheat. A large body of bourgeois cavalry, intermingled with deputies, women, and grenadiers, surrounded the king’s carriage, which moved with the greatest slowness to accommodate itself to the pace of the multitude.

The women who preceded the royal carriage sang allegorical airs, applying their pointed and naive allusions to the king and queen. “We shall not lack bread any more,” they cried; “we bring the baker, the baker’s wife, and the little baker’s boy.” The heads of two massacred bodyguards, those of Desuttes and de Varicourt, were carried on pikes at the head of the procession, preceded by the man called Coupe-Tête, his hair, his garments, his hands dripping with blood, a bloody hatchet on his shoulder. The cortege offered at once the spectacle of a carnage, a civic festival, a saturnalia, and a triumphal march. The king might equally have been taken for a father in the midst of his children, for a victim surrounded by his assassins, or for a dethroned prince paraded in triumph by rebel subjects.

Gouverneur Morris, watching from Paris, recorded what he learned as the news arrived: “Two Heads of the Gardes du Corps are brought to Town.” The queen, he heard from Capellis, escaped the château “in her Shift and Petticoat with her Stockings in her Hand.”

The march was so slow that it was near six o’clock in the evening when the royal family, prisoners of their own people, reached the Hôtel de Ville. Bailly received them. The king spoke with assurance and goodness, saying that he came always with pleasure and confidence into the midst of the inhabitants of his good city of Paris. Bailly repeated the phrase to the representatives of the Commune, but forgot the word “confidence.” The queen recalled it to him immediately and in a loud voice.

The Consequences

The king and queen, their children and Madame Élisabeth, proceeded to the Tuileries Palace. Nothing was prepared to receive them. The palace had been uninhabited for years; it was furnished with what could be found in haste. The royal family was installed in what was, in effect, a gilded prison.

The National Assembly, declaring that it could not remain separated from the king’s person, resolved on 10 October to transfer itself to Paris. The deputies arrived on 19 October and established themselves in the riding school of the Tuileries, the Manège, where they would sit for the next four years. The king’s first act in Paris was his acceptance of the first articles of the constitution and the sanction of the Declaration of the Rights of Man.

The October Days thus accomplished what the fall of the Bastille had not. The king was now in Paris, under the eye of the capital, at the mercy of every popular movement. Versailles, the great palace of the Bourbon monarchy, was abandoned as a royal residence. The court ceased to exist as a distinct political force; the buffer between the king and the Paris crowd had been removed. The monarchy had lost its independence, and the royal family would never again leave the capital alive — except for the doomed flight to Varennes, which itself ended in a forced return to Paris.

The two days of October 1789 were, as Pagès observed, the second revolution within the Revolution. The first, 14 July, had saved the National Assembly and armed the people. The second, 5 and 6 October, had captured the king and placed the Revolution’s ultimate authority — the person of the monarch — in the hands of the capital. From this moment, the Tuileries was a cage, and the Revolution, whatever course it might take, would be enacted under the eyes of the Parisian crowd.
