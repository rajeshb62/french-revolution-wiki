10 August 1792

I. The Approaching Storm

By the summer of 1792, the French Revolution had arrived at a crisis from which there was no turning back. The Legislative Assembly had been at war with Austria since April, and the war was going badly. The French armies had fled before the advancing Austrians; the émigré princes were gathering on the frontier; and the Duke of Brunswick, commanding the combined armies of Austria and Prussia, stood ready to invade. On the twenty-fifth of July, Brunswick issued his famous Manifesto — a document drafted by French émigrés and approved by the Bourbon princes — which reached Paris on the third of August. Its terms were brutal: any harm done to the royal family would be met with “exemplary and unforgettable vengeance”; the city of Paris would be destroyed if the Tuileries were again violated; the National Guard itself would be shot down as armed civilians if it dared to resist. The Manifesto, which Morris summarised as “Be all against me, for I am against you all; and make a good Resistance, for there is no longer any Hope,” produced the opposite effect from the one intended. Instead of cowing the revolutionaries, it drove men in crowds to enlist. It was the final proof, for those who still needed it, that the King was in league with the enemies of France.

The political temperature in Paris had been rising for months. The King had vetoed the decrees against the refractory priests and the proposed camp of twenty thousand fédérés; the Girondin ministers — Roland, Servan, Clavières — had been dismissed in June; Lafayette had appeared before the Assembly to demand the suppression of the Jacobins, and had been howled down. The sections of Paris, in constant session since the summer began, had voted on the thirtieth of July to petition for the deposition of the King — the déchéance. The fédérés — volunteers from the provinces who had come to Paris for the Federation of the fourteenth of July — remained in the city, armed and restless. Among them were the Marseillais, five hundred men from the southern port, who had marched into Paris singing the war-hymn that would one day bear their name. They bivouacked in the Cordeliers district and drilled openly in the gardens of the Palais-Royal. The court knew what was coming. On the fourth of August, the King and Queen, expecting to be murdered that night, sat up until dawn in the Tuileries, waiting for the blow that did not fall.

II. The Night of the Ninth of August

The day of Thursday the ninth of August was sweltering. Paris was in great agitation. Morris, the American Minister, noted the heat — the same oppressive weather that later in the week would cause a perch caught at six in the morning to be spoiled by dinner-time, so rapid was the state of putrefaction. He went about his business that day: a call on Monciel, a visit to court, a meeting with Constable and Chaumont about a land company, a happy moment with his friend at the Louvre, dinner with the British Ambassador, an evening at Madame d’Albani’s. He returned home near midnight. Paris was in great agitation.

The tocsin began to sound after midnight. It was the signal. From the Cordeliers district, from the Faubourg Saint-Antoine, from the Faubourg Saint-Marceau, the sections rose. Danton, from the Cordeliers, and Robespierre, from the Jacobins, had been concerting with the Commune and the fédérés for weeks. The plan was simple: seize the Hôtel de Ville, replace the legal municipal government with an insurrectionary Commune, and march on the Tuileries. By three in the morning of the tenth, the sections had taken control of the city. The old municipal authorities were arrested or dispersed. The tocsin rang from every church tower in Paris. The drums beat the générale. The streets filled with armed men.

III. The Morning at the Tuileries

At the Tuileries, the royal family had been warned. Mandat, the commander of the National Guard, had stationed what troops he could around the palace — a few battalions of guardsmen, the Swiss regiments, some gentlemen volunteers. But the National Guard was unreliable; many of its men were already in sympathy with the sections. The Marseillais and the fédérés spent the night assembling at the Place de la Bastille and the Place du Carrousel, under the very windows of the palace.

The King had reviewed the National Guard early in the morning. He passed along the ranks in the courtyard of the Tuileries, and the cries that greeted him were not the loyal acclamations of former days but the cold silence of men who had already made their choice. The battalions of the Faubourg Saint-Antoine and the Faubourg Saint-Marceau, which had been stationed to defend the palace, defected to the insurgents before the morning was half gone. The guard at the Carrousel opened the gates without a struggle. Mandat was summoned to the Hôtel de Ville by the new Commune, and there he was arrested and murdered — shot dead on the steps of the building he had commanded. The National Guard was left without a leader, and the defence of the palace fell almost entirely to the Swiss.

Inside the palace, the confusion was terrible. Madame Campan, the Queen’s first lady-in-waiting, was not on duty that day, but she did not leave the Queen’s apartment. Two of her sisters and a niece were with her. The King, seeing the defection of the guard, the approach of the mob, and the impossibility of defence, made a decision that would be debated for a century: he would not fight. At the urging of his ministers and of Roederer, the procureur-syndic of the department, he resolved to take his family to the National Assembly and place themselves under the protection of the legislature.

IV. The Flight to the Assembly

The royal family left the Tuileries between seven and eight in the morning. The King, the Queen, the Princess Elisabeth, the Dauphin, and Madame Royale crossed the garden of the Tuileries on foot, guarded by a handful of National Guards, and entered the hall of the Legislative Assembly through the garden door. They were received by the President of the Assembly and placed in the logograph — the reporters’ box, a narrow, stifling enclosure behind the President’s chair, where the stenographers usually sat. The heat was suffocating. There was no food, no change of clothes, nothing but the desperate hope that the Assembly could protect them. Lady Sutherland, the British Ambassador’s wife, sent over some clothes for the little Dauphin, whose son was about the same age. There they sat, the royal family of France, crowded into a box meant for clerks, listening to the debates that would decide their fate.

The King spoke to the Assembly. He said he had come to avoid a great crime, and that he and his family would trust themselves to the representatives of the nation. The Assembly heard him coldly. The deputies of the right sat in silence; the majority, under the pressure of the armed crowd that had gathered outside the hall, leaned towards suspension. Vergniaud rose to speak — the great orator of the Gironde — and pronounced the words that ended the monarchy: the King was suspended from his functions; a National Convention would be summoned to decide the future form of government.

V. The Attack and the Massacre

While the King sat in the Assembly, the Tuileries fell. The insurgents, many thousands strong, poured into the courtyard of the Carrousel and the gardens. The Swiss Guard, about nine hundred men, had been left without orders. Some of their officers had followed the King to the Assembly; others had fled. The Swiss, seeing the crowd surge towards the palace gates, fired. The first volley stopped the advance. But the insurgents, reinforced by the fédérés and by battalions of the National Guard that had gone over to the insurrection, returned the fire from behind the trees of the Tuileries garden and from the windows of the houses on the Carrousel. The fighting was fierce but brief. The Swiss, isolated and outnumbered, sent a messenger to the Assembly to ask for instructions, but no orders came. They began to retreat through the palace, and then the tide turned.

The Swiss Guard laid down their arms. They had been promised safe passage. They were not spared. As Morris noted in his diary that evening: “The Château, undefended but by the Swiss, is carried and the Swiss wherever found are murder’d.” The massacre was systematic and terrible. The Swiss were pursued through the corridors, the salons, the kitchens of the palace. Those who surrendered in the courtyard were shot down. Those who had thrown down their muskets and tried to mix with the crowd were recognised by their uniforms and killed on the spot. Some fled into the gardens and were hunted through the trees. The corpses were stripped, mutilated, and piled in heaps. The palace itself was ransacked: the furniture was smashed, the mirrors shattered, the wine cellars drunk dry. The mob poured through the great apartments, tearing down tapestries, breaking open cabinets, defacing portraits of kings. The papers of the royal household were scattered across the Carrousel. Campan’s house, which stood at the entrance to the court of the Tuileries, was pillaged and partly burned; the Marshal de Villars’ baton, preserved for generations, was thrown onto the pavement and only saved by a servant who recognised its value.

By noon, the palace was a ruin of broken glass and bloody marble. The Swiss dead lay in rows in the courtyard. The Carrousel was strewn with torn clothing, papers, and the debris of a thousand homes. The heat was extreme. Morris noted that some perch alive in the morning were spoiled by dinner — so rapid was the state of putrefaction in that summer heat. Those who had seen the massacre, like Mr Francis, gave accounts so dreadful that they said they would not dare to tell them in America.

VI. The King Suspended

The Assembly met all day under the eyes of the armed crowd that filled the galleries and the approaches to the hall. The deputies of the Gironde — Vergniaud, Gensonné, Guadet — dominated the debates; the Mountain — Robespierre, Danton, Marat — pressed from the tribunes for the abolition of the monarchy itself. The King, in his box, heard himself denounced as a traitor and a tyrant, and could not reply. The Queen, pale and composed, held the Dauphin close and listened in silence.

The decree of suspension was passed in the afternoon. The King was stripped of all his powers. The ministry was replaced: Roland returned to the Interior, Servan to War, Clavières to Finance; Danton, the violent orator of the Cordeliers, took the Ministry of Justice. The King and his family were not allowed to return to the Tuileries; they were sent that evening to the Temple, the old fortress of the Knights Templar in the northern quarter of Paris. The Temple had not been used as a prison for years; it was cold, damp, and unfurnished. But it was secure. And there, in the great tower that had once sheltered the order of soldier-monks, the Bourbon monarchy went to its captivity.

VII. The Aftermath

The insurrection of the tenth of August was the most decisive day of the Revolution since the fall of the Bastille. Where the Bastille had been the destruction of an old symbol, the tenth of August was the destruction of the monarchy itself — not in principle but in fact. The King was no longer the King; he was a prisoner, a hostage, a man awaiting trial.

The Commune of Paris, born in the insurrectionary night of the ninth of August, now ruled the city with an authority that no legal power could challenge. It had armed the sections; it had seized the Hôtel de Ville; it had murdered Mandat; it had forced the suspension of the King. Its leaders — men like Hébert, Chaumette, and the formidable Santerre — were not deputies but militants, and they answered not to the Assembly but to the streets. The Commune demanded a revolutionary tribunal, the arrest of suspects, the confiscation of royalist property. It prepared the September Massacres that would follow within weeks — the slaughter of the prisoners of the Abbaye, the Force, and the Carmes, in which the friends of the court, the Swiss who had not been killed on the tenth, and the priests who had refused the oath would be butchered in the name of revolutionary justice.

The Girondins, who had led the attack on the King and orchestrated the political crisis that made the tenth of August possible, now found themselves the prisoners of forces they could no longer control. Buzot and Louvet, who had been among the fiercest advocates of the déchéance, watched with horror as the Commune seized the authority they had hoped to exercise. The sections, the fédérés, the Jacobin clubs — these were the real powers, and they had no use for the constitutional scruples of the Gironde.

As for the King, he passed his first night in the Temple in a bare room, without a bed, without a servant, without even a change of linen. For the first time in his life, the man who had ruled France locked his own door and slept, if he slept at all, as a prisoner. The monarchy of a thousand years had fallen, and the man who had been its last embodiment lay alone in the darkness of the Temple tower.

Source Material

  • Gouverneur Morris, A Diary of the French Revolution, Volume II (1790–1793). Morris was the American Minister in Paris; his diary entry for 10 August 1792 records the cannon and musquetry, the fall of the Tuileries, the murder of the Swiss, the King’s suspension, and the stifling heat in the days that followed.
  • Madame Campan, Mémoires sur la vie privée de Marie-Antoinette (3 vols., 1823). Campan was the Queen’s first lady-in-waiting and present in the Tuileries during the insurrection; her account covers the preparations, the morning of the tenth, the flight to the Assembly, and the destruction of her house on the Carrousel.
  • François Buzot, Mémoires sur la Révolution française (1823). Buzot, a Girondin deputy, provides the political context of the Girondins’ role in orchestrating the déchéance and the insurrection.
  • Jean-Baptiste Louvet, Mémoires de Louvet (1823). Louvet, also a Girondin, recounts the political manoeuvring before the tenth of August.
  • Madame Roland, Mémoires de Madame Roland (1823). Roland, whose husband was Minister of the Interior, gives the governmental perspective on the crisis and the fall of the Girondin ministry.
