The Egyptian Campaign

The Armament of the East

The Treaty of Campo Formio, signed in October 1797, had left France alone in Europe against a single enemy she could not reach: England. The Directory, beset by financial collapse and political faction, needed both a victory and a diversion. Bonaparte, returned from the Italian campaigns with his ambition inflamed and his ambition uneasy in the atmosphere of Paris, needed a new theatre for his genius. Two needs converged in the spring of 1798 upon a single project: the conquest of Egypt.

It was an enterprise that appealed to every element of Bonaparte’s character. The Orient had haunted European imaginations since the Crusades; a French expedition to the Nile would revive the ancient dream of a Mediterranean empire, threaten British India from its western flank, and remove from Paris the most popular general of the Republic at a moment when the Directory found his presence increasingly inconvenient. On the 5th of March 1798, Bonaparte laid before the Directory a detailed memorandum on the dispositions necessary for the seizure of Egypt and Malta. The same day, decrees were issued under cover of a commission on the coast defences of the Mediterranean. The preparations began in earnest.

For three months the ports of Toulon, Marseilles, Genoa, and Civita-Vecchia hummed with extraordinary activity. Thirteen ships of the line, fourteen frigates, and over four hundred transports were assembled—the greatest armament France had ever launched upon the sea. The army numbered some thirty-five thousand men, the finest troops of the Republic, veterans of the Italian campaign, commanded by generals who would give their names to an epoch: Kléber, Desaix, Lannes, Murat, Berthier, Caffarelli, Reynier. But the expedition carried more than soldiers. Bonaparte had insisted on bringing a Commission of Sciences and Arts—over one hundred and sixty savants, mathematicians, engineers, naturalists, chemists, antiquarians, painters, and printers—whose task was to survey, describe, and catalogue the ancient land of Egypt. It was a singular marriage of Mars and Minerva, unprecedented in the history of warfare.

The Treasury was empty; the Directory was borrowing seventeen million francs from the Bernese treasury, seized after the invasion of Switzerland, and draining the other cantons in proportion. Another eighteen million were raised by requisitions. The Swiss Helvetia was plundered, the Papal States occupied, to fund the voyage to the Nile.

The Sailing and the Elusion

On the 19th of May 1798, the great armada weighed anchor from Toulon. The wind was fair, the sea calm, the spectacle immense. As the fleet filed through the narrow exit between the Îles d’Hyères and the coast, Bonaparte addressed his army in the tone that had already become his signature: “Soldiers, you are about to undertake a conquest of which the effects on civilisation and commerce are incalculable. The English nation, which you have already beaten in Italy, awaits you with impatience. The seas which we must cross are infested with their cruisers; but we shall force them to fly before us, or sink in the abyss.”

The British had indeed been watching. On the 2nd of May, the day before Bonaparte left Paris for the coast, Lord St. Vincent off Cadiz had dispatched his most aggressive subordinate into the Mediterranean with three ships of the line, later reinforced to fourteen. Rear-Admiral Sir Horatio Nelson’s orders were to find the French armament and destroy it. He reached the neighbourhood of Toulon on the 17th of May, only to be scattered by a gale that dismasted his flagship. While he refitted, Bonaparte slipped past unseen.

What followed was one of the most remarkable chases in maritime history. Nelson, a blind man in a vast sea, had no frigates—those eyes of the fleet without which a commander gropes in darkness. He guessed correctly that the French were bound for Malta and Egypt, but he could not prove it. He arrived off Alexandria on the 28th of June; finding no French, he turned north in frustration, searching the coasts of Asia Minor. Two days after his departure, Bonaparte’s fleet anchored off the Egyptian coast. On the 1st of July, the French army disembarked near Alexandria.

During the voyage, the French had seized Malta with remarkable ease. The ancient fortress of the Knights of St. John, weakened by internal decay and apathy, capitulated after a brief show of force. Bonaparte stayed six days, reorganised the government, abolished the Order’s privileges, confiscated its treasury, and sailed eastward with the island’s treasure in his holds. The Knights, who had resisted the Ottomans for centuries, had offered the French army hardly a day’s resistance.

The Landing and the Pyramids

When the French army stepped ashore near Alexandria on the 1st of July, it found a country that had not changed substantially since the age of the Ptolemies. Egypt was a province of the Ottoman Empire in name only; in reality, it was ruled by the Mamelukes, a military caste of Circassian and Georgian slave-soldiers who had governed the country for centuries through a system of feudal exploitation that had reduced the native population to poverty and the land to neglect. The Mameluke beys, Murad and Ibrahim, commanded a formidable cavalry that had defeated every Ottoman attempt to reassert imperial authority. They had never faced European infantry.

Bonaparte stormed Alexandria on the 2nd of July, the troops wading ashore through the surf under a hail of musketry. The city fell within hours. The army then began its march across the Delta toward Cairo, a movement of extraordinary hardship through a landscape of heat, dust, and irrigation canals that forced the columns into constant delay and disorder. The Mameluke cavalry harassed the flanks; the Bedouin of the desert intercepted stragglers with a ferocity that the French infantry, accustomed to the formal battles of Europe, found shocking.

On the 21st of July, the French army came in sight of the Pyramids. At the village of Embabeh, on the left bank of the Nile opposite Cairo, the Mameluke army was drawn up in full array: Murad Bey’s horsemen, perhaps twelve thousand strong, the finest cavalry in the East, their sabres gleaming in the morning sun, their horses caparisoned in silk and gold. Behind them lay the Pyramids of Giza, ancient witnesses to a civilisation that had already risen and fallen while Europe was still barbarous.

Bonaparte formed his army into divisional squares, five massive quadrangles of infantry and artillery, with the baggage and the savants in the centre. The Mameluke cavalry charged with the fury that had carried everything before them for centuries. They broke against the French squares like waves against granite. Volley after volley of musketry and canister tore through the horsemen; the squares held, and the Mameluke power was shattered in a single afternoon. The Turks and Mamelukes who had not been killed on the field were driven into the Nile, where hundreds drowned. Cairo submitted the next day; on the 25th of July, Bonaparte entered the city.

The victory had been complete, but the French were not yet masters of Egypt. Murad Bey retreated into Upper Egypt with the remnants of his army; Desaix was despatched in pursuit, a campaign that would occupy him for months through the ruined temples and barren deserts of the upper Nile. Ibrahim Bey fled eastward toward Syria. Bonaparte settled into Cairo, began to organise the administration of the conquered province, and waited for news from his fleet.

The Battle of the Nile

The news, when it came, was the worst that could be imagined.

While Bonaparte had been advancing through the Delta, Admiral Brueys had anchored his squadron in the Bay of Aboukir, fifteen miles east of Alexandria. The anchorage was an open roadstead, sheltered from the prevailing north-west wind by a line of shoals and rocks. Bonaparte had given explicit orders: if the fleet could not enter the old harbour of Alexandria, it must find a defensible position at Aboukir or, failing both, make for Corfu. Brueys, whose temperament was irresolute and whose health was broken, chose the easier alternative. He moored his thirteen ships of the line in a single column, a mile and a half from the shoals, and waited.

He waited for nearly a month. On the 1st of August, the lookout sighted sails. At one in the afternoon, the British fleet of fourteen ships of the line was seen bearing down under full sail. Nelson, who had returned to Egypt after his fruitless search of the Levantine coast, had found the French at last.

The battle that followed was the most complete naval victory of the age. Nelson attacked at once, without hesitation, without a council of war; the sun was already sinking, the shoals were uncharted, but his mind had been running on this moment through the long weeks of the pursuit. He had discussed his plans with his captains; he had prepared them to act on their own initiative. Now he trusted them.

Captain Foley of the Goliath, leading the British line, did something that no French officer had thought possible. Instead of anchoring on the seaward side of the French column—the side that Brueys had prepared for—he steered inside the French line, crossing ahead of the leading ship, and anchored on the inner flank. The Zealous followed; then the Orion, the Theseus, the Audacious. Five British ships were inside the French line, doubling on the van. The French were caught between two fires.

The flag-ship Orient, a three-decker of one hundred and twenty guns, became the focal point of the battle. The Bellerophon, a seventy-four, anchored abreast of her and fought with a fury that astonished both fleets until she was a wreck. The Swiftsure and Alexander arrived in the darkness and completed the concentration on the French centre. At about nine in the evening, the Orient was seen to be on fire. Admiral Brueys, twice wounded already, had a cannonball carry away his left leg at the thigh; he refused to be taken below and died on his quarterdeck moments before the fire broke out. At ten o’clock, the Orient blew up with a detonation that shook the sea and silenced both fleets for a long moment of awe.

By dawn on the 2nd of August, eleven of Brueys’s thirteen ships of the line had been destroyed or captured. Two escaped—the Guillaume Tell and the Généreux—carrying the news to France. The French lost some thirty-five hundred men; the British, under nine hundred. Nelson himself was wounded in the head by a heavy splinter and believed for a time that he was dying.

The strategic consequences were incalculable. In a single night, the entire architecture of Bonaparte’s eastern expedition had been swept away. The Mediterranean passed from French to British control. The Ottoman Porte, which had been hesitating, declared war on France. The Second Coalition—Austria, Russia, Naples, the Porte—crystallised into active hostility. And the Army of the East, which had conquered Egypt, was now marooned in it.

When the news reached Bonaparte, the soldiers gave way to despair. “Here we are,” they cried, “abandoned in this barbarous country.” Their general met the moment with the iron self-control that was the mark of his genius. “We have then laid on us the obligation to do great things,” he told them. “Seas which we do not command separate us from home; but no seas divide us from Africa and Asia. We will found here an empire.”

The Administration of Egypt and the Institute of Cairo

Throughout the autumn of 1798, Bonaparte bent his extraordinary energy to the conquest of Egypt not merely by arms but by organisation. The ancient land, for centuries the private estate of a corrupt military caste, was to be remade in the image of European civilisation. The feudal privileges of the Mamelukes were abolished. A new system of taxation was imposed, the cadastre surveyed, the irrigation canals repaired, the bazaars regulated. The French army imposed order with a severity that the Egyptians, accustomed to the desultory cruelties of Ottoman governance, found novel and oppressive.

On the 22nd of August, Bonaparte founded the Institute of Cairo, modelled on the Institute of France. The savants who had accompanied the expedition—mathematicians like Monge and Fourier, chemists like Berthollet, naturalists like Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire, engineers, antiquarians, orientalists—were organised into a learned society that met in the former palace of a Mameluke bey on the shores of the Nile. The Institute undertook the monumental work that would eventually produce the Description de l’Égypte—the most comprehensive survey of an ancient civilisation ever attempted by European science. They measured the temples of Luxor and Karnak, mapped the course of the Nile, catalogued the flora and fauna, studied the customs of the fellahin, and contemplated the riddle of the hieroglyphs. The Rosetta Stone, discovered by a French engineer during the fortification of Fort Saint-Julien in July 1799, would be the most famous fruit of their labours.

Bonaparte attended the meetings of the Institute in civilian dress, participating in the debates on geometry and chemistry with the absorption of a savant. He proposed questions for study: Could the bread of the army be improved by chemical means? Could the Nile floods be regulated by ancient irrigation works? Could Egypt be connected to the Red Sea by a canal through the Isthmus of Suez? The soldiers, who had fought their way through Italy and across the desert, watched with a mixture of bemusement and pride as their general debated the nature of heat with Monge or inspected a collection of scarabs with Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire.

But beneath the intellectual activity, the strategic situation grew steadily worse. In October, an insurrection broke out in Cairo, provoked by a new system of house taxes and by rumours that the Porte had declared war. Bonaparte suppressed it with a ferocity that was carefully calibrated: thirty beheaded in the streets, the heads exposed on the gates of the city, the survivors cowed into submission. The iron grip held, but the underlying hostility of the population was never extinguished.

Desaix, meanwhile, was pursuing Murad Bey through Upper Egypt in a campaign that reads like a prose epic. Through the autumn and winter of 1798, he chased the Mameluke chieftain among the ruins of Thebes and the cataracts of the Nile, fighting actions at Sediman and Samhoud, living off the land, enduring a climate that destroyed his men by disease faster than the enemy could kill them. It was a masterpiece of perseverance, but it drained the French army of resources it could not replace.

The Syrian Campaign

Bonaparte had not abandoned the larger vision that had brought him to Egypt. The destruction of his fleet had not destroyed his ambition. If he could not communicate with France, he could still advance eastward. The road to India ran through Syria, and the road to Syria ran through the desert.

By the end of 1798, he had learned that the Porte, now at war with France, was assembling an army in Syria under the Pasha Djezzar—“the Butcher”—a man whose reputation for cruelty was European legend. A second Turkish army was gathering on the Island of Rhodes, destined to land in Egypt when the summer weather permitted. Bonaparte determined to strike first: to march into Syria, defeat Djezzar’s army before it could take the offensive, seize the ports, and break the pincer before it could close.

On the 5th of February 1799, General Reynier advanced toward the oasis of El Arish, the gateway to Syria through the Sinai desert. The Turks were routed and driven from the oasis; Fort El Arish capitulated on the 20th. The army of thirteen thousand men, stripped of everything that could not be carried on the march, began the crossing of the desert.

They advanced through Gaza, which fell without resistance, and on the 7th of March reached Jaffa. The ancient port, sacred to Christian and Muslim alike, was carried by assault after a short siege. What followed cast a shadow over the campaign that Bonaparte’s apologists would spend the rest of their lives trying to erase. After the surrender of the garrison, by a variety of accounts numbering between two and three thousand men, Bonaparte ordered the prisoners shot—the entire garrison, marched out in parties and executed on the beach. The order was defended on the grounds that the garrison had been paroled after an earlier capitulation and had violated its parole, but the massacre was exceptional even by the standards of an age that did not flinch at severity. The soldiers who carried out the execution were sickened by it.

From Jaffa, the army marched north along the coast toward Acre—Saint-Jean-d’Acre, the ancient fortress that commanded the road to Constantinople. Acre was the hinge of Syria. If it fell, the way to Damascus and Aleppo lay open; the Turkish army could be broken before it could concentrate; the French could raise the population in rebellion against the oppressive rule of Djezzar. If it held, the campaign was lost.

The siege of Acre began on the 17th of March 1799. It would last sixty-two days—a drama of such narrow margins that the outcome seemed to tremble in the balance week after week.

The Siege of Acre

The defence of Acre was organised by a man who had been Bonaparte’s classmate at the military school of Brienne: Colonel Phélippeaux, a French royalist émigré who had escaped from Paris with the assistance of the British captain Sir Sidney Smith. It was one of those symmetrical encounters that history sometimes arranges—two men who had studied mathematics at the same school in France now confronted each other across the walls of a Syrian fortress, one commanding the attack, the other organising the defence.

Smith had arrived off Acre on the 15th of March with his flagship, the Tigre, and another ship of the line, the Theseus. The day before, his squadron had captured the French siege train—heavy artillery that had been sent by sea from Damietta to support the army’s advance. The guns that should have battered the walls of Acre into rubble were now mounted on its ramparts, served by British seamen. It was a stroke of fortune that probably decided the issue.

For two months, the French army flung itself against the fortifications with a desperation that increased as the defenders’ resistance stiffened. The siege was fought at close quarters—the trenches pushed to within yards of the walls, mines and counter-mines criss-crossing the earth beneath the bastions. Phélippeaux, working with feverish energy, repaired the medieval fortifications, connected the houses and garden walls into a second line of defence, and directed the fire of the captured guns with an engineer’s precision. He died at his post in April, but his work survived him.

Bonaparte pressed the assaults with all the skill and ferocity of his Italian campaigns. The attack of the 9th of May came closest to success. The French carried the outer wall and planted their flag on an angle of the tower; for a few hours, it seemed that the city must fall. But Smith, seeing the crisis, manned the boats of his squadron, pulled ashore, and led the British seamen armed with pikes to the breach. The Turkish reinforcements from Rhodes landed in the boats behind them. The fighting raged through the entire day and into the night; at dawn, the French were still outside, and Acre was saved.

The plague had come to the camp at Jaffa. The hospitals were choked with dying men. The army, which had started the campaign thirteen thousand strong, was reduced by killed, wounded, and disease to less than half that number. On the 20th of May, Bonaparte gave the order to retreat.

The withdrawal through the desert was worse than the advance. The plague-stricken were abandoned or, by some accounts that haunted Bonaparte’s reputation, hastened on their way by means that the general’s defenders denied and his enemies insisted upon. The army trudged through the heat of the Syrian summer, carrying its wounded, burning its stores, living on what it could find. On the 2nd of June, it encamped at El Arish, having lost since its departure fifteen hundred killed or dead of disease and more than two thousand wounded. On the 14th of June, the remnants re-entered Cairo.

The Syrian campaign had been a failure. Acre had held; Djezzar’s army had not been broken; the eastern empire that Bonaparte had imagined was as distant as before. But worse news was coming.

The Aboukir Landing and the Return

After the Syrian campaign, the army re-entered Cairo on the 14th of June. For a few weeks, there was breathing space. Then, on the 11th of July, Sir Sidney Smith anchored in Aboukir Bay with a Turkish fleet of thirteen ships of the line and over a hundred transports, carrying an army variously estimated at between ten and thirty thousand men. The blow that Bonaparte had anticipated fell precisely as he had feared.

The Turks landed, stormed the Castle of Aboukir, and entrenched themselves on the peninsula. Bonaparte, who was in Cairo when he learned of the landing, acted with the speed that had made his reputation. He concentrated his forces around Alexandria—Desaix was ordered to evacuate Upper Egypt, every available battalion was drawn to the coast—and on the 25th of July, he attacked.

The Battle of Aboukir was a masterpiece. The French army, outnumbered and exhausted, fell upon the Turkish positions on the same peninsula where Nelson had destroyed the French fleet a year before. The Turks were driven into the sea in a slaughter as complete as any Bonaparte had ever inflicted. Thousands drowned; the Turkish commander-in-chief was taken prisoner. The survivors who escaped were pursued by the French cavalry into the surf.

The victory was total, but it changed nothing. After the battle, flags of truce passed between Bonaparte and Sir Sidney Smith. Through this channel, Bonaparte received English newspapers—the first reliable news from Europe he had seen in months. What he read decided his future.

The news was catastrophic. The armies of the Second Coalition had driven the French out of Italy. The Russian general Suvarov had crossed the Adda, entered Milan, rolled back everything that Bonaparte had won in 1796 and 1797. France itself was threatened. The Directory, which had sent him to Egypt to be rid of him, was crumbling.

Bonaparte took his decision within days. He would return to France. His army—the finest soldiers of the Republic, the men who had followed him across the Alps and through the deserts of Africa—would be left behind under the command of Kléber. The secret was kept so closely that not even the senior generals were informed of the full plan.

On the 22nd of August, Admiral Ganteaume prepared two frigates that had fallen to France from the Venetian fleet. Bonaparte, accompanied by a small party that included Berthier, Lannes, Murat, Monge, and Berthollet, embarked at Alexandria under cover of darkness. Sir Sidney Smith, who had been blockading the port after the Battle of Aboukir, had withdrawn to Cyprus for water on the 9th of August; the window of opportunity was narrow, and Bonaparte sailed through it.

The voyage was long and anxious—forty-two days of constant vigilance against British cruisers. On the 9th of October, the frigates sighted the coast of France. Bonaparte landed at Fréjus, near Cannes, and was received with an enthusiasm that astonished even him. The news of his return spread like wildfire. Within a month, he had overthrown the Directory in the coup of 18 Brumaire and made himself First Consul of the French Republic.

The Army of Egypt: Kléber and Surrender

The army that Bonaparte abandoned in Egypt did not learn of his departure until he was safely at sea. The news produced a fury of indignation that only the iron rectitude of Kléber could have contained. The general to whom Bonaparte had left the command was a man of formidable abilities and a reputation as solid as his frame. He wrote to the French government a letter that fell into British hands and became famous: “I know all the importance of the possession of Egypt… but to do this effectually, a powerful lever is required, and that lever is a Navy. Ours has ceased to exist.”

Kléber recognised what Bonaparte had refused to admit: the Army of the East was trapped. It could neither be reinforced nor evacuated. The only rational course was to negotiate a surrender that would save the soldiers for France. With the countenance of Sir Sidney Smith, who seems to have acted beyond his instructions, Kléber entered into negotiations with the Turkish commander-in-chief. On the 24th of January 1800, the Convention of El Arish was signed: the French army would be allowed to evacuate Egypt and return to France.

The British government, however, repudiated the convention. Lord Keith, commanding in the Mediterranean, received instructions that no evacuation would be permitted unless the French laid down their arms and surrendered as prisoners of war. Smith’s authority to negotiate was disavowed. The convention collapsed.

Kléber resolved to fight. On the 20th of March 1800, he met the Turkish army at Heliopolis—the same battlefield where the ancient Greeks had defeated the Persians twenty centuries before—and inflicted a decisive defeat on the Grand Vizier’s army. The French position in Egypt was temporarily secured. But Kléber was not to enjoy his triumph. On the 14th of June 1800—the same day, as it happened, that Bonaparte won the Battle of Marengo—he was assassinated in Cairo by a Syrian student, a young man named Sulayman al-Halabi, who stabbed him through the heart in the gardens of his palace.

The command passed to General Menou, an incompetent who had converted to Islam, married an Egyptian woman, and lost any realistic sense of the situation. Under his direction, the French army held out for another year—a year of increasing isolation, dwindling supplies, and desperate hopes for a relief that never came. The British landed an army under Sir Ralph Abercromby in March 1801, defeated the French at the Battle of Alexandria, and drove the survivors into a series of fortresses from which they could not escape. One by one, the French positions surrendered. On the 2nd of September 1801, the last French soldiers in Egypt laid down their arms.

They were allowed to return to France with their weapons and their honour—terms far more generous than those that had been offered at El Arish, but by then circumstances had changed. Marengo had been fought and won; the Treaty of Lunéville had made peace on the Continent; the French troops would no longer go to swell the armies of Britain’s allies. The war had moved on, and Egypt had become a side-show.

The Shadow Across the Descent

The Egyptian campaign was the most singular military expedition of the age. It was at once a strategic conception of extraordinary brilliance—the attack on British India through the Middle East—and a catastrophic miscalculation of the fundamental realities of sea power. Bonaparte had defeated every army that opposed him, from the Mamelukes at the Pyramids to the Turks at Aboukir. He had founded an institute, assembled a scientific commission, surveyed the monuments of an ancient civilisation, and laid the foundations of modern Egyptology. But he had not been able to secure the sea.

The campaign’s most enduring legacy was not military but intellectual. The Description de l’Égypte, the massive multi-volume work produced by the savants, opened the ancient civilisation of the Nile to European understanding as it had never been opened before. The Rosetta Stone, discovered in the ruins of a fort, provided the key to the decipherment of hieroglyphs. The Institute of Cairo, though short-lived, established a model for the intersection of scholarship and empire that would shape European relations with the East for generations.

Politically, the campaign transformed Bonaparte’s career. He had left France as a general of the Republic, one among several. He returned as the saviour of the nation, the man who had brought glory to French arms even in defeat, the only leader who could rescue France from the chaos of the Directory. The failures of the Syrian campaign, the abandonment of his army, the loss of the fleet—all were forgotten in the intoxication of his return. Within a month of landing at Fréjus, he was master of France.

For the army he abandoned, the judgment was harsher. Kléber’s letter to the Directory was a damning epitaph for the entire enterprise: “Since that period everything has changed; and peace with the Porte is, in my opinion, the only expedient that holds out to us a method of fairly getting rid of an enterprise no longer capable of attaining the object for which it was undertaken.” The Army of the East, the finest soldiers France had produced, had been locked away in a land they could not leave, to fight battles that decided nothing, for a general who had sailed home to seize a throne.
