The Fall of the Girondins

I. The Gathering Storm

From the opening of the National Convention in September 1792, the Republic had been divided against itself. The great party that had led the Revolution through the fall of the monarchy and the trial of the King — the Girondins, named for the department of the Gironde that furnished so many of their leading voices — found themselves face to face with an adversary they had helped create. The Mountain, as the radical deputies from the upper benches of the Convention were called, represented the insurrectionary power of the Paris sections, the Commune that had risen on the night of 9–10 August, and the political clubs where men like Robespierre, Danton, and Marat had forged their ascendancy.

The conflict between the two factions was not a disagreement over first principles. Both sides were republican; both had voted the death of Louis XVI; both believed in the sovereignty of the people. The quarrel was about the location of that sovereignty. For the Girondins, the Republic was one and indivisible, and its will was expressed through the elected representatives of the nation assembled in the Convention. For the Mountain, the Republic was to be found in the people in motion — in the sections of Paris, in the Jacobin Club, in the Commune that had saved France in the September days. Between these two visions no compromise was possible.

By the spring of 1793 the situation had become untenable. The war was going badly. Dumouriez, the general whom the Girondins had trusted and the Mountain had suspected, had defected to the Austrians after his defeat at Neerwinden, taking with him a cavalry regiment and leaving the northern frontier exposed. The charge of treason fell indiscriminately on all who had supported him. The Vendée had risen in open rebellion. The assignat had lost three-quarters of its face value. Paris was hungry, and the hunger was blamed on the profiteers and the politicians who protected them.

The Mountain had learned how to mobilise the city’s desperation. From the tribune of the Jacobin Club, from the columns of Marat’s Ami du Peuple and Hébert’s Père Duchesne, the cry went up that the Girondins were federalists who wished to break the Republic into independent provinces, royalists in republican disguise, agents of Pitt and Coburg who were paid in English gold. The charges were not true — Buzot, one of the most hunted of the proscribed, later refuted them line by line in his memoirs, tracing the Girondin record from the States-General through the Convention and asking at each point where the conspiracy was supposed to have been. But the truth mattered less than the psychology of a city at war, and the Mountain understood psychology better than its opponents.

The Girondins made mistakes. They were brilliant in debate — Vergniaud’s speeches were read and admired across France — but they were poor organisers of the street. They trusted the departments to sustain them, and the departments were far away. They believed in the force of constitutional argument against men who had already demonstrated that they did not recognise constitutional limits. The Mountain, by contrast, worked the machinery of Paris with relentless precision: the Commune, the sections, the Jacobin network, the patriotic press — everything that could apply pressure to the Convention was under their control or their influence.

In March 1793, a first attempt was made. The conspiration du 10 mars — a coordinated plan by the Cordeliers and the Commune to purge the Convention — failed when the sections proved unwilling to move. Louvet, who had been watching the preparations, published a pamphlet exposing the conspiracy, which he described as his political testament, convinced he would not survive. The failure taught the Mountain a lesson: the next assault would need more preparation, more co-ordination, and a greater show of necessity.

In April, the Girondins struck back. They created the Commission of Twelve — a special committee charged with investigating the activities of the Commune and the sections, particularly the conspiracies that surrounded the March events. The Commission began summoning witnesses, demanding documents, and exposing the network of agitation that the Commune had built. It was the most serious threat the Mountain had faced since the Convention opened. The Commission of Twelve was doing precisely what the Girondins had always done best: using the forms of parliamentary procedure to attack the centres of extra-parliamentary power.

But the forms of parliamentary procedure could not stop the tocsin.

On the night of 24 May, the Commission of Twelve ordered the arrest of Jacques Hébert, the editor of Père Duchesne and one of the most powerful voices of the Parisian sans-culottes. It was a catastrophic miscalculation. Hébert was not merely a journalist; he was the tribune of the Commune, the spokesman of the sections, the man whose violent prose had made the Père Duchesne the most widely read newspaper in revolutionary Paris. Arresting him was like arresting the voice of the street itself. Two days later the Commission added Jean-François Varlet, a young agitator from the Cordeliers who had helped organise the 10 March demonstration. The Commune responded by summoning the sections to permanent session. The tocsin was about to sound.

II. The Thirty-First of May

The morning of Friday, 31 May 1793, began with the church bells of Paris. Not the ordinary call to mass, but the tocsin — the rapid, insistent clangour that signalled danger and summoned citizens to arms. From the towers of Notre-Dame, from Saint-Germain-l’Auxerrois, from every church in every section of the city, the bells rang. Drums beat the générale. The gates of Paris were closed. The insurrection that had been brewing for weeks was now in the open.

At the Hôtel de Ville, the insurrectionary Commune — the same body that had organised the August 10 uprising and the September Massacres — reconstituted itself under the direction of Pache, the mayor, and the revolutionary committees that had been preparing for this day. The sections were called to arms. The National Guard was summoned to the Place de la Bastille and the Place de la Révolution. By midday, Paris was under the control of the insurrection.

At the Tuileries, where the Convention sat in the Salle du Manège, the deputies arrived through streets lined with armed men. The galleries were packed with spectators from the sections, many of them armed, many of them drunk. The Commune had submitted its demands in advance: the dissolution of the Commission of Twelve, the arrest of twenty-two named Girondin deputies, and the establishment of a revolutionary army of sans-culottes to enforce the will of the people. The petitioners filed into the Convention hall and stood at the bar, presenting their case to the deputies under the eyes of the crowd in the galleries.

The Convention debated through the afternoon. The Girondins, those who had come, defended themselves. Vergniaud rose to speak, his great voice filling the hall, but the roar from the galleries drowned him out. Robespierre, from the Mountain, demanded that the accused deputies be heard — a tactical move that gave him the appearance of moderation while the pressure from outside did his work.

Madame Roland, who had watched the whole drama of the Revolution from her husband’s side, was at home on the rue de la Harpe when the tocsin sounded. She was preparing to leave Paris with her daughter — her passports had been delayed by the zealous Maratists of her section, who considered her suspect. At half-past five in the evening, six armed men arrived at her door with a warrant from the revolutionary committee for the arrest of Jean-Marie Roland. Her husband, the former Minister of the Interior, refused to recognise the authority. “I know no law that constitutes the authority you cite,” he told them, “and I will not comply with orders emanating from it. If you employ violence, I can only oppose you with the resistance of a man of my age.”

The officer withdrew to consult his superiors, leaving his men at the door. Roland immediately understood that he needed to denounce this publicly. His wife wrote a letter to the Convention president, called a cab, and went alone.

The Tuileries courtyard was full of armed men. She passed through them “jumping like a bird.” At the Convention doors, closed and guarded, she used a trick: adopting the tone of a devout Robespierrist, she told the guards that she had important notes for the president, that the safety of the country depended on their delivery. The door opened. Inside the petitioners’ hall she waited an hour for Vergniaud, who finally came. He told her plainly: “In the state the Assembly is in, I cannot flatter you, and you can barely hope. The Convention can no longer do anything.”

It was the verdict of the evening, delivered by the greatest orator of the Gironde to the wife of its most prominent minister. The Convention could no longer do anything.

By nightfall, the Convention had conceded what it could to save itself. It dissolved the Commission of Twelve. It promised to consider the other demands. The Girondins believed, for a few hours, that they had bought time. But the insurrection had not been launched to extract concessions. It had been launched to break the Girondins, and broken they would be.

III. The First of June: The Incomplete Journée

Saturday, 1 June, was a day of suspended animation. The Convention met, but under the same armed pressure as the day before. The sections had not demobilised. The Commune had not recalled its troops. The tocsin could ring again at any moment.

The Girondins who had not fled remained in the Assembly. Some had spent the night in hiding — Buzot had been warned the night before that his name was on the arrest lists, and had gone before dawn to warn his colleague Grangeneuve. The twenty-two deputies whom the Commune had proscribed were still, for the moment, free men.

The Convention voted to confirm the dissolution of the Twelve and to place the armed force of Paris under the joint command of the Department and the Commune, a concession that effectively legalised the insurrection. The Mountain pushed for more, but the Plain — the uncommitted deputies who held the balance — hesitated. A full journée, with the arrest of the proscribed deputies, would require a larger show of force than the sections had yet produced.

The Commune understood. That night, the revolutionary committees met to prepare the final act.

Hébert, released from his brief imprisonment, appeared before the Commune and demanded that the sections be called out again, in greater numbers, with artillery. Hanriot, the commander of the National Guard — a man of violent temper and absolute loyalty to the Commune — was ordered to assemble every available battalion for the following morning. The cannons were to be brought from the Place de la Bastille to the Place du Carrousel, where they would command the approaches to the Convention hall itself.

IV. The Second of June: The Cannons at the Carrousel

Sunday, 2 June, was the day the Republic changed forever.

The morning broke clear and hot. By eight o’clock, the Place du Carrousel and the courtyard of the Tuileries were filled with National Guardsmen — between sixty and eighty thousand men, by some estimates, with cannon batteries trained on the entrances to the Convention hall. Hanriot rode among them on a black horse, a sabre in his hand, his face flushed with drink and triumph. The sections had obeyed the Commune’s summons: the Faubourg Saint-Antoine had marched in full strength, the sans-culottes of the Cordeliers had brought their pikes, the revolutionary committees had sent their armed detachments. Paris was under siege from within.

The deputies made their way to the Convention through lines of bayonets. The Mountain arrived early, filling their benches with the satisfaction of men who knew the day was won. The Girondins came in smaller numbers; some had already gone into hiding, warned by friends in the Commune that the decree of arrest would be presented to the Convention that day. Those who came knew they were walking into a trap.

The session opened at the usual hour. The Mountain demanded action: the Convention had temporised long enough; the people were demanding the arrest of the traitors. A new petition was presented, more peremptory than the one of 31 May. The names were read: twenty-seven deputies of the Gironde, joined by two ministers — Roland and Clavières — were to be placed under arrest and confined to their houses, pending judgment by the Convention.

The debate that followed was not a debate but a death sentence pronounced in parliamentary form. Vergniaud spoke again, but his voice carried no force against the roar of the galleries and the rumble of the cannons outside. Buzot rose and attempted to speak, but the president — the pacifist and mathematician Condorcet, who had himself been named in the proscription and was now presiding — could not restore order. The Mountain voted as a bloc; the Plain, under the visible pressure of the armed sections, fell silent or voted with the majority. The decree was passed, or was about to pass.

Then the Convention made a desperate gesture. A motion was proposed: the deputies should prove their freedom by leaving the hall and returning. If the armed force allowed them to pass, the decree would be reconsidered. If it did not, the Republic would know that its representatives were prisoners.

The entire Convention rose and walked to the doors.

Hanriot was waiting. When the deputies reached the entrance to the Salle du Manège, they found it blocked by a line of National Guards with fixed bayonets. The president of the Convention demanded passage. The guards did not move. Hanriot was called for. He appeared on his black horse, glanced at the deputies, and gave the answer that would be remembered as long as the Revolution was remembered: “I have no orders from the people to let the Convention leave. You may return to your posts.”

The deputies stood in the doorway, the representatives of the French nation, trapped by the armed force that was supposed to obey them. Some of the Mountain — Danton among them, according to some accounts — urged the crowd to let the deputies pass, but the gesture was theatrical; the guns were already aimed, and the order to fire would come from the Commune, not from the Convention. The deputies turned back. They returned to their seats. The decree of arrest was voted.

Twenty-nine deputies were named for house arrest: the leaders of the Gironde — Brissot, Vergniaud, Gensonné, Guadet, Buzot, Barbaroux, Louvet, Pétion, Salles, and the others who had formed the intellectual and political command of the party. Roland and Clavières, the former ministers, were included. Two of the ministers of the current government, Lebrun and Clavières, were also arrested. The decree ordered them confined to their homes under the guard of the National Guard, pending the presentation of formal charges.

V. The Flight and the Proscription

For the twenty-nine, the decree of house arrest was a sentence of death thinly disguised. The Revolutionary Tribunal had been established in March, its juries packed with September massacrists and its procedure designed to produce convictions. Brissot, Vergniaud, Gensonné, and the others who obeyed the decree and remained in their houses would be tried in due course, convicted, and executed. Some of them understood this and accepted it with the stoicism of men who had already lived through more than they had expected.

Others chose flight.

Buzot, Barbaroux, Louvet, Pétion, and several of their colleagues escaped from house arrest in the days following the decree. They made their way out of Paris separately, threading through the barriers and the National Guard patrols with the help of friends, false papers, and the confusion that followed the coup. Buzot went first to Évreux, his home department, where the local authorities had risen in protest against the Convention’s violation. Louvet followed a more circuitous route, through the countryside of the Loiret, travelling at night and sleeping in ditches, making for Caen.

The flight of the proscribed deputies was the beginning of the federalist revolt. In Caen, the departmental administration of Calvados declared itself in resistance to the Convention. The proscribed deputies who had gathered there — Buzot, Barbaroux, Louvet, Pétion, Guadet, and others — formed an assembly of resistance and issued a proclamation drafted by Louvet himself, calling on all departments to rise against the usurpation of the national representation. The “Manifesto of Caen,” as it came to be called, was a republican document — it denounced the Mountain, not the Republic — but it was immediately branded by the Jacobins as a federalist call for the dissolution of the Republic.

Military force was assembled under General Wimpfen. Volunteers from Normandy, Brittany, and the Bordelais began to gather. For a few weeks, the resistance seemed a genuine threat to the Convention’s authority. From Caen to Bordeaux, from Lyon to Marseilles, the departments that had sent the Girondins to the Convention now mobilised to defend them.

The resistance collapsed almost as soon as it began. The army raised by Wimpfen was defeated at Pacy-sur-Eure in July 1793 by a smaller but more determined force of Convention troops. The Breton volunteers, urged by their primary assemblies to accept the new constitution, melted away. The Norman departments submitted one by one. The federalist revolt in Lyon was crushed in October after a siege of two months. Bordeaux fell without a fight. Marseilles was taken by counter-revolutionary forces that turned out to be more royalist than federalist, discrediting the entire enterprise.

Louvet wrote the epitaph of the federalist resistance from his hiding place in the Jura. He was honest about the failure. The property-owning republicans of the provinces were fundamentally unwilling to leave their homes and fight. They sent hired men whose loyalty could be bought. Meanwhile, the Mountain — ardent, audacious, inured to crime — sent its emissaries into every department, offering provincial compliance the name of patriotism. “The most formerly proud cities,” Louvet wrote, “began to fall before two or three Jacobins.”

VI. The Convention Under the Mountain

With the Girondins proscribed and the federalist resistance crushed, the Mountain was supreme in the Convention. The Republic that the Girondins had imagined — a republic of laws, of departments, of constitutional checks — gave way to the republic of the Committee of Public Safety. The Constitution of 1793, drafted by the Girondin-dominated Convention in the spring, was ratified by a national referendum in August — a document of advanced democracy, universal male suffrage, the right of insurrection, the right to subsistence. It was suspended before it could come into force. The Republic would be revolutionary or it would not survive.

The decree of 2 June had named twenty-nine deputies. The Convention, under Montagnard pressure, expanded the proscription. Seventy-three Girondin deputies who had signed protests against the coup were placed under arrest. Others, like Condorcet, went into hiding. The prisons filled with the men and women who had made the Revolution of 1789: the Rolands, the Brissots, the Vergniauds, the widows and wives of the fallen.

The departments that had resisted were punished. Lyon was bombarded, its finest buildings demolished, its name changed to Ville-Affranchie. The representatives-on-mission — Collot d’Herbois, Fouché, Fréron, Carrier — were given extraordinary powers to purge, to requisition, and to kill. The guillotine, which had been a Parisian instrument of justice, became a national machine of terror.

VII. The Trial of the Girondins

On 3 October 1793, the Committee of General Security, through its reporter Amar, presented the indictment against the proscribed Girondin deputies. The charges were comprehensive and impossible: conspiracy against the unity of the Republic, correspondence with the enemies of France, royalism, federalism, corruption by foreign gold. Twenty-one of the arrested deputies were brought before the Revolutionary Tribunal. Brissot, Vergniaud, Gensonné, Guadet, and the others who had remained in Paris — or been recaptured — stood together in the dock.

The trial began on 24 October and lasted six days. The prosecution called witnesses; the accused defended themselves with the eloquence that had once made them the masters of the Convention. Vergniaud spoke for hours, dismantling the charges one by one. But the verdict had been decided before the trial began. The jury — chosen from the same pool of sans-culottes that had manned the September tribunals — deliberated for less than an hour. The verdict was unanimous: guilty. The sentence was death.

On 31 October 1793, the twenty-one Girondins were led from the Conciergerie to the tumbrils. They sang the Marseillaise as they passed through the streets of Paris. They had been proscribed as federalists who wished to break the Republic; they died singing the song that had become the anthem of the Republic itself. At the foot of the scaffold, the singing faltered, stopped, resumed, and faltered again as the blade fell. One by one they died: Brissot, who had been the theorist of the abolitionist movement and the founder of the Société des Amis des Noirs; Vergniaud, whose eloquence had been the glory of the Convention; Gensonné, the cold logician of the Gironde; Guadet, the impetuous tribune of the Bordelais. They had governed France for a year and a half. They had led the Republic through the trial of the King. They had fallen because they believed that the Revolution could be constitutional in a time when constitutional forms had become impossible.

Valazé, one of the condemned, had already taken his own life in the courtroom when the verdict was read. The tribunal condemned his corpse to the guillotine anyway.

VIII. The Fate of the Fugitives

The fugitives lasted longer.

Buzot, Pétion, Barbaroux, Louvet, and the others who had fled to Caen and then to Bordeaux scattered after the collapse of the federalist resistance. Some made their way to the Gironde — the wine country that had given their party its name — where sympathetic republicans sheltered them. At Saint-Émilion, Buzot, Pétion, and Barbaroux were hidden in a house that became their prison as surely as the Conciergerie was the prison of the condemned. They could not leave; they could not show their faces; they lived in a few rooms, cooking their own meals, waiting for news that never came and could not have been good.

For almost a year they survived. Louvet escaped further, crossing to the Jura with his wife Lodoïska, where he wrote his memoirs in a limestone cave in April 1794, expecting to die. “I throw down notes, and that is all,” he wrote. “Do not expect either stylistic concision or abundance of detail. Truly, I am writing neither history nor what is called memoirs. I am recording notes that may help me, if some day of genuine leisure is given to me — or help someone else, if I do not survive.”

He survived. Thermidor — the fall of Robespierre on 27 July 1794 — saved him. The Convention, under Thermidorian pressure, began reversing the proscriptions. Louvet returned to Paris, resumed his seat, and died in 1797, named as French consul to Palermo but not yet installed.

Buzot, Pétion, and Barbaroux were not so fortunate. A first search of Saint-Émilion found nothing. A second, in June 1794, caught them. Barbaroux shot himself through the head — he survived the wound, was arrested, and was guillotined in Bordeaux. Buzot and Pétion fled into the fields around Saint-Émilion. They were found days later, their bodies partly consumed by wolves. Whether they had taken poison, starved, died of exposure, or killed themselves in some other way, no one could say with certainty. They had been the leading men of the Republic. They died in a field, unrecognisable, their papers scattered, their names proscribed from the nation they had helped to found.

Madame Roland died on 8 November 1793, a week after the Girondin deputies. Her husband, Jean-Marie Roland, learning of her execution while in hiding near Rouen, walked into a field on a country road and drove a sword-cane through his own heart. The body was found by a peasant, papers scattered around it. The last of the Roland party was gone.

Charlotte Corday, the young Norman who had assassinated Marat on 13 July 1793 — partly in the hope of saving the Girondins — had gone to the guillotine in the summer, believing her act would turn the tide. Louvet, who had seen her in Caen before she left for Paris, recorded that she never said a word to any of them about her design. He added: if she had consulted them, they would not have directed her toward Marat — they knew he had at most two days left to live from his disease. “Let us humble ourselves before the decrees of Providence,” Louvet wrote. “It wished that Robespierre and his accomplices live long enough to destroy each other.”

IX. The Reckoning

The fall of the Girondins was the single event that made the Terror possible. Before June 1793, the Convention had been a contest between two organised parties, each with a vision of the Republic, each capable of blocking the other. After June 1793, the Mountain had no effective opposition. The Plain, terrified by the fate of the Girondins, voted with the victors. The Committee of Public Safety, created in April as an emergency measure, became the de facto government of France. The Law of Suspects, the Revolutionary Tribunal, the representatives-on-mission, the maximum, the levée en masse — all the instruments of the Terror — followed from the premise that the Mountain had established on the second of June: that the sovereignty of the people was not in the Convention but in the revolutionary movement, and that anyone who opposed that movement was an enemy of the Republic.

The charge of federalism, which had been the Mountain’s most potent weapon against the Girondins, was always a slander. The Girondins had not proposed a federal republic. They had proposed a republic in which the departments would balance the power of Paris — a republic of laws rather than of insurrections. Buzot, in his memoirs, refuted the charge with cold precision: the Girondins never proposed dissolving the Republic into independent parts. The word “federalism” had been deployed, he wrote, to stigmatise any resistance to Parisian sectional power. It worked because it could not be disproved quickly enough, and because in revolutionary times the accusation is as good as the proof.

The Revolution would devour its own. Within a year, the Mountain itself would split into factions — the Dantonists, the Hébertists, the Robespierrists — and each would fall in turn. The men who had ordered the arrest of the twenty-nine Girondins would themselves be arrested, tried, and executed by the same Tribunal they had created for their enemies. Madame Roland, writing from Sainte-Pélagie prison in the summer of 1793, had foreseen it with terrible clarity. “Robespierre jealous rises against Danton,” she wrote. “The Cordeliers and Jacobins are ready to divide. Grand spectacle preparing for us victims: the tigers are going to tear each other apart.”

She did not live to see it, but she was right.

The fall of the Girondins, then, was not merely the victory of one faction over another. It was the moment when the French Revolution abandoned the principle that the law should govern the revolution and accepted, instead, that the revolution could dispense with the law. The Girondins had believed that the Republic could be made by persuasion, by debate, by the slow construction of constitutional forms. The Mountain had demonstrated that the Republic could be made by force, by terror, by the mobilisation of the streets against the halls of power. Both were right, in their way. The Republic was made. But it was not the Republic the Girondins had imagined, and the France that emerged from the Terror was a France in which the men of 1793 — both Girondins and Montagnards — had been consumed by the forces they had unleashed.
