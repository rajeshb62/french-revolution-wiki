The Terror

The Terror (1793–1794) was both a state system and a social condition: a period in which the revolutionary government in Paris wielded arbitrary power through surveillance, denunciation, and the guillotine, and in which the entire society was shaped by the presence of that power. From the prison cell to the fugitive’s hiding place, from the gambling halls of the Palais-Égalité to the chambers of the Revolutionary Tribunal, the Terror pervaded every level of French life.

The Prisoner’s Experience

In the Abbaye and Sainte-Pélagie prisons between August and November 1793, the experience of imprisonment unfolded in ways that revealed the character of the regime. Those arrested faced not merely confinement but a system designed to strip them of every legal protection before delivering them to the guillotine.

The Abbaye and Sainte-Pélagie

On 1 June 1793, the day after the coup of 31 May, the first arrests swept through Paris. One of those taken was a woman arrested on the order of Dorat-Cubières — a minor poet who had once tried to ingratiate himself through flattery when her husband was minister, now wielding power over life and liberty. The arrest order bore his signature, a detail that spoke volumes about the kind of men now holding the power of the state. She examined the warrant, found it defective in form, and refused to accompany the commissioners until they returned with a properly executed order.

From the Abbaye prison, letters were sent to the Committee of General Security demanding to know the grounds of detention. No satisfactory answer ever came. After a brief release came rearrest and transfer to Sainte-Pélagie, a prison that held both common criminals and political prisoners. From her cell there, the factional struggle beginning to consume the Montagnards themselves was visible: Robespierre rising against Danton, the Cordeliers and the Jacobins ready to divide. A grand spectacle was preparing for the victims — the tigers were going to tear each other apart.

Daily Life of the Prisoner

Prison life was a matter of confinement punctuated by interrogation and uncertainty. Writing became the prisoner’s primary occupation and only defence: political notices that would become memoirs, portraits of contemporaries, last writings prepared for the day of trial. In twenty-two days of August 1793, roughly three hundred pages were filled. The regime permitted writing materials, but prisoners had no guarantee that their writings would survive — the Abbaye authorities destroyed some of the earlier papers.

Prisoners were held in common rooms or cells depending on their status and the state of overcrowding. Family members were permitted visits when the authorities allowed. Petitions could be sent to the authorities, but petitioning was itself a form of subordination: it acknowledged the legitimacy of a system that had no legal basis for detaining anyone. The letters sent to the Committee of General Security from both prisons illustrated the prisoner’s attempt to use the forms of law against a government that had abandoned them.

The Revolutionary Tribunal

The Revolutionary Tribunal had been created by the Convention in March 1793 after the abortive Montagnard attempt to seize power known as the conspiration du 10 mars. When the Girondins succeeded in nominating honest men to serve as jurors, Marat intervened directly — he cried counter-revolution, threatened to call the people, had the ballot annulled, and imposed his own list. The jurors who sat in judgment were drawn for the most part from those who had participated in the September Massacres.

The encounter with the Tribunal came in November 1793. The proceeding was a formula of judgment from which the victims were systematically prevented from speaking. The trial of the twenty-two Girondin deputies had already shown the pattern: accused of conspiracy against the Republic, they were condemned to death and executed on the same day. A projected defence that one prisoner prepared was never permitted to be delivered.

The Execution of the Girondins

The execution of the twenty-two Girondin deputies — Brissot, Vergniaud, Guadet, Gensonné, and others — took place on 31 October 1793. From prison the reaction was one not of sympathy but of indictment: the twenty-two were condemned not for their principles but for their failure to act. They had temporised with crime, succumbed shamefully without being pitied. Instead of obeying their tyrants and descending from the Assembly like a timid flock, they should have fallen on the monsters to annihilate them rather than receiving their verdict.

Roland’s Own Trial and Death

The Revolutionary Tribunal delivered its judgment on 18 Brumaire, Year II (8 November 1793), sentencing one of its prisoners to death. The judgment, signed by Vice-President Dumas and three judges — Deliège, Denizot, Subleyras — condemned the accused to death, ordered goods confiscated to the Republic, and required the sentence to be executed within twenty-four hours. That same day, in the Place de la Révolution, the condemned woman was executed. Her last reported words, addressed to the statue of Liberty in the square, were: “Oh Liberty, what crimes are committed in thy name!”

Jean-Marie Roland, learning of her execution while in hiding near Rouen, died by his own hand with a sword-cane beside him.

The Fugitive’s Experience

For those who escaped the initial arrests, the Terror became a year-long chase across the countryside of France — a period of hiding, flight, and gradual loss of every support that society had once provided.

Buzot on the Run

Proscribed after the coup of 31 May–2 June 1793, one group of fugitives escaped Paris and made their way first to Caen in Normandy, where a federalist resistance had formed under the protection of the Calvados department administration. With them went Pétion, Barbaroux, and Louvet. From Caen they witnessed Charlotte Corday’s departure for Paris — she came ostensibly to beg a favour for relatives, but her real motive was to know the founders of the republic for which she was about to devote herself.

After the federalist military force under General Wimpfen was defeated at Pacy-sur-Eure in July 1793, the fugitive deputies scattered. Some made their way south and west, eventually reaching the Gironde and finding shelter in Saint-Émilion. There a sympathetic republican in the wine country hid them: Pétion occupied one bed, Barbaroux lay on a mattress, and another sat in an old armchair; together they cooked their own meals.

The year in hiding was a period of being “buried alive in this dwelling of death.” A first search of Saint-Émilion found nothing. A second search caught them: Barbaroux shot himself through the head, survived the wound, and was arrested and guillotined. Pétion and another were found dead in a field, their bodies partly consumed by wolves. Whether from self-administered poison, starvation, exposure, or some combination was not established with certainty.

Portraits of Marat, Robespierre, and Danton

Written in two phases (1793 and 1794), the fugitive memoirs of this period included sustained character studies of the three men identified as the principal architects of Montagnard domination.

Marat served as the Mountain’s instrument for destroying individual reputations. His denunciations in the Ami du peuple were systematic and deliberate. His acquittal by the Revolutionary Tribunal after a formal accusation in October 1792 made the Convention permanently subject to factional terror — a moment when the institution proved it could not defend its own members.

Robespierre rose from the Jacobin Club to ascendancy in the Convention through tactical patience: he let Marat remain the visible extremist while consolidating his own position. Only after Marat’s assassination by Charlotte Corday did Robespierre drop his cover and move toward dictatorship.

Danton was assessed through his conduct as Minister of Justice after 10 August 1792, his role in the September Massacres, and his management of the Committee of Public Safety as an instrument of factional power rather than national defence. The final verdict on the two: “Danton and Robespierre are the sole authors of the afflictions that have fallen on France.”

Louvet’s Flight from Caen

The most detailed fugitive narrative of the Terror period was written from a limestone cave in the Jura in April 1794. The author and his wife Lodoïska moved through a series of hiding places as the revolutionary authorities tightened their control.

After Caen’s collapse, the fugitives crossed into Brittany, where the Breton deputies and sympathisers who had formed the core of Wimpfen’s force were gradually drawn off by Parisian emissaries bearing news that their primary assemblies had accepted the new constitution. The growing political isolation of the fugitives became stark: people who had been protected by the Girondins for ten months refused to give them shelter for a single night; friends of twenty years drove them from their doors.

From Brittany the fugitives reached Bordeaux and the surrounding wine country of the Gironde. The Gironde remained longer than other provinces in a state of relative republican independence, but Bordeaux’s submission to the Terror narrowed the window. The decision was made to move further east. Eventually they reached the Jura mountains, where they sheltered in a cave for several months — cold, inadequately fed, writing by whatever light was available. “I throw down notes, and that is all,” one wrote. “I am recording notes that may help me if some day of genuine leisure is given to me — or help someone else, if I do not survive.”

Charlotte Corday and Marat’s Assassination

Charlotte Corday came to Caen before the assassination and met the assembled deputies. She never spoke to any of them a word about her design. If she had consulted them, they would not have directed her toward Marat — they knew he had at most two days left to live from his disease. Her portrait was of a woman proud and gentle, decent and beautiful, with a bearing full of assurance and a gaze tempered by modesty. She was seen the evening before she left for Paris.

Corday left the Caen group for Paris, carried out the assassination in Marat’s apartment on 13 July 1793, and was herself executed four days later.

Return After Thermidor

The fall of Robespierre on 9 Thermidor Year II (27 July 1794) ended the immediate danger for the fugitives. The return to Paris was compressed in the writings produced during the hiding, not after. What the records show is the first steps of political rehabilitation. The Convention, under Thermidorian pressure, began reversing the proscriptions. One of the fugitives resumed his journalistic and political career, helped found the journal La Sentinelle in its second form, and represented the moderate republican position through the remaining years of the Convention and the early Directory. Not all survived to return.

Social History of the Terror

Beyond the politics of the Convention and the ordeal of the prisoner lay a different world: the social underworld of prostitution, gambling, and erotic life in revolutionary Paris. This world was not separate from the Terror — it was shaped by it, and the machinery of the revolutionary state eventually reached into it.

Prostitution in Paris Under the Terror

The women who had been passionate patriots in 1789 reverted during the Terror to a different role — lovers of love and nothing more. The street prostitution of Paris under the Terror was a fierce and persistent reality: the prostitutes of the streets, the former fishwives and market women, carrying on traditional behaviours under the new regime. Police surveillance reports, contemporary pamphlets, and archival documents from the Archives nationales reconstruct the everyday reality of women who lived by prostitution.

The Palais-Égalité as a Centre of Vice

The Palais-Royal — renamed Palais-Égalité during the Revolution — became the principal centre of organised prostitution, gambling, and erotic entertainment in revolutionary Paris. The gardens functioned as a vast open-air brothel, with women ranging from elegant courtesans (the “nymphs” and “odalisques”) to common prostitutes advertising at the famous price of “four women for five livres.” The Allée des Soupirs (Alley of Sighs) was the most notorious walk.

The Palais-Royal had passed from Cardinal Richelieu’s original construction through its ownership by Philippe-Égalité, the duc d’Orléans, and its transformation into a commercial and entertainment centre. The warning sign placed in the gardens — “Attention! il y a des filles et des filous ou avis à nos frères des départements!” — gave the flavour of the period: an official notice to provincial visitors that the gardens contained both prostitutes and thieves.

Fashion, Gambling, and Erotic Literature

Women’s fashion during the Terror reflected the proximity of pleasure to death. Earrings were designed “à la guillotine” and the “redingotte à la Thessalie” served as practical attire for hurried gentlemen. The Palais-Égalité housed thirty-two gambling houses, which had roots under the monarchy — the queen’s gaming table, the ambassador as croupier — and underwent a revolutionary transformation that included a card game replacing kings and queens with revolutionary titles.

Erotic literature flourished at the Palais-Égalité. Libelles and pamphlets against Marie-Antoinette circulated widely. The police served as what might be called the last refuge of public modesty, and the Revolutionary Tribunal occasionally turned its attention to erotic publishers.

The Case of Madame de Sainte-Amaranthe

The fatal intersection of sexual license with the machinery of the Terror found its central narrative in the story of Madame de Sainte-Amaranthe and her daughter Émilie. Jeanne-Françoise-Louise de Saint-Simon d’Arpajon, daughter of a governor of Besançon, had become a celebrated courtesan of the Revolution whose salon and establishment in the fashionable demimonde connected the worlds of prostitution and high politics. She was caught up in the “foreign conspiracy” investigations of 1794, arrested with her daughter, tried before the Revolutionary Tribunal presided over by Fouquier-Tinville, and executed in the batch (fournée) of 29 Prairial Year II (17 June 1794). Her case illustrated how the Revolutionary Tribunal constructed conspiracy narratives that could sweep up anyone connected to the demimonde.

The Marquis de Sade Under the Revolution

The Marquis de Sade was present in revolutionary Paris, involved in political clubs including the Section des Piques, and imprisoned under the Terror. He narrowly escaped the guillotine. His life during the revolutionary decade — the intersection of sexual deviance, literary production, and political violence — connected his works (Justine, Juliette, Aline et Valcour) to the world he inhabited.

Police Surveillance

Throughout the Terror, the relationship between the revolutionary government and prostitution was ambiguous. The police both regulated and tolerated the trade, with surveillance reports providing the documentary record. Within the Palais de Justice itself during the Terror, sexual scandals abounded: judicial officials, clerks, prisoners, and prostitutes operated within the precincts of the revolutionary courts.

The Theory of the Terror

In 1797, as the Directory struggled to stabilise the Republic, a new doctrine emerged: that the Terror’s violence had saved the Republic and therefore justified its methods. This claim met a sustained refutation on three grounds.

The Terror Did Not Save the Republic

The factual claim that the Terror saved the Republic was false. The Republic was saved by the armies, by geography, and by the incompetence of the coalition — not by the guillotine. The military victories that secured the Republic’s survival were won by generals and soldiers fighting on the frontiers, not by the Revolutionary Tribunal executing prisoners in Paris. The military defence of the Republic must be separated from the internal terror that accompanied it.

The Precedent Destroys Constitutional Government

Even if the Terror had produced beneficial effects, the claim that it was justified set a precedent that systematically destroyed constitutional government. Any emergency could be used to justify any measure. Once the principle was accepted that the state’s survival justifies the destruction of individuals, there was no logical stopping point. The precedent of the Terror taught that safety comes from arbitrary violence, that enemies can be defined by category rather than by individual act, and that the state’s survival justifies any means.

The Corruption of Political Culture

Beyond the factual and structural arguments lay a deeper corruption: the Terror changed French political culture at the level of expectations and habits. It taught citizens to believe that security could be obtained through violence against other citizens. It habituated the political class to governing through emergency powers. A republic that accepts this teaching as even partially valid has undermined the only foundation on which a durable republic can rest: the rule of law, individual rights, and constitutional procedure.

What harmed France most was precisely the impatience to do good — the willingness of well-intentioned men to use arbitrary measures in pursuit of legitimate ends. When honest men used revolutionary means against revolutionary brigands — seizing émigrés’ property, deporting suspects, suspending legal process — they consecrated illegality by making it serve good purposes, and thereby destroyed the only moral argument against illegality: that it always produces harm.

The General Narrative

A six-volume synthetic history of the Revolution, published between 1799 and 1802, presented the Terror from an anti-Jacobin moderate republican perspective — contemptuous of the aristocracy, scathing toward the Jacobins and Robespierre, and broadly favourable to the Directory and Bonaparte as restorers of order.

The Convention and the Establishment of the Terror

The Convention was established in September 1792, abolishing royalty and proclaiming the Republic. Marat and Robespierre stood as the twin architects of the Terror, each with distinct vices. The Convention’s division into factions — Girondins, Montagnards, and the Plain — was the precondition for the Terror, since it was the Mountain’s determination to dominate the Convention by force that made systematic violence necessary.

The Fall of the Girondins and the 31 May Coup

The coup of 31 May 1793 was not a popular revolution but a criminal conspiracy carried out by the armed sections and the Commune with the support of the Orléanist faction. Charlotte Corday’s assassination of Marat was treated sympathetically — she was portrayed as a heroine, her courage and character praised.

The Committee of Public Safety

The Committee of Public Safety — the “decemvirate” — governed France through the Terror. The successive purges of the factions unfolded in sequence: the Hébertists, the Dantonists, and finally Robespierre’s own following. The decrees of accusation against Camille Desmoulins, Hérault de Séchelles, Danton, and the others were enumerated. The executions of Marie-Antoinette and the Duke d’Orléans took their place in the sequence. The magic and ever-powerful words — “patrie, liberté, salut public, conspiration” — were used to impose on the multitude.

Thermidor

On 8–9 Thermidor, Robespierre’s conspiracy came to an end. Tallien’s speech at the Convention triggered the arrest of Robespierre and his followers. The conspirators were executed, including seventy-one members of the Paris Commune. Among the most infamous representatives-on-mission were Lebon at Arras and Carrier at Nantes, whose drownings Carrier himself eventually disavowed.

The Thermidorian Reaction

The period after Thermidor was a reaction against the Terror rather than a clean break. New terrorist gangs or “égorgeurs” emerged, along with royalist youth groups — the Companies of Jesus and of the Sun. Cultural conflicts erupted over revolutionary songs: the Marseillaise and the Réveil du Peuple. The Babeuf conspiracy represented a final Jacobin attempt to revive the Terror, placing it within the broader sequence of revolutionary upheaval that continued through the Directory.

The Terror as Depicted Across the Sources

Taken together, the sources present the Terror as simultaneously a state system and a social condition — a period in which the revolutionary government claimed the right to define, pursue, and destroy its enemies without legal constraint, and a period in which the entire society was shaped by the presence of that power.

The sources are unanimous that the Terror was not merely a set of institutions — the Revolutionary Tribunal, the Committee of Public Safety, the surveillance committees — but a condition that pervaded every level of French society. The prisoner in her cell, the fugitive in the Jura cave, the prostitute in the Palais-Égalité arcades, and the revolutionary judge in the Palais de Justice all inhabited the same world: a world in which the state could at any moment define any person as an enemy and destroy them, and in which survival depended on invisibility, denunciation of others, or the uncertain protection of factional loyalty.
