Fall of the Bastille

The fall of the Bastille on 14 July 1789 was the first great insurrection of the French Revolution. The royal fortress and prison in the east of Paris, which was also a symbol of royal authority and a store of arms and powder, was stormed by a crowd that had assembled in the hours after Necker’s dismissal became known. The event was not the result of a long-planned assault; it was the accidental product of three converging pressures: the political crisis at Versailles, the spontaneous insurrection of the Paris crowd, and the failure of the military cordon intended to hold the city.

The Background in the Days Before

Paris in the first fortnight of July 1789 was a city in suspension. The States-General had been in session since May. The National Assembly had declared itself in June. The king had responded by concentrating troops around Paris and Versailles — Swiss regiments, German regiments, the loyal Gardes Françaises — and had dismissed the popular minister Necker on 11 July. The dismissal was the spark.

The news arrived at Jefferson’s house on the evening of 11 July: Necker had been dismissed by a letter delivered by hand — he received it at dinner, proposed a walk to his wife, communicated the news on the road, and departed France that night. The minister Montmorin resigned immediately. Camille Desmoulins mounted a table at the Palais-Royal and called on the people to arm.

12 July: The City Breaks Open

The afternoon and evening of Sunday 12 July saw the first acts of public violence. The city broke open: the armourers’ shops were forced open and their weapons distributed; the armouries of the Invalides and of several hôtels were raided; the Monastery of Saint-Lazare was attacked and its grain distributed — each cart accompanied by a friar to assure the crowd it was legitimate. Wooden barriers around the Palais-Royal and the statue of Louis XV were pulled down or burned.

The green cockade appeared — made from the leaves of trees in the Tuileries gardens, the fashion of the day with which everybody was obliged to comply who meant to walk in peace. Carriages stopped running; the city was on foot. Benjamin Franklin’s portrait was paraded through the streets with black crepe, mourning the old king of France and the new king of America.

The military response on 12 July was ineffective. The Prince de Lambesc, commanding the Royal-Allemand cavalry, charged a crowd in the Tuileries gardens; the Gardes Françaises, the regiments of the line stationed in Paris, refused to fire on the people. The Gardes Françaises joined the crowd: the soldiers who had been the instruments of authority overnight became the leaders of the insurrection. The Baron de Besenval, commanding the troops in the Paris region, found he could not rely on his own regiments.

13 July: Organisation and Arms

By Monday 13 July, the insurrection had taken an organised form. The electors of Paris — the men who had chosen the city’s deputies to the States-General — assembled at the Hôtel de Ville and constituted themselves as a Comité Permanent. They authorised the formation of a civic militia (the future National Guard), called for the creation of a municipal government independent of the traditional city authorities, and sent deputations to the National Assembly at Versailles.

The search for arms intensified. The Hôtel des Invalides was approached; its governor refused to surrender its muskets. The crowd spent the day finding weapons where they could — breaking into the City Hall’s own armouries, taking swords and halberds from private collections, and continuing to raid armourers’ shops.

14 July: The Fortress

The morning of Tuesday 14 July saw the main body of the crowd march on the Hôtel des Invalides. The governor, having no orders from Versailles and seeing the crowd’s size, opened the gates. The insurgents took approximately 28,000–30,000 muskets and several cannon from the cellars. There was no powder: the gunpowder had been moved the night before to the Bastille for safekeeping.

The Bastille was commanded by the Marquis de Launay, the governor. The fortress was garrisoned by about eighty to ninety invalides (veteran soldiers unfit for field service) and thirty Swiss Guards. Its walls were thirty feet high; it had eight towers; the ditch around it was eighty feet wide. Its defensive capacity against artillery was limited — it was not a modern fortress — but against a crowd armed with muskets it was formidable.

The negotiations began in the late morning. A deputation from the Hôtel de Ville approached the Bastille, asking de Launay to receive a garrison of the new civic militia. He agreed to receive them but would not surrender the fortress. The crowd began to enter the outer courtyard. The first shots — fired either by the crowd or by the garrison, the sources disagree — killed several people.

The siege proper lasted about four hours. The crowd had brought cannon captured from the Invalides; the Swiss and invalides fired on them from the towers. The crowd, led by members of the Gardes Françaises who knew how to aim the cannon, fired back. De Launay’s position was hopeless: he had no food for a long siege, no reinforcements were coming, and his garrison was willing to open the gates.

The surrender was negotiated through a note pushed through the wall. De Launay agreed to lay down arms if the garrison’s lives were spared. The drawbridge was let down. The crowd poured into the fortress.

The Killing of de Launay and de Flesselles

The surrender agreement was not honoured. De Launay was seized, beaten, stabbed, and shot on the way to the Hôtel de Ville. The crowd demanded his head; a kitchen boy cut it off with a knife as de Launay lay dying. The head was placed on a pike and carried through the streets. (A counter-version of the episode has a young man named Désilles jumping between de Launay and the crowd to protect him, only to be killed himself — but this detail appears in no other account and may be a conflation.)

Jacques de Flesselles, the prévôt des marchands (the head of the city government), had been suspected of deceiving the crowd about the location of arms during the previous days. On the evening of 14 July, he was shot in the Palais-Royal by a young man named Lallée — “what name, what punishment have they preserved for you?” a contemporary commentator remarked. Flesselles’ head was also placed on a pike. The two heads were carried together through the streets, the first such display of the Revolution.

The King’s Reaction: 15–17 July

The news reached Versailles on the night of 14 July. The Duc de Liancourt, the king’s first gentleman of the bedchamber, woke the king and convinced him to go in person to the National Assembly. The king appeared before the Assembly without escort, announced that he had ordered the troops to withdraw from Paris and Versailles, and was received with an extraordinary ovation.

On 17 July, Louis XVI went to Paris. He was met at the city gates by the new mayor Bailly and the new commander of the National Guard, Lafayette, who presented him with the tricolour cockade — red and blue of the city of Paris, white of the monarchy. The king put it in his hat. The crowd shouted “Vive le Roi.”

Morris, who visited the Bastille on 17 July with Lafayette, thought the day would prove a useful lesson for the rest of the king’s life — “but he is so weak that unless he be kept out of bad Company it is impossible that he should not act wrongly.”

What the Bastille Contained

The Bastille at the time of its fall held only seven prisoners: four men convicted of forgery, two insane men (one English, one French), and the Comte de Solages, imprisoned on his family’s request for a particularly atrocious crime. The discovery that the symbol of royal despotism held so few prisoners — and none of them the political prisoners of the lettre de cachet — became part of the early revolutionary mythology: the Bastille’s evil was symbolic rather than practical, but the symbolism was enough.

The Consequences

The fall of the Bastille had an immediate and durable effect on the Revolution’s direction. The king’s acceptance of the tricolour cockade and his visit to Paris signalled the collapse of the court’s resistance to the early reforms of the National Assembly. The establishment of the Paris Commune and the National Guard gave the insurrection a permanent institutional form. The émigration of the king’s younger brother the Comte d’Artois and of the leading conservatives of the court — the Polignacs, the Prince de Condé — began within days. And the killing of de Launay and de Flesselles established the pattern: the Revolution’s first great victory was also its first public execution.
