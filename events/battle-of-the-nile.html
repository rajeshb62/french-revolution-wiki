The Battle of the Nile

The Great Pursuit

Throughout the spring and summer of 1798, the Mediterranean had been the theatre of a vast and uncertain chase. On the 19th of May, Admiral Brueys had sailed from Toulon with the greatest armament France had ever assembled: thirteen ships of the line, fourteen frigates, and over four hundred transports, carrying the Army of the East under General Bonaparte. Their destination was Egypt. Their purpose was the conquest of the Orient and the destruction of British power in India.

The British fleet, meanwhile, had scarcely a frigate to its name in those waters. Lord St. Vincent, commanding off Cadiz, had sent his most aggressive subordinate into the Mediterranean with a small squadron of three ships of the line, later reinforced to fourteen. Rear-Admiral Sir Horatio Nelson was charged with a mission that seemed almost absurd in its disproportion: to find and destroy the French armada before it could accomplish its design.

What followed was a drama of missed connections that has no parallel in naval history. Nelson reached the neighbourhood of Toulon on the 17th of May, only to be scattered by a gale that dismasted his flagship and nearly drove him onto the Italian coast. While he refitted, Bonaparte slipped past with his entire fleet. Nelson guessed the destination correctly—he reasoned that Malta must be the first objective, Egypt the ultimate one—but he could not prove it, for he had no frigates to scout ahead, no fast vessels to run down the elusive rumours that crossed his path. He was a blind man searching the vastness of the inland sea.

The French seized Malta on the 12th of June, the ancient fortress of the Knights of St. John capitulating after a brief show of force. Bonaparte stayed six days, reorganised the government, confiscated the treasury, and sailed eastward. Nelson, receiving intelligence of the French departure from Malta, pressed on for Egypt. He reached Alexandria on the 28th of June and found no French. Concluding that he had been mistaken—that the French must be bound for Constantinople or the Black Sea—he turned northward in frustration, searching the coasts of Asia Minor and the southern shore of Crete. Two days after his departure, Bonaparte’s fleet arrived off Alexandria. The French army landed on the 1st of July, and within a week Bonaparte had stormed the city and begun his march on Cairo. The French fleet, its troops disembarked, was free to choose its anchorage.

The Dispositions at Aboukir

Admiral François-Paul Brueys d’Aigalliers was a brave officer, but he suffered from a vacillating temperament that the responsibilities of high command only exacerbated. Bonaparte had given him explicit orders: take the fleet into the old harbour of Alexandria if possible; if not, find a defensible anchorage in Aboukir Bay; failing both, make for Corfu. The preservation of the fleet was essential, for the army’s survival in Egypt depended upon it.

Brueys ordered soundings. The old harbour of Alexandria, it was reported on the 18th of July, could indeed accommodate the ships of the line, but the admiral hesitated. The winds were capricious, the passage narrow, and the season advanced. He chose instead the easier alternative: the open roadstead of Aboukir Bay, fifteen miles east of Alexandria.

The Bay of Aboukir stretches from the promontory of the same name to the Rosetta mouth of the Nile, a distance of some eighteen miles. In its western portion, where Brueys moored his fleet, the shore recedes in a long tongue of sand behind which lies the shallow Lake Madieh. North-east from Aboukir Point stretches a line of shoals and rocks, rising at two and a half miles into a small island—since known as Nelson’s Island—and continuing for another mile and a quarter. Behind this four-mile barrier, the fleet found shelter from the prevailing north-west summer wind.

Here Brueys established his line. His thirteen ships of the line were moored in a column running north-west to south-east, their broadsides turned to the sea. The north-western ship—the van, the one most exposed to the wind and to any approaching enemy—was anchored a mile and a half south-east of Aboukir Island, in five fathoms of water. This was the critical error. She should have been moored so close to the shoal that no ship could pass around her. Instead, a gap was left, and the few guns the French had placed on the island were far too light to cover it. The intervals between ships were wide, and the line itself was bent at the rear, but not sufficiently to bring the rear ships into shoal water. The flank that should have been impregnable was open to any captain bold enough to attempt the passage.

Brueys seems to have believed two things that would prove fatal: that no enemy would risk the unknown shoals on the inshore side, and that no attack would be attempted after nightfall. He had anchored his fleet for defence against a conventional approach from the seaward side, and his guns were run out only on that side. The landward guns remained stowed, their ports blocked by the cables that held the ships to their anchors. The assumption was natural, but it was founded on premises that proved disastrously false.

Brueys himself seems to have expected that the attack, if it came, would fall upon his rear. He had written to the minister of marine expressing more fear for that part of his order than for the van. The French fleet had been at anchor for three weeks when, on the 1st of August, the lookout sighted sails on the western horizon. A British squadron of fourteen ships of the line was bearing down upon them under a press of sail.

Nelson’s Resolution

The evening before, Nelson had sent the Alexander and Swiftsure ahead to reconnoitre, expecting to make Alexandria the following day. It was a measure born of necessity—he had no frigates, and the main body could not afford to be caught unprepared. When, at one in the afternoon, the British fleet sighted the French line lying at anchor in Aboukir Bay, Nelson did not hesitate for a moment. There was no council of war, no weighing of risks. The sun was already falling toward the horizon. The waters were unknown—the only chart in the entire British fleet was a rough sketch taken from a captured merchant vessel. The shoals that protected the French position were uncharted. Yet Nelson made the signal to prepare for battle and to anchor by the stern.

It was a decision that ranks among the most audacious in naval history. To attack a fleet at anchor in a defended bay, with night falling and the ground uncharted, was to stake everything on the quality of his captains and the fighting spirit of his crews. Nelson had been thinking of this moment throughout the long weeks of his pursuit. He had discussed the problem with his captains, sounded their views, prepared their minds. Now he trusted them to execute what he could not prescribe from the flagship.

As the British ships rounded Aboukir Island, Nelson hailed Captain Hood of the Zealous and asked whether he thought they were far enough eastward to clear the shoal. Hood replied that he would bear up, sounding as he went, and that the other ships keeping outside him would be safe. Thus the fleet was piloted into action—practical seamanship of the highest order, performed under the eyes of an enemy already opening fire.

The Attack

The leading British ship, the Goliath under Captain Foley, was the first to show what Nelson had intended. Instead of anchoring on the seaward side of the French line—the obvious course, the one the French had prepared for—Foley steered his ship ahead of the French column and crossed the bows of the leading French ship, the Guerrier. He intended to anchor on her inner side, but his anchor hung for a moment, and he brought up instead on the quarter of the second French ship, the Conquérant. Hood, following close in the Zealous, anchored precisely on the bow of the Guerrier.

So the battle began. The French van was caught between two fires: the British ships on its inner flank, firing from the shore side, and the British ships that followed, anchoring on the seaward side. Within five minutes of the first exchange—just as the sun was sinking below the horizon—the foremast of the Guerrier crashed over the side. Three cheers rang across the British line.

The Orion swept wide around the two leading ships, passing between them and the shore, and anchored on the inner side of the fifth French ship, the Peuple Souverain. The Theseus brought up abreast the third, the Spartiate, having passed between the Goliath and Zealous and their antagonists. The Audacious steered between the Guerrier and the Conquérant and anchored on the bow of the latter, already engaged on the quarter by the Goliath. Five British ships were now inside the French line—Goliath, Zealous, Orion, Theseus, and Audacious—doubling on the van. The French had never conceived that ships could pass between their line and the shoals; the landward guns were useless, the crews unable to train their pieces on the enemy that had appeared from the one quarter they had thought secure.

Nelson himself, in the Vanguard, was sixth in the order of battle. He had deliberately allowed the leading ships to pass ahead of him, calculating that by the time his flagship entered the bay the shoals would be well marked and the enemy’s attention fully engaged. He anchored on the seaward side of the third French ship, the Spartiate, already engaged on her inner side by the Theseus. It was a deliberate choice: he could have followed Foley’s example and taken the inner passage, but he anchored outside instead, signalling by his example what he expected of those who came after him. The leading ships doubled the van; the remainder would double the centre.

The French, caught utterly unprepared, fought with desperate valour. Hundreds of their men were ashore fetching water when the British sails appeared; most of them never returned to their ships. Brueys himself, who had clung to the hope that the enemy would not attack at night, faced the catastrophe with a courage that redeemed the hesitations of the previous weeks. His flagship, the Orient, a three-decker of one hundred and twenty guns—the largest vessel in either fleet—became the focal point of the British assault.

The Destruction of the Orient

For half an hour Brueys was a helpless spectator of the destruction of his van, watching from the quarterdeck of the Orient as ship after ship in the leading division was battered into submission. Then the British centre came up, and he had his own battle to fight.

The Bellerophon, a seventy-four, anchored abreast of the Orient and opened fire. The disparity in force was enormous—a seventy-four against a hundred-and-twenty—but the Bellerophon fought with a fury that astonished even her opponents. For three-quarters of an hour she endured the full weight of the French flagship’s broadside, losing her main and mizzen masts. When she could no longer sustain the punishment, her cable was cut and she drifted out of action. As she passed down the French line, her foremast fell, and the successive broadsides of the French rear raked her helpless hull. Out of a ship’s company of six hundred and fifty men, she had lost forty-nine killed and one hundred and forty-eight wounded.

But the Bellerophon had bought time, and with time came reinforcements. The Swiftsure, arriving after dark, anchored on the starboard bow of the Orient and opened fire. The Alexander followed, anchoring on the port quarter of the three-decker, while the fifty-gun Leander placed herself on the port bow of the Franklin, the next French ship ahead of the Orient, and raked both vessels. The French centre was now caught in a concentration of fire as devastating as that which had overwhelmed the van.

The Culloden, under Captain Troubridge—Nelson’s most trusted captain—had been separated from the main body and, attempting to round the shoal in the failing light, ran hard aground on its outer extremity at a quarter before seven. There she stuck, despite all efforts, until two o’clock the next morning, when the battle was already decided. Yet even in disaster she served the fleet: her wreck became a beacon warning the Alexander and Swiftsure clear of the same fate.

At about nine in the evening, flames were seen rising from the stern of the Orient. The cause has never been determined with certainty—paint, tar, the combustibles of a ship cleared for action, a chance spark from the British guns. The fire spread with terrifying speed, and the British, recognizing the danger of proximity to a burning ship, ceased their attack on the flagship and concentrated their efforts on saving themselves.

Brueys himself was spared the sight of his flagship’s end. He had been twice wounded already; at half-past eight, a cannonball carried away his left leg at the thigh. He refused to be taken below. He died calmly on his quarterdeck moments before the fire broke out.

At ten o’clock, the Orient blew up. The explosion was heard miles inland, a thunderclap of such violence that it seemed to shake the very sea. A profound silence fell across the bay, broken only by the crackling of wreckage and the cries of drowning men. Then the battle resumed. Captain Casabianca, commander of the Orient, went down with his ship, and with him his young son—a story that would pass into legend.

The Morning After

Five French ships ahead of the Orient had already surrendered before the explosion. The eighty-gun Franklin struck her flag before midnight. When dawn broke on the 2nd of August, six ships of the French line still flew their colours, but they had drifted far astern in the confusion of the night—their cables cut by shot, their commanders uncertain whether to fight or flee.

The British, repairing their damages through the night, attacked again at first light. Four of the remaining French ships—the Tonnant, Mercure, Heureux, and Timoléon—ran ashore to avoid destruction. The first three hauled down their colours; the fourth was burned by her own officers.

Only two ships of the line escaped: the Guillaume Tell, bearing the flag of Rear-Admiral Villeneuve, and the Généreux. With them fled the frigates Diane and Justice. They slipped away to the south-east as the morning light revealed the scale of the catastrophe, carrying news of the disaster to France.

Of the thirteen French ships of the line that had anchored in Aboukir Bay on the 1st of August, eleven were destroyed or captured. Of the four frigates, one was sunk, one captured, and two escaped with Villeneuve. The French lost some three thousand five hundred men killed, wounded, or drowned, among them their commander-in-chief and three captains. The British loss was two hundred and eighteen killed and six hundred and seventy-eight wounded. Nelson himself was among the casualties: a heavy splinter struck him in the head, and for a time he believed the wound to be mortal. It disabled him for several days. “I think,” he wrote four weeks later, “that if it had pleased God I had not been wounded, not a boat would have escaped.”

The Consequences

The Battle of the Nile was not merely a victory; it was an annihilation. Within the space of a single night, the entire strategic architecture of Bonaparte’s eastern expedition had been swept away. The French fleet, which was to have secured the army’s communications with France and overawed the Ottoman Porte, had ceased to exist. The Mediterranean, which had been a French lake at the beginning of the year, passed utterly into British hands.

For Mahan, writing a century later, the Nile was the most decisive naval battle of the period—the battle that made Waterloo possible. Its strategic consequences radiated across every theatre of the war. The command of the sea, which France had contested since 1793, passed irrevocably to Great Britain, and with it the power to determine where and when the next war would be fought.

The blow struck home in every quarter. The Ottoman Porte, which had been hesitating before the prestige of French arms, declared war on France within weeks of receiving the news of Aboukir. The Second Coalition—Austria, Russia, Naples, and the Porte—which had been slowly forming through the summer of 1798, now crystallized into active hostility. Czar Paul of Russia, already outraged by the French seizure of Malta, eagerly promised troops for the war. Austria, which had been holding back after Campo Formio, signed a convention with Russia on the 10th of August. The conferences at Rastadt were broken off. The war that had seemed to be drawing to a close in 1797 blazed up anew along the whole frontier of Europe.

For the Army of the East, the consequences were immediate and inescapable. The troops had fought the Battle of the Pyramids on the 21st of July and entered Cairo in triumph. They had believed themselves masters of a new empire. When the news of the naval disaster reached them in August, Paganel records that the soldiers gave way to despair. “Here we are,” they cried, “abandoned in this barbarous country, without communication with home, without hope of return.” The entire enterprise, which had seemed so brilliant at its conception, now revealed itself as a vast trap: the army could conquer but it could not leave, it could win battles but it could not communicate with the nation that had sent it.

Bonaparte met the moment with the iron self-control that was the mark of his genius. “We have then laid on us the obligation to do great things,” he told his men. “Seas which we do not command separate us from home; but no seas divide us from Africa and Asia.” The magical influence he exerted over his soldiers restored their courage—but it could not restore their communications, nor bring news from France, nor conjure supplies from the empty desert. Paganel observes that Bonaparte had chosen to sail for Egypt partly to remove himself from the failures of the Directory; the Nile now made that removal permanent, and his army a garrison marooned in a hostile land.

The blockade of Egypt by British squadrons was established immediately and maintained until the final French surrender in 1801. Malta, where Bonaparte had left a garrison of four thousand men, was invested by the combined forces of the British navy and the Maltese insurgents, and held out only until September 1800. The Ionian Islands, which Bonaparte had intended as a base for the eastern empire, fell to a Russo-Turkish squadron in October 1798.

The Battle of the Nile was the most complete naval victory of the age. Lord Howe, asked to comment on the achievement, remarked that it “stood unparalleled and singular in this instance, that every captain distinguished himself.” The judgment was just. The battle was the product not merely of Nelson’s audacity but of the tactical discipline he had instilled in his captains during the long weeks of the pursuit. The combination of doubling the enemy’s line, of passing inside the shoals to bring the French between two fires, of trusting subordinates to act on their own initiative—these were the marks of a new school of naval warfare, one that would find its ultimate expression seven years later at Trafalgar.

For France, the defeat was a wound from which the navy never fully recovered. The Directory, which had counted on the Egyptian expedition to secure its eastern ambitions, saw its entire Mediterranean position collapse in a single night’s fighting. The Army of the East was cut off and stranded, its general reduced to conquering a desert he could not leave, its soldiers condemned to fight battles that decided nothing, for the sea that should have carried them home was held by their enemies. Villeneuve, the fugitive of Aboukir, would carry the memory of that night with him through seven years of command, until at Trafalgar he would face Nelson again—and again be beaten.

The Battle of the Nile did not end the war, but it determined the conditions under which the rest of the war would be fought. The command of the sea had passed decisively to Great Britain, and it would not be challenged again for a generation.
