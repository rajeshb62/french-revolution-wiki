The Italian Campaigns 1796-97

The Army of Italy

In the spring of 1796, the French Army of Italy was a collection of starving, ragged, unpaid battalions huddled along the Ligurian coast. The Directory, which governed France from the Luxembourg Palace, had neglected this army for months. Its soldiers wore tattered uniforms and broken shoes; they had not been paid in bread or coin; the artillery horses were dying of famine in the stables of Nice and Savona. The army’s commander had been the elderly General Schérer, a man of the old school whose talents did not extend to the improvisation of victory from the materials of defeat. Schérer had written despairing letters to Paris — his men could not fight, could not march, could barely exist.

The Directory, preoccupied with the more immediate threat of the Rhine and the internal crises of the Republic, had little attention to spare for the Italian frontier. But the strategic logic of 1796 demanded a decision. The war against Austria had reached a stalemate on the Rhine; the armies of Jourdan and Moreau faced the Archduke Charles across a line of fortresses that neither side could reduce. If the war was to be won, it must be won elsewhere. The Directory resolved to make Italy the decisive theatre.

To command the Army of Italy, the Directory chose a young general of twenty-six whose name was known in Paris only for his role at the siege of Toulon — where he had placed the batteries that forced the British to evacuate the port — and for the suppression of the Vendémiaire insurrection, when his cannon had cleared the streets of the capital. Lazare Carnot, the great organiser of victory who sat in the Directory, recognised something in the young Corsican that others had missed. Bonaparte was appointed commander-in-chief of the Army of Italy on the 2nd of March 1796.

He arrived at Nice on the 26th of March to find an army in ruins. His first act was to reorganise it. The old division structure was broken and recast. New generals were appointed — Augereau, Massena, Serrurier, Joubert — men of the Revolution whose origins were as humble as their ambitions were great. Bonaparte wrote to the Directory that he needed time to train his army; he did not wait for time. Within a fortnight of his arrival, the Army of Italy was in motion.

The Strategy of the Central Position

The problem that faced Bonaparte was a problem of geometry. The allied armies of Piedmont-Sardinia and Austria, commanded respectively by General Colli and the aged Field Marshal Beaulieu, held the passes of the Maritime Alps and the Apennines. They outnumbered the French by nearly two to one. Their positions were connected by the network of valleys that converge at the junction of the Alps and the Apennines. Bonaparte saw that the key to the campaign was not to fight the combined armies but to drive between them — to place himself at the point where the Piedmontese and Austrian armies touched each other, to break that connection, and to defeat each in turn before they could reunite.

This was the strategy of the central position, and it required speed, deception, and a willingness to accept risk that would have horrified a general of the old school. Bonaparte massed his army opposite the point where the two allied armies joined, near the village of Montenotte, on the northern slope of the Apennines above Savona. The Austrian left and the Piedmontese right met at a pass called the Bocchetta. Bonaparte intended to force it.

On the 10th of April, Beaulieu moved first. The Austrian general, mistaking the French concentration for a defensive precaution, advanced against the French left at Voltri with his main body. It was a fatal error. By detaching his main force to the east, Beaulieu left the junction between the Austrian and Piedmontese armies unguarded. Bonaparte struck the next day.

Montenotte, Millesimo, Dego

On the 12th of April, the French divisions under Massena and Augereau attacked the Austrian position at Montenotte. The fighting was savage and close — bayonet work in the narrow valleys, where the slopes rose so steeply that the dead rolled down into the streams. The Austrian general Argenteau, who commanded the Austrian division at the junction, was overwhelmed before he could be reinforced. By nightfall, the French had driven a wedge between the two allied armies. The central position had been seized.

Bonaparte did not pause. On the 13th, Augereau marched west against Millesimo, where the Piedmontese general Provera held a fortified position that commanded the road to Ceva. The fighting at Millesimo was among the most desperate of the campaign. Provera’s garrison, cut off and surrounded, held out for two days in the castle above the village before surrendering. On the 14th, Massena struck the Austrians at Dego, the second hinge of the allied position. The Austrians, shaken by Montenotte, broke and fled eastward toward the Po. In three days, Bonaparte had destroyed the allied junction and separated the Piedmontese from their Austrian allies.

The price of victory was that Bonaparte now had two enemies on his hands. Colli’s Piedmontese army, though isolated, was intact and had taken up a strong defensive position at Ceva, guarding the road to Turin. Beaulieu’s Austrians, though beaten, had withdrawn to Alessandria and could still strike the French flank. Bonaparte chose to pursue Colli first. On the 19th of April, the French forced the position at Ceva; on the 21st, Augereau’s division broke the Piedmontese at Mondovi in a charge that scattered Colli’s army across the Piedmontese plain.

King Victor Amadeus III of Sardinia had staked his crown on the Austrian alliance. With his army destroyed and the French at the gates of Turin, he had no choice but to sue for terms. On the 28th of April, at the village of Cherasco, the Piedmontese signed an armistice that withdrew their kingdom from the war and delivered the fortresses of Coni, Tortona, and Alessandria into French hands. Bonaparte, with characteristic audacity, had dictated terms not merely as a general but as a plenipotentiary of the Republic. The Directory, which had not authorised him to negotiate, was presented with a fait accompli. They accepted it.

The Crossing of the Po and the Bridge at Lodi

The Piedmontese question disposed of, Bonaparte turned east against the Austrians. Beaulieu had taken post along the line of the Po, the great river that divides northern Italy, and had fortified every bridge and crossing from Valenza to Piacenza. The conventional approach would have been to force a crossing at Valenza, where the Austrian defences were strongest. Bonaparte chose instead to feint toward Valenza, drawing Beaulieu’s attention to his left, while his main army marched swiftly east along the south bank of the Po to Piacenza.

On the 7th of May, the French vanguard reached Piacenza and discovered that the Austrian detachment guarding the crossing was dangerously weak. Bonaparte ordered an immediate crossing. By nightfall, the entire French army was across the Po, the boats shuttling ceaselessly in the darkness, while Beaulieu, deceived by the feint at Valenza, remained immobile forty miles to the west.

The Austrians, outflanked and outmanoeuvred, fell back in disorder toward the Adda, the next great river line. Bonaparte pursued with the speed that would become his signature. On the 10th of May, the French caught the Austrian rearguard at the town of Lodi, where the road from Piacenza to Milan crossed the Adda by a long wooden bridge. The Austrian general Sebottendorf had posted twelve thousand men and fourteen guns on the far bank, covering the bridge with artillery fire. The approaches to the bridge were swept by cannon.

What followed became the founding legend of Bonaparte’s army. The French grenadiers formed up under the covering fire of their own artillery. Bonaparte placed himself at the head of the column. At the signal, the grenadiers charged — not marching but running, bent low, the balls of the Austrian cannons tearing gaps through their ranks. The first ranks fell, and the second ranks stepped over them. Bonaparte, on foot among his men, was knocked into the marshy bank by the impact of a passing ball; his staff pulled him out, and he went forward again. The column reached the Austrian end of the bridge, hesitated for a moment under the close-range fire of the Austrian infantry, and then surged forward with the bayonet. The Austrian line broke.

The Battle of Lodi was not a great battle by the numbers — the Austrians lost perhaps two thousand men, the French somewhat fewer — but it was a great event in the history of Bonaparte’s reputation. The men of the Army of Italy had seen their general lead the charge on foot over a bridge swept by case-shot. From that day, they called him simply ‘the Little Corporal,’ and they would follow him anywhere. Bonaparte understood what he had achieved. ‘I saw the future,’ he said later. ‘In that moment, I knew I could become something in the world.‘

Milan and the Siege of Mantua

The army entered Milan on the 15th of May 1796. The Milanese, who had chafed under Austrian rule, received the French as liberators. Bonaparte installed himself in the Palazzo Serbelloni and set about the work of state-building that would run in parallel with his military operations for the next year. The old Austrian administration was swept aside; a provisional government was established; the first steps toward the creation of the Cisalpine Republic were taken. The treasures of Lombardy — its churches, its galleries, its libraries — were systematically inventoried and the choicest works dispatched to Paris.

But before the new order could be consolidated, there was a military problem that demanded solution. The great fortress of Mantua, the strongest in Italy, lay in Austrian hands, garrisoned by the remnants of Beaulieu’s army and commanded by a veteran officer of the Empire. The fortress was built on an island formed by the Mincio River, surrounded by lakes and marshes, approachable only by narrow causeways that were swept by its guns. As long as Mantua held out, Austria had a gate into Italy. Bonaparte resolved to take it.

The siege of Mantua began in earnest in June 1796. The French invested the fortresses on both sides of the Mincio, cutting the land approaches, while a flotilla of flat-bottomed boats patrolled the lakes. The siege works crept forward under the fire of the Austrian guns. Bonaparte, however, had not enough heavy artillery to breach the walls by bombardment alone. He settled in for a blockade, waiting for hunger to do what cannon could not. He would wait seven months.

The First Relief: Wurmser at Castiglione

The Austrian court at Vienna had not abandoned Italy. Throughout the summer of 1796, a new army was assembling in the Tyrol under Field Marshal Wurmser, one of the most experienced commanders of the Imperial service. Wurmser’s plan was to descend from the mountains into the Lombard plain with sixty thousand men, crush the French army before Mantua, and relieve the fortress.

Bonaparte, who had dispersed his forces across Lombardy to live off the land, found himself in a dangerous position when Wurmser’s advance was reported in late July. The Austrian army descended from the Tyrol in three columns: one down the west side of Lake Garda, one down the east side along the Adige valley, and one through the mountains toward the Brenta. Bonaparte faced the prospect of being caught between Wurmser’s army advancing from the north and the Mantua garrison sortieing from the south.

His response was a masterstroke of improvisation. He abandoned the siege of Mantua on the 1st of August, raised the blockade, and concentrated his army in a central position between the three Austrian columns. The artillery train was sent south to safety; the sick and wounded were evacuated. Bonaparte had accepted the loss of the siege works in exchange for the freedom to manoeuvre.

Wurmser entered Mantua in triumph on the 2nd of August, believing the French had been driven off. In fact, he had walked into a trap. Bonaparte had placed himself on the Austrian line of communications, and the three Austrian columns — spread out across the lakes and hills of the Garda region — could not support each other.

The battle of Castiglione, fought on the 5th of August 1796, was Bonaparte’s first great defensive-offensive battle. For four days the armies manoeuvred for position in the rolling hills between Lake Garda and the Mincio. On the morning of the 5th, Bonaparte had his army in place. Massena held the centre at Lonato; Augereau commanded the right at Castiglione; the division of General Guieux, moving along the western shore of Lake Garda, was to strike the Austrian left. Bonaparte’s plan required Guieux’s column to arrive at precisely the right moment — too soon and Wurmser would retreat, too late and the French centre would be crushed.

The timing was perfect. The battle raged through the heat of the Italian August afternoon. The Austrian infantry, the best in Europe, fought with the disciplined courage that had made them the terror of the Turks. But Bonaparte’s divisions moved like interlocking gears. At the critical moment, Guieux’s column appeared on the Austrian flank; Wurmser, his line of retreat threatened, ordered a general withdrawal. The French pursuit was relentless. By nightfall, the Austrian army was streaming north toward the Tyrol, having lost its artillery, its baggage, and twelve thousand men. The siege of Mantua was re-established the next day.

The Second Relief: Bassano and Arcola

Wurmser, however, was not finished. He had retreated into the Tyrol, reorganised his shattered divisions, and received reinforcements from Vienna. In September, he descended again into Italy — this time not from the west of Lake Garda but from the east, through the valleys of the Brenta and the Adige. Bonaparte met him at Bassano on the 8th of September and defeated him again. Wurmser, with the remnants of his army, retreated into Mantua itself, where he became the commander of the very garrison the French were besieging. He had exchanged a field army for a besieged fortress.

The Austrian court sent a third army under Field Marshal Alvinzi, a determined if unimaginative commander, who advanced from the Frioul in November. Alvinzi’s plan was to relieve Mantua by a concentric advance: one column under Davidovich descended the Adige valley from the Tyrol, while Alvinzi himself advanced from the east through the Vicenza plain. The two columns would meet before Mantua and crush the French between them.

Bonaparte, who had spent the autumn reducing the Papal Legations and pacifying the Romagna, raced north to meet the threat. The campaign that followed, in the marshes and canals of the Adige valley in the winter rains, was the most desperate of Bonaparte’s career.

The key to Alvinzi’s position was the village of Arcola, set in the marshes formed by the Alpone River, a tributary of the Adige. The causeways through the marshes were narrow; the Austrian artillery commanded the approaches; the infantry could advance only in columns of two or three men abreast. For three days — the 15th, 16th, and 17th of November — the armies fought for possession of this miserable village in the mud.

The first assault, led by Augereau in person, was driven back by the Austrian fire. Bonaparte ordered a second assault, and a third. He seized a flag and led the grenadiers himself through the marshes, the water up to their waists, the Austrian case-shot tearing the columns apart. His horse was shot from under him; he was knocked into a drainage ditch and nearly drowned in the mud; his staff pulled him out while the bullets chipped the earth around them. The fighting continued through the night, the flares from the burning villages lighting the faces of the dead.

On the third day, Massena, advancing from the left, managed to turn the Austrian flank. A diversionary force from the garrison of Porto-Legnago struck the Austrian rear. Alvinzi, his communications threatened and his army exhausted, ordered the retreat. The battle of Arcola was won, but the French had paid for it in blood. Bonaparte, when he wrote to the Directory, spoke of ‘a battle of giants’ — the phrase of Marshal Trivulzio describing Marignano.

The Third Relief: Rivoli

Alvinzi, however, would try once more. In January 1797, reinforced with fresh troops from the Tyrol, he advanced again toward Mantua with the same plan — a concerted movement by two columns through the Adige valley. The Austrian court had staked everything on this attempt. If Mantua fell, the war in Italy was lost.

This time, the Austrian plan very nearly succeeded. Davidovich’s column seized the heights of Rivoli, a plateau overlooking the Adige north of Verona, and established a fortified position that commanded the valley. Alvinzi’s main army advanced from the east. The French forces, scattered across northern Italy in their winter quarters, had only the two divisions of Joubert and Massena available to meet the threat.

Bonaparte rode through the night from Mantua to Rivoli, arriving on the plateau as the dawn of the 14th of January was breaking. What he saw was not encouraging. Joubert’s division, outnumbered three to one, was holding the edge of the plateau against the Austrian masses. The Austrian columns were visible in the valley below, their campfires still smoking, taking up positions for an assault that would crush Joubert against the cliff.

Bonaparte’s response was the most brilliant tactical improvisation of the campaign. He saw that the Austrian deployment required the columns to approach the plateau by separate ravines, each narrow and dangerous, and that the columns could not support each other while they were climbing. He ordered Joubert to hold the centre at all costs, committed Massena’s division to the left, and sent several battalions of light infantry to scale the cliff on the Austrian right — a movement the Austrians had believed impossible.

The battle of Rivoli, fought on the 14th and 15th of January 1797, was Bonaparte’s masterpiece. The Austrian columns, unable to deploy on the narrow plateau, were fed into the French fire piecemeal. Wave after wave of Austrian infantry climbed the slopes, formed up under the French artillery, and were broken by the bayonet charges of the French line. Massena’s flanking movement — a charge delivered at the double across the plateau, the bayonets glittering in the winter sun — threw the Austrian left into confusion. By nightfall, the Austrian army had ceased to exist as a fighting force. The remnants streamed back toward the Tyrol, leaving fourteen thousand prisoners and fifty guns on the field.

The Fall of Mantua

With Alvinzi’s army destroyed, the fate of Mantua was sealed. Bonaparte returned to the siege lines and delivered Wurmser an ultimatum: surrender or starve. The fortress had been under blockade for seven months; the garrison had eaten the horses, the dogs, the leather of their own harnesses. Typhus was raging in the casements. On the 2nd of February 1797, Wurmser surrendered. The Austrian field marshal, a white-haired veteran of the Seven Years’ War, marched out of the fortress at the head of his garrison — twenty thousand men who had held out longer than any fortress of the age, reduced to skeletons in tattered white uniforms. Bonaparte received him with the courtesy that great commanders owe to great opponents, and sent him to Vienna on parole.

The fall of Mantua ended the Italian campaign in its military phase. In six months, Bonaparte had defeated four Austrian armies, destroyed two of the Empire’s most celebrated commanders, and conquered the richest provinces of Italy.

The Pope, Leoben, and Campo Formio

With Austria prostrate, Bonaparte turned south against the Papal States. Pius VI, who had broken the armistice Bonaparte had imposed after the Faenza campaign in hope of an Austrian resurgence, now found himself defenceless. The French army advanced through the Romagna without opposition. On the 19th of February 1797, the Pope signed the Treaty of Tolentino, surrendering Avignon and the Legations of Bologna, Ferrara, and Ravenna to France, paying thirty million francs in indemnities, and delivering the choicest treasures of the Vatican to the commissioners of the Republic. The temporal power of the papacy, which had stood for a thousand years, was broken at a stroke.

Bonaparte now turned north again. The road to Vienna was open. The Archduke Charles, the most capable Austrian commander, was transferred from the Rhine to the Italian front, but neither his skill nor the reinforcements he brought could stop the French advance. Bonaparte crossed the Tagliamento, defeated the Austrians at Gradisca, took Trieste, and drove through the Norican Alps into the Austrian heartland. By the first week of April 1797, the French vanguard was at Leoben, less than a hundred miles from Vienna. The Austrian court sued for peace.

On the 18th of April, the Preliminaries of Leoben were signed. The terms were generous to Austria — Venice and its territories were ceded to the Emperor in exchange for the Belgian Netherlands and the Austrian recognition of the Cisalpine Republic — but the generosity was Bonaparte’s own calculation. He had exceeded his authority in negotiating terms of peace, as he had exceeded it at Cherasco, but he knew that the Directory, presented with a treaty, would accept it.

The Treaty of Campo Formio, signed on the 17th of October 1797, confirmed the Leoben terms and ended the war between France and Austria. The First Coalition was dissolved. Britain stood alone. Bonaparte returned to Paris in December 1797 to a reception that surpassed any triumph of the ancient world. The streets of the capital were lined with cheering crowds; the Directory received him in the Luxembourg Palace with a ceremony that was half homage, half fear.

The Birth of a Republic

The true significance of the Italian campaigns was not merely military. Bonaparte had not simply conquered Italy; he had remade it. The Cisalpine Republic, created from the conquered territories of Lombardy, the Duchy of Modena, and the Papal Legations, was a French satellite state organised on the model of the Republic — a directory of five members, a legislative council, and a constitution that proclaimed the sovereignty of the people. The old mosaic of duchies and principalities that had divided northern Italy for centuries was swept away. The Republic was a child of the French bayonet, and it would not survive the French defeat, but for the moment it was the most advanced political experiment the Italian peninsula had ever known.

The campaign also created Bonaparte. The young general who had ridden out of Nice in March 1796 had been an unknown appointment, a protégé of Carnot’s in whom no one outside the Directory had much confidence. The general who returned to Paris in December 1797 was the most famous man in Europe. He had given the Republic its greatest victories, its richest provinces, and its most durable peace. The soldiers of the Army of Italy had become his soldiers; the Cisalpine Republic was his creation; the treaties of Tolentino, Leoben, and Campo Formio bore his signature. He had proved himself not merely a great general but a statesman, a diplomat, an administrator of genius. The Directory, which had hoped to use him as an instrument, had created a rival.

Paganel, writing in 1815, looked back on the Italian campaign as the revelation of Bonaparte’s character as much as his military talent. The campaign displayed the qualities that would make him master of Europe: the cold audacity, the precision of calculation, the ability to see a battlefield as a single geometric problem in which the movements of divisions were the lines of a proof. It also displayed the qualities that would destroy him: the insatiable ambition, the contempt for constituted authority, the conviction that he alone could judge the interests of the peoples he conquered. Italy was liberated and conquered, freed and plundered, in the same gesture. It was a pattern that would repeat itself across a continent.

In the history of the French Revolution, the Italian campaigns of 1796-97 occupy a place of unique importance. They saved the Republic from military defeat, destroyed the First Coalition, and created the political conditions for the Directory’s survival. But they also created the man who, two years later, would overthrow the Directory and begin the long march from the Republic to the Empire. The bridge at Lodi was the beginning of everything.
