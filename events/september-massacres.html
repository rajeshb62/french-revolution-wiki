The September Massacres

The September Massacres of 1792 were the slaughter of more than a thousand prisoners in the Parisian prisons between the afternoon of Sunday, 2 September, and the evening of Thursday, 6 September. The killing was organised by the insurrectionary Commune of Paris and the Cordeliers club, carried out by bands of armed volunteers recruited from the sections, and tacitly permitted by the Minister of Justice, Georges Danton. The massacres stand as the first great act of state-sanctioned bloodletting of the Revolution — the passage from the popular insurrection of 10 August to the institutional terror that would follow in 1793.

The Precipice

The fall of the monarchy on 10 August 1792 had left France in an impossible position. The royal family was imprisoned in the Temple. The Legislative Assembly, stripped of executive authority, had named a Provisional Executive Council in which Danton held Justice, Roland the Interior, and Servan the War. The Prussian and Austrian armies, commanded by the Duke of Brunswick, were advancing across the frontier. Verdun, the last fortress on the road to Paris, was under siege and expected to fall within days. The manifesto that Brunswick had issued in July — threatening to deliver Paris to “exemplary and forever memorable vengeance” if the royal family were harmed — had produced the opposite of its intended effect: it had inflamed Parisian opinion and convinced the sections that the city was infested with an internal enemy that would rise in support of the invaders.

The prisons were crowded with the arrestees of the previous weeks. After 10 August, the sections and the Commune’s Committee of Surveillance had carried out waves of arrests against non-juring priests, suspected royalists, Swiss guards captured at the Tuileries, and anyone deemed suspect. The prisons of the Abbaye, the Carmes (the Carmelite convent used as a detention centre), the Conciergerie, La Force, the Châtelet, and Bicêtre held somewhere between 2,600 and 3,000 prisoners.

In the last days of August, a current of talk had begun among the sections — carried first in the Cordeliers district where Danton’s influence was strongest. The enemy was at the gates; the prisoners inside the city must be considered a fifth column; the only sure measure was to eliminate them before they could act. The coming of war news from the frontier — and above all the fall of Verdun — would provide the signal.

2 September: The Signal

The morning of Sunday, 2 September, brought the confirmation that Paris had dreaded: Verdun had fallen, or was about to fall — the reports differed on the day, but the substance was the same. The road to Paris lay open. At the Hôtel de Ville, the Commune ordered the tocsin sounded. The alarm bell of Notre-Dame began to toll. Drums beat the générale through the streets. The sections called their citizens to arms, and the volunteers preparing to march to the frontier gathered in the great squares to receive their marching orders.

It was in this atmosphere — the city braced for invasion, the bells ringing, the drums rolling — that the prisons were opened.

The first attack fell upon the prisoners being transferred from the Hôtel de Ville to the Abbaye prison. A crowd stopped the carriages at the doors of the Abbaye and began the killing. The method was the same across all the prisons: bands of armed men — some in the uniform of the National Guard, others in ordinary dress with sabres, pikes, and axes — entered the prisons, established what they called “popular tribunals” in the courtyards or gatehouses, examined the prisoners one by one, and delivered verdicts of liberty or death. Those condemned were handed to the executioners waiting at the gates, who killed them with sabres, pikes, and axes. The bodies were stripped and piled in the streets or thrown into carts.

At the Abbaye, the first of these “commissions” was improvised. A group of perhaps a hundred to a hundred and fifty men, armed and resolute, took possession of the prison gates. The prisoners were called out one by one. A brief interrogation was conducted — name, profession, the grounds of detention. Those who satisfied the judges of their patriotism were released with a shout of “Vive la Nation!” Those who did not were thrust through the gate to the waiting executioners. The judgments were final; there was no appeal.

Gouverneur Morris, the American Minister to France, was at his house in the Rue de la Planche in the Faubourg Saint-Germain when the news reached him. A servant reported that the people were breaking into the prisons. He could hear the tocsin sounding from the churches. By evening the streets were empty of ordinary traffic; only armed patrols moved through the city. Morris noted in his diary that the city felt as though it were under military occupation — the same city that had been, four days earlier, in a state of revolutionary exaltation, now descended into something darker.

At the Carmes prison, where the non-juring priests had been held since August, the killing began in the afternoon. The Carmelite convent had been converted into a temporary prison after the fall of the monarchy, and held approximately 160 priests — clergy who had refused to swear the Civil Constitution of the Clergy and were therefore considered enemies of the Revolution. A mob of about two hundred men, led by an actor named Collot d’Herbois and a former priest named Jourdan, broke into the convent garden. The priests were dragged out of their cells, lined up against the walls of the cloister, and shot or cut down with sabres. Some tried to climb over the walls; most were caught before they could escape. The killing continued into the night by torchlight. By the end, about 115 priests lay dead in the garden — the “Carmes” massacre became a byword among Catholic Europe for revolutionary cruelty.

The same pattern spread to the other prisons. At the Conciergerie, the old royal prison on the Île de la Cité, the commissioners of the Commune arrived and demanded the keys. The prisoners were brought before a hastily assembled tribunal. The common criminals were released; the political prisoners and the Swiss guards were killed. At La Force prison, where the most distinguished prisoners were held — including the Princesse de Lamballe, the superintendent of the Queen’s household and her closest friend — the killing began in the evening.

Roland’s Protest

Jean-Marie Roland, the Minister of the Interior, was one of the few members of the Provisional Council who attempted to stop the massacres. As minister charged with the general surveillance of public order — though without direct command over the armed force — he had written urgently to the Commune in the person of the mayor, Jérôme Pétion, calling on the municipal authorities to deploy their vigilance. He had addressed himself to the commandant-general, Santerre, requesting him to reinforce the posts guarding the prisons. When he learned that the prisons were directly threatened, he issued a formal requisition calling on the commandant to guard them scrupulously, placing the responsibility for any event on the commandant’s head. To give effect to this measure, he ordered the requisition printed and posted at every street corner — a public appeal to the citizens to watch over the safety of the prisons themselves, since the military commander appeared to neglect his duty.

Roland was not at the Interior Ministry when the crowd arrived there at about five o’clock on the evening of Sunday, 2 September — the same hour the prisons were being invested. Approximately two hundred men appeared in the courtyard, demanding to see the minister and demanding arms. Madame Roland, who was at home, received them. She ordered that ten of them be invited upstairs. They told her they were good citizens about to depart for Verdun but lacked arms. She explained that the Minister of the Interior had never had arms at his disposal; they must apply to the Ministry of War. After a tense exchange, she persuaded them to search the hôtel and satisfy themselves that Roland was not hiding, and directed them to the council meeting at the Ministry of the Marine. They withdrew, taking a domestic servant as a hostage who was released an hour later.

The ministers learned of the massacres only the following morning, Monday, 3 September. Shocked by the abominable crimes, the evident complicity of the Commune, and the impotence of honest men to arrest them, Roland and his wife resolved that the only course left to a minister of honour was to denounce the killings with the greatest possible éclat, to appeal to the Assembly to stop them, to raise the indignation of all honest citizens, and to expose himself, if necessary, to the daggers of the assassins rather than participate by silence in the crime.

Roland wrote his famous letter to the Legislative Assembly on the 3rd of September — a document as celebrated in its way as the letter he had addressed to the king the previous June. The Assembly received it with transports of applause; it ordered the letter printed, distributed, and posted. The Assembly applauded, as weak men applaud, at signs of a courage they cannot themselves imitate but which touches them and awakens some hope.

Later that same day, a former colleague of Roland’s brought to dinner Anacharsis Clootz, the self-styled “orator of the human race.” Clootz defended the massacres as a necessary and salutary measure, reciting commonplaces about the rights of the people, the justice of popular vengeance, and its utility for the happiness of the species. He spoke long and loudly, ate more than anyone, and bored the company. He did not return.

Danton’s Role

Danton, the Minister of Justice, was at the Hôtel de Ville throughout the critical hours, seated in the Committee of Surveillance from which the orders for the multiplied arrests of the previous days had issued. It was there that he had embraced Marat after a feigned quarrel of twenty-four hours. He then went upstairs to Pétion, took him aside, and in his characteristic language — always heightened with vigorous expressions — said: “Do you know what they’ve done? They’ve issued a warrant for Roland’s arrest. Who? That mad Committee. I took the warrant — here it is — we can’t let them act this way. Against a member of the Council!” Pétion took the warrant, read it, smiled, and handed it back. “Let them do it,” he said, “it will produce a good effect.” “A good effect!” replied Danton, examining the mayor curiously. “Oh, I will not suffer that; I will put them in their place.” The warrant was never executed.

The question that follows the reader of these events is whether the two hundred men who appeared at Roland’s hôtel that evening had been sent by the authors of the warrant; whether the failure of their attempt, which introduced delay into the execution of the plan, caused those who had conceived it to hesitate; and whether Danton’s démarche to the mayor was that of a conspirator who wished to test the effect of the blow, or to gain credit for having parried a stroke that had already miscarried.

When the commandant of the prisons, Grandpré — who had spent the morning of 2 September visiting the detenus and had managed to facilitate the release of a considerable number — approached Danton at the council’s exit that evening to speak of what he had seen and of the requisitions that the Minister of the Interior had addressed to the armed force, Danton replied with his bellowing voice and an appropriate gesture: “I don’t give a damn about the prisoners — let them become what they can!” The remark was made in the presence of twenty people, who shuddered to hear a minister of justice speak so.

The Princesse de Lamballe

Of all the victims of the September Massacres, none struck contemporaries with greater horror than the murder of the Princesse de Lamballe. Marie-Thérèse-Louise de Savoie-Carignan, the Princesse de Lamballe, was the superintendent of the Queen’s household and the closest friend Marie-Antoinette had. She had returned from emigration in 1791 to be near the Queen, and after the storming of the Tuileries on 10 August she had been arrested with the other royalists and imprisoned at La Force.

On the afternoon of 3 September, the crowd that had already killed at the Abbaye and the Carmes arrived at La Force. The Princesse was brought before the improvised tribunal in the prison courtyard. She was asked to swear hatred of the king, the queen, and the monarchy. She refused. The tribunal condemned her. She was pushed through the gate and seized by the mob in the street. The killing was prolonged and savage.

Her head was cut off, placed on a pike, and carried through the streets of Paris to the Temple, where the royal family were imprisoned. The mob intended to force the Queen to witness the head of her friend. The guards at the Temple prevented the crowd from entering, but the head was paraded past the windows.

In the Temple that evening, the Queen asked why the noise of the crowd was so great. She was told nothing. But she saw the shadow of the pike move past the window, and she understood — or sensed — what had happened. When the news was confirmed, Marie-Antoinette fainted.

Campan, who wrote the Queen’s story from the testimony of those who were with her in the Temple, recorded that the Queen remained changed from that day forward. The friend who had refused to abandon her, who had chosen imprisonment over safety, was gone, and the Queen’s captivity, already harsh, became a descent into a deeper solitude.

The Third and Fourth Days

The massacres continued on Tuesday, 4 September, and into the following days. By Tuesday the focus had shifted to the remaining prisons: Bicêtre, which held common criminals and the insane as well as political prisoners; the Salpêtrière, the women’s prison; and the more distant prisons of the city. The fury was beginning to exhaust itself, but the Commune did nothing to stop it.

Morris, writing from his house on the outskirts of Paris, recorded the growing tension. The weather was pleasant. Business was suspended. The thoroughfares were no longer safe. The Commission that had been established at the Hôtel de Ville continued to meet, examining papers and directing arrests. On Monday, 3 September — the day of the Princesse de Lamballe’s murder — Morris noted that the prisoners at Versailles had also been killed. “The City of Paris remains in a state of insurrection,” he wrote. “It has named the Ministers, who act in subordination to it. The Commune assumes all powers.”

On Tuesday, 4 September, between two and three in the afternoon, Morris received a note from the Commune informing him that his horses had been stopped and that orders had been given to permit their passage for the future. The assassins were beginning to formalise their procedures.

By Wednesday, 5 September, the killing had begun to subside, though isolated murders continued. Robespierre, speaking at the Jacobins, denounced those who had tried to save the prisoners. The Commune, far from being shamed, defended the massacres as a necessary act of popular justice.

The Aftermath

When the prisons fell silent, the count was appalling. Estimates ranged from 1,100 to 1,600 dead. About half were non-juring priests and royalist political prisoners; the rest were common criminals, including many detained for minor offences and some who were simply in the wrong place. The proportion of priests among the dead was the highest — roughly three-quarters of the non-juring clergy in Paris had been killed.

The Legislative Assembly, which had sat in helpless session throughout the massacres, issued no condemnation. A few deputies protested. Roland’s letter had been printed and applauded, but no deputy proposed to arrest the leaders of the Commune or to send troops to stop the killing. The Assembly, like the city, was paralysed by fear and by the knowledge that the Commune commanded the armed sections.

The Provisional Executive Council was divided. Danton had been at the Hôtel de Ville throughout the three days; he had not lifted a finger to prevent the massacres, and his celebrated dismissal of the prisoners’ fate had been overheard by twenty witnesses. Servan, the Minister of War, sent no troops. Roland alone had acted and had failed; his letter to the Assembly was his only weapon, and it had produced applause and nothing more.

The Girondins — the republican faction that had emerged from the Legislative Assembly and that included Roland, Brissot, Vergniaud, and their allies — broke decisively with the Commune and with Danton after the massacres. The breaking point had been reached; they could no longer pretend to share a common cause with the men who had organised the slaughter. But the break came too late to save them. The Commune and the Montagnards — Robespierre, Marat, Danton — now had the upper hand. The Girondins became the object of the most constant hatred, the most exact surveillance, the most severe rigours. Every act became a crime for them; every accusation became legitimate; every defence was forbidden. Brissot, publicly calumniated by Barère, could not find a single printer in all of France courageous enough to publish the rebuttal he addressed to his calumniator.

The September Massacres left an indelible mark on the Revolution. They demonstrated that the Parisian Commune was willing to exercise a direct and violent sovereignty beyond the control of any constituted authority. They introduced the principle — which the Revolutionary Tribunal of 1793 would later institutionalise — that revolutionary justice could be administered summarily and without appeal. They deepened the division between the Girondins and the Montagnards that would culminate in the proscription of the Girondins in June 1793. And they accustomed Paris to the spectacle of mass killing — a preparation for the Terror that would begin in earnest nine months later.

In the prisons that survived the massacres, the survivors were left with the memory of the days when the tocsin rang and the crowd came. Some of those who had been released by the popular tribunals — able to satisfy the judges of their revolutionary credentials — emerged into a city that had become unrecognisable. The prisons that had been emptied were soon filled again: the Convention, which had replaced the Legislative Assembly on 21 September 1792, passed laws that would fill the Abbaye and the Conciergerie and the Luxembourg with a new generation of prisoners — the Girondins, the Hébertists, the Dantonists — who would be tried by a properly constituted Revolutionary Tribunal and sent to the guillotine, not by pike and sabre, but by the cold machinery of the law.
