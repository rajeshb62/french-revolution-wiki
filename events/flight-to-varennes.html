The Flight to Varennes

On the night of 20–21 June 1791, the King and Queen of France, their two children, the king’s sister Madame Élisabeth, and three servants slipped out of the Tuileries Palace in disguise and fled Paris in a heavy berline coach, heading for the fortress of Montmédy on the eastern frontier. They had been prisoners in their own capital since the October Days of 1789. They would be free by dawn — or so they believed. By midnight on the 21st, they were under arrest in the small town of Varennes-en-Argonne, sixty miles short of their destination. The coach was turned around, and the royal family was driven back to Paris in a silent procession through crowds that refused to remove their hats. The monarchy never recovered the trust it lost on that road.

The Prisoners of the Tuileries

From October 1789, when the market-women of Paris had brought the king and his family from Versailles to the Tuileries Palace, Louis XVI had lived under the shadow of the Revolution. He had sanctioned the Civil Constitution of the Clergy, abolished the nobility, and accepted the Declaration of the Rights of Man. Each concession had been wrung from him by the threat of violence; each had deepened his conviction that he could not govern with the Assembly’s restraints.

The breaking point came in April 1791. The king wished to leave Paris for his country residence at Saint-Cloud, where he could receive Easter communion from a priest who had not sworn the oath to the Civil Constitution. On the morning of 18 April, the royal carriage was ready; the household had already been sent ahead; the king’s dinner was prepared at Saint-Cloud. The National Guard posted at the Tuileries refused to let him pass. They closed the gates, shouted insults, and forced the king and his family to descend from their carriage and return to their apartments. Two persons who approached the king’s carriage were roughly handled. An elderly courtier who tried to intervene was seized by the guards and his sword taken from him.

The Saint-Cloud incident decided the matter. The king was a prisoner in plain sight. The outrage, though deeply felt, was not altogether unwelcome: it gave the royal family a public justification for the plan they had already begun to form.

The Plan: Bouillé and the Army of the East

The escape depended on the Marquis de Bouillé, commander of the Army of the East. Bouillé commanded the garrisons of Metz, Montmédy, and the fortified towns of the eastern frontier. He was loyal to the king, had suppressed the mutiny at Nancy the year before, and had placed his regiments along the road from Châlons to Montmédy. The plan was simple: the royal family would travel by coach through Châlons, Sainte-Menehould, and Varennes to Montmédy, where Bouillé’s troops would meet them. From Montmédy, the king intended to negotiate with the Assembly from a position of strength — or, if necessary, to cross into the Austrian Netherlands and join the émigré princes.

The choice of Montmédy was deliberate. It was close to the frontier but still on French soil. The king did not wish to be seen as fleeing the kingdom; he would declare that he was establishing himself in a loyal province from which he could restore order.

The route was surveyed by the Comte Goguelat, Bouillé’s aide-de-camp. Goguelat rode from Paris to Montmédy and back in a light post-chaise, calculating the time required for the journey. He made a fatal error: he timed the trip alone, without the heavy berline that would carry the family, and without the courier who would ride ahead. A light chaise without a courier was nearly three hours faster than a lumbering berline preceded by a postillion. The miscalculation would decide everything.

The Preparations

Throughout March and April 1791, the queen occupied herself with the preparations. She ordered a complete wardrobe for herself and her children to be sent to Brussels. Her first chambermaid, Madame Campan, went out alone and almost in disguise to commission the trousseau from different shops — six chemises from one linen-draper, six from another, dresses, peignoirs, and complete sets of clothes for the little Dauphin, measured against Campan’s own son. A trunk full of these items was addressed to a trusted former servant in Arras, who was to stand ready to carry them to Brussels.

The queen also wished to send her travelling-necessaire — a fitted case containing her toilette articles — ahead to the Austrian Netherlands. She proposed to do this openly, under the pretext of ordering an identical one as a gift for the Archduchess Christina, the governess of the Netherlands. Campan opposed the plan, arguing that in a palace full of spies, someone would surely see through the ruse. The queen insisted, and the only compromise Campan could obtain was that the necessity would not vanish from the queen’s room; instead, the Austrian chargé d’affaires would come to the queen’s toilette and ask publicly for her to order a second one. This was done, at the cost of five hundred louis.

These precautions were useless. The queen’s wardrobe-woman, a woman who had served since the time of the queen’s marriage and who enjoyed a comfortable income of more than twelve thousand francs a year, was a spy. She had an apartment in the entresol above the queen’s room, she received Third Estate deputies in her quarters, and her lover was an aide-de-camp of Lafayette. After the royal family’s return from Varennes, the mayor of Paris, Bailly, handed the queen a denunciation from this woman, dated 21 May, in which she reported that preparations for a departure were under way at the Tuileries. She had noticed that Campan had spent an evening shut up with the queen repacking the diamonds. She had found the jewels scattered with cotton on the sofa of the queen’s private cabinet. She had a duplicate key.

The queen’s diamonds — her own property, not the crown jewels, which had already been surrendered to commissioners of the Assembly — were packed and entrusted to Léonard, the queen’s hairdresser, who set out with the Duc de Choiseul and left the deposit in Brussels.

The Night of 20 June

The escape was set for the night of 20 June 1791. The Marquis de Fersen, the Swedish nobleman devoted to the queen, was the principal organiser of the departure. He had procured the berline and arranged for relays of horses along the route. He himself would drive the family out of Paris.

The plan required the royal family to leave the Tuileries separately and in disguise, meeting at a prearranged spot outside the city. The king was to be attended by the Marquis d’Agoult, disguised as a valet; the queen would be accompanied by the governess of the royal children, the Marquise de Tourzel, and her daughter; the Dauphin was disguised as a girl. Madame Élisabeth, the king’s sister, would leave by a different exit.

The king’s valet had prepared a complete set of disguises: a round hat, a plain coat, a wig — the costume of a valet rather than a monarch. The queen wore a simple gown, a black hat, and a veil. The children were dressed as the children of a respectable bourgeois family.

At eleven o’clock on the evening of 20 June, the king, who had been seen by his valet-de-chambre to bed at the usual hour, rose and dressed in his disguise. He left the palace through a small door that had been left unlocked, crossed the courtyard, and found Fersen at the wheel of a hired cabriolet. The queen left through her own apartments, passing through rooms that had been carefully arranged to seem empty. She was recognised by Lafayette’s aide-de-camp, who was on guard that night — but the aide-de-camp looked the other way and said nothing.

The queen and the children reached the cabriolet. Fersen drove them through the city streets, past the barriers, to the Porte Saint-Martin, where the great berline was waiting. The king arrived separately. By half past midnight, the family was together, the berline was rolling eastward on the road to Châlons, and Fersen, having seen them off, turned back to Paris.

The Journey East

The berline was a vast carriage, painted green and yellow, drawn by six horses, large enough to seat the king, the queen, Madame Élisabeth, the two children, the Marquise de Tourzel, and one or two attendants. It was heavy, conspicuous, and slow. The plan called for the berline to make good time through the night, meeting relays of Bouillé’s cavalry at prearranged points along the road.

The first stage went well. The family reached Bondy before dawn, changed horses, and continued. By mid-morning they were in Châlons, where they stopped briefly. No one recognised them. The king, in his valet’s wig and round hat, was taken for a steward travelling with his employer’s family. The queen, who had been seen so often in silks and diamonds, was unrecognisable in her plain gown. The Dauphin, dressed as a girl, played quietly beside his sister.

The trouble began at Sainte-Menehould, a market town on the road to Varennes. Here the berline stopped to change horses. The postmaster’s son, Jean-Baptiste Drouet, looked at the heavy coach, the fine linen of the passengers’ clothes beneath their plain outer garments, the large number of attendants. He compared the faces to the image on an assignat — a revolutionary banknote whose design included a profile of the king. Drouet was not certain, but he was suspicious. He decided to follow the coach.

Meanwhile, the relays planned by Bouillé were falling apart. A troop of hussars that should have been waiting at Pont-de-Sommevelle had been withdrawn. Goguelat, who commanded them, had left the main road and waited at a different point. The berline passed the rendezvous without meeting the escort. Goguelat, realising his mistake, raced after the coach but could not catch it.

Drouet reached Varennes shortly after the berline. Varennes-en-Argonne was a small town of perhaps a thousand souls, straddling the river Aire. It was not on the direct road to Montmédy — the berline had taken a detour to cross the river at the bridge in Varennes. The town was asleep. The hour was past eleven at night.

The Arrest

Drouet raised the alarm. The town’s mayor, Jean-Baptiste Sauce, was awakened. The National Guard of Varennes was roused. When the berline reached the bridge over the Aire, it found the way blocked by a cart that had been deliberately overturned. Armed men surrounded the coach.

The king was taken to Sauce’s house — the home and shop of the grocer-mayor. The family waited in the back room, the queen seated between two bundles of tallow candles. The king spoke at length with Sauce, explaining that he was merely travelling to Montmédy to negotiate with the Assembly, that he meant the kingdom no harm, that his departure was not hostile but was prescribed by his love for his subjects. Sauce wavered. He might have let the king go — the queen was speaking to Madame Sauce, who seemed the dominant partner in the household, and received only the reply that to let the king pass would expose M. Sauce to having his head cut off.

“Very well,” the queen replied, “mine is your king; he has made your happiness for a long time, he wishes to make it still.”

But Madame Sauce spoke again of the danger to her husband, and the moment for decision passed. The municipal officers arrived, and with them the National Guard from the surrounding villages. The king was recognised beyond doubt. The aides-de-camp of the Assembly arrived soon after, bearing decrees that ordered the king’s arrest.

Goguelat had reached Varennes with his hussars. He asked the king for orders. The king hesitated. He had earlier asked Goguelat whether the fighting would be hot; Goguelat had answered that it would be very hot. That answer now hung in the air. The king, who abhorred bloodshed, gave no order to fight. Goguelat asked the hussars whether they were for the king or for the nation. The hussars replied for the nation.

The Dauphin’s gouvernante, hoping to gain time for Bouillé to arrive with forces, threw herself on a bed and cried out with an invented colic. The queen played along, insisting that a woman who had devoted herself to following them on a dangerous journey could not be abandoned. The ruse was seen through. No delay was granted.

At dawn on 22 June, the berline was turned around. The king and his family began the journey back to Paris.

The Return: Barnave and Pétion

The Assembly had dispatched three commissioners to bring back the king: Barnave, Pétion, and Latour-Maubourg. They met the royal family at Épernay and joined them in the great berline for the slow return journey. The contrast between the two men could not have been greater.

Pétion, the future mayor of Paris, was a republican of the advanced school. His behaviour on the journey was calculatedly insulting. He ate and drank in the king’s berline with deliberate sloppiness, throwing chicken bones out the window at the risk of striking the king’s face. When Madame Élisabeth poured him wine, he raised his glass without a word to indicate he had enough. The king attempted to open a conversation about the state of France, explaining that he had left Paris to give the executive power the force necessary for the constitutional settlement. Pétion replied: “Not yet, in truth, because the French are not ripe for a republic.” The king fell silent and did not speak again until Paris.

Pétion held the little Dauphin on his knees. He amused himself by rolling the child’s beautiful blond curls around his fingers and, speaking with animation, pulled hard enough to make the boy cry. “Give me my son,” said the queen, “he is accustomed to cares and attentions which little dispose him to such familiarities.”

Barnave was the opposite. A young deputy from Grenoble, he had been one of the most eloquent orators of the revolutionary party. But the experience of the return journey transformed him. He treated the royal family with studied respect. When the queen pressed him to take refreshment, he replied: “Madame, the deputies of the National Assembly, in so solemn a circumstance, ought to occupy their Majesties only with their mission, and not at all with their needs.”

Near Varennes, a nobleman named the Chevalier de Dampierre had approached the carriage and was killed by the crowd that surrounded it. A few leagues further, a poor village curé imprudently approached to speak to the king. The crowd turned on him. “Tigers!” Barnave cried, leaning half out of the carriage, “have you ceased to be French? Nation of brave men, have you become a nation of assassins?” Madame Élisabeth caught him by the skirt of his coat to keep him from falling, and the curé was saved.

The queen later described the impression Barnave made on her. She told Campan that his conduct on the road had been perfect, that he had a noble spirit, that he belonged to that class distinguished by education and only led astray by the ambition that real merit creates. She said that Barnave was a man worthy of esteem. “If ever power returns to our hands,” she said, “Barnave’s pardon is already written in our hearts.”

Paris Learns the News

The news of the king’s flight reached Paris on the morning of 21 June. The city woke to find the Tuileries empty, the royal apartments deserted, the king vanished. The alarm was sounded. The National Guard was mobilised; the barriers of Paris were closed; couriers were sent racing down every road eastward.

Gouverneur Morris, the American diplomat, was in London on business when he heard the news. He recorded in his diary on 25 June: “We hear that the King and Queen of France have effected their Escape from the Tuilleries and have got six or seven Hours the Start of their Keepers. This will produce some considerable Consequences. If they get off safe a War is inevitable, and if retaken it will probably suspend for some Time all monarchical Government in France.”

Morris hurried back to Paris, arriving on 1 July. He spent the next day reading the various details of the king’s flight and arrest. Paris was in ferment. He visited the Comte de Montmorin, the foreign minister, and learned the intentions of the Assembly: “The Intention of the Assembly is I find to cover up if possible the King’s Flight and cause it to be forgotten. This proves to me great Feebleness in every Respect and will perhaps destroy the Monarchy.”

He called on Lafayette, who was not at home, but saw his wife: “She seems to be half wild.”

The Constitutional Fiction

The Assembly faced a dilemma. The king had fled — but the constitution they were writing required a king. A king who had voluntarily abandoned his post and attempted to join the enemies of the Revolution could not simply resume his throne. But to depose him would be to admit that the Revolution had no constitutional monarch and to open the door to either a republic or a regency. The Jacobins were already demanding the latter; the Cordeliers were calling for the former.

The Assembly chose a fiction. The king, they declared, had not fled. He had been kidnapped. The enemies of the Revolution had abducted him from the Tuileries against his will. The Assembly’s decree of 21 June had ordered the king’s arrest and the suspension of his functions, but the fiction of kidnapping allowed the monarchy to survive. The king’s declaration — the written statement he had left behind, denouncing the Revolution and repudiating the constitution — was quietly ignored.

On 15 July, the Assembly completed its work. The king would be reinstated as soon as he accepted the constitution. The Jacobins split on the question; the more radical members, led by Robespierre, walked out and established their club in the former convent of the Jacobins on the Rue Saint-Honoré. The Cordeliers, the more advanced society of the Left, attempted to rally the people with a petition demanding the king’s abdication. On 17 July, a crowd gathered at the Champ de Mars to sign it. The National Guard, commanded by Lafayette, was called out. Shots were fired. Fifty people were killed. The Cordeliers’ leaders were arrested or went into hiding. The republican movement, born on the day of the king’s flight, was driven underground for a season.

The King’s Acceptance

On 14 September 1791, Louis XVI appeared before the Assembly and accepted the constitution. He was restored to his functions, but the restoration was hollow. The cordon of surveillance around the Tuileries was stricter than ever. The king’s bedchamber was guarded by a National Guard officer who slept in a chair between two doors, the inner door kept open so that he could see the king and queen in their beds. The queen’s own wardrobe-woman — the same who had betrayed her — was to have been left as the only woman admitted to her service; the queen, on her return, had insisted that Lafayette remove her from the palace.

The effect of the journey showed on the queen. When Campan saw her for the first time after the return, the queen took off her cap and showed her hair: in a single night, it had turned white as that of a woman of seventy. She had a ring made of a lock of this white hair for the Princesse de Lamballe, inscribed: “blanched by misfortune.”

Morris, watching from his place in Parisian society, became convinced that the monarchy could not survive. He discussed the situation with Montmorin and found him determined to preserve both the monarchy and the monarch, but without any viable plan. “There is no other measure that can be attempted,” Montmorin said. Morris noted privately: “Of course they must go on with the miserable creature which God has given. His wisdom will doubtless produce good by ways to us inscrutable.”

Thomas Paine and the Marquis de Condorcet had posted a manifesto for a republic on the very day after the king’s flight. Their Société Républicaine survived only a few weeks before the Champ de Mars massacre scattered its members. But the seed had been planted. When Morris dined at Short’s a few days after his return, he found Paine “inflated to the eyes and big with a litter of Revolutions.”

The Significance

The Flight to Varennes was the watershed of the French Revolution. Before it, the king could still be imagined as the reluctant champion of reform, the prisoner of evil counsellors, the constitutional monarch who had freely accepted the new order. After it, the king’s declaration and his attempted flight revealed him as an implacable enemy of the Revolution, ready to repudiate everything he had sanctioned at the point of a foreign bayonet.

The trust on which the constitutional monarchy of 1791 depended was broken beyond repair. The Assembly’s fiction of kidnapping fooled no one. The republican movement that appeared in the streets in July 1791 was suppressed, but it returned in August 1792 with overwhelming force. The king’s flight made the republic inevitable. It made the king’s trial and execution possible. And it turned the Revolution from a struggle within France into a war against all the crowned heads of Europe.

The failure was not inevitable. It was the product of a series of small accidents: Goguelat’s miscalculation of the time, the missing troop of hussars at Pont-de-Sommevelle, the suspicious postmaster at Sainte-Menehould, the overturned cart at the Varennes bridge, Sauce’s fear of losing his head. Several hours’ difference in the timing would have brought the bouille to Varennes before the alarm was raised. A faster coach would have passed Sainte-Menehould before Drouet had time to act. A firmer king would have ordered Goguelat to fight. But the berline was heavy, the king was hesitant, and the Revolution was watching.

The Comte de Provence, the king’s brother, escaped the same night through a different route and reached Brussels safely. He would wait. The throne would be his in another twenty-three years.
