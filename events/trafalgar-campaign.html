The Trafalgar Campaign

The Trafalgar Campaign (1803–1805) was the culminating naval episode of the Napoleonic Wars, in which the combined fleets of France and Spain were destroyed by the British Mediterranean fleet under Vice-Admiral Horatio Nelson off Cape Trafalgar on 21 October 1805. The campaign forms an indivisible whole with Napoleon’s invasion project: the destruction of the Franco-Spanish fleet at Trafalgar compelled Napoleon to abandon his planned descent upon England and to turn the Grande Armée eastward, producing the victory at Austerlitz two months later. Trafalgar caused Austerlitz — Nelson’s victory at sea determined the course of the war on land.

The Invasion Preparations

After the rupture of the Peace of Amiens in May 1803, Napoleon resolved to strike directly at Great Britain by a cross-Channel invasion. This was not a feint: Napoleon meant to cross, and the preparations were on a scale appropriate to his determination. An army of 130,000 men — the most brilliant soldiery of the age, veterans of Italy, Egypt, and Germany — was encamped along the coast between Étaples and Vimereux, with the principal concentration at Boulogne. By July 1805, the port of Boulogne sheltered over a thousand gunboats and transports, capable of carrying 40,000 men in a single tide. Three subsidiary harbours — Vimereux, Ambleteuse, and Étaples — held another seven hundred vessels, raising the total force to 62,000 soldiers who could be embarked from the four Channel ports, with a further 27,000 in the North Sea ports as a reserve.

The crossing itself presented exceptional difficulties. The flat-bottomed boats were designed to be beached directly on the English coast, so that troops could land without transshipment; they were built to be moved by oars as well as sail, so that a calm or fog might allow them to slip past the British cruisers. Napoleon understood that his gunboats could not fight heavy ships-of-the-line. The flotilla was never intended to force the passage unsupported. Its purpose was to fix British attention on the Channel coast while the real plan — the concentration of the French battle-fleet to cover the crossing — unfolded elsewhere.

The Naval Combination

The key to the invasion was the Toulon fleet. Napoleon’s original design called for the Toulon squadron to evade the British blockade, pick up the Rochefort and Ferrol squadrons en route, and appear off Boulogne with a superior force, holding the Channel long enough — four or five days was the emperor’s phrase — for the army to cross. The choice of admiral was crucial. Napoleon selected Latouche Tréville, his boldest naval commander, and fixed the sailing for January 1804. But Latouche Tréville died in August 1804, and after some hesitation Napoleon chose Vice-Admiral Pierre de Villeneuve — a decision that proved deeply unfortunate. Villeneuve was accomplished, brave, and skilful, but he saw the defects of the French navy with a clarity that sapped his will to take risks. He was incapable of sacrificing his command as a forlorn hope upon which a greater design might follow.

Nelson, meanwhile, commanded the Mediterranean fleet blockading Toulon. The conditions under which Nelson operated were punishing: his ships were poorly equipped and under-manned, the legacy of Lord St. Vincent’s peacetime reforms at the Admiralty. The winter gales of the Gulf of Lyon forced Nelson to keep his fleet at a distance from Toulon, and he was chronically short of frigates to maintain contact with the enemy. Despite these handicaps, Nelson kept the sea for ten consecutive months without a single ship refitting in port. His central problem was the inherent uncertainty of the defence: he could not know whether the Toulon fleet, once it escaped, would steer east for Egypt or west for the Atlantic, and he had to provide against both.

The West Indies Sortie

Villeneuve sailed from Toulon on 30 March 1805 with eleven ships-of-the-line, successfully evading Nelson’s blockade in a north-west gale. Contrary winds delayed Nelson’s pursuit, and by the time he learned of the French departure they had a lead of several weeks. Villeneuve passed through the Straits of Gibraltar, picked up a Spanish squadron at Cadiz, and crossed the Atlantic to Martinique, arriving in mid-May. The West Indies were not the ultimate objective: Napoleon intended the sortie to draw the British squadrons across the Atlantic, clearing the way for a concentration in the Channel.

Nelson pursued with characteristic energy. He guessed correctly that Villeneuve had gone west, crossed the Atlantic in hot chase — outstripping the French, who had a head start — and arrived at Barbados on 4 June, only to find that Villeneuve had already sailed north from Martinique. For two weeks Nelson searched the Caribbean, but Villeneuve had left the West Indies on 9 June, bound for Europe. Nelson’s pursuit, though it failed to bring the enemy to action, was strategically sound: by following Villeneuve he retained the possibility of interception, whereas remaining in the Mediterranean would have surrendered the initiative entirely.

Calder’s Action Off Cape Finisterre

The decisive moment of the campaign prior to Trafalgar occurred on 22 July 1805, when Villeneuve, returning from the West Indies, encountered a British squadron under Vice-Admiral Sir Robert Calder approximately one hundred miles west of Cape Finisterre. This engagement was a lost opportunity — one of the great ‘might-have-beens’ of naval history.

The encounter was precipitated by the alertness of the British Admiralty. A fast brig, the Curieux, had sighted Villeneuve’s fleet returning across the Atlantic and reached Plymouth on 7 July. Lord Barham, the aged First Lord of the Admiralty, acted on the intelligence with remarkable promptness. Within hours of receiving the dispatch, Barham ordered Cornwallis to raise the blockade of Rochefort, sending the five ships there to reinforce Calder off Ferrol, and directed Calder to cruise a hundred miles west of Finisterre with fifteen sail-of-the-line to intercept Villeneuve. The movement was a stroke of generalship comparable to Napoleon’s abandonment of the siege of Mantua in 1796 to throw himself upon the Austrian relief armies.

When the fog lifted on 22 July, Calder found himself facing twenty ships-of-the-line — the Curieux had reported only seventeen. The action that followed was inconclusive. Two Spanish ships were taken, but darkness and fog separated the fleets, and Calder, preoccupied with the safety of his prizes and anxious about the possibility of the Ferrol squadron emerging, declined to renew the engagement the following day. Calder had ‘made to himself a picture’ — that classic error against which Napoleon warned his own generals — and allowed the imagined dangers of the Rochefort and Ferrol squadrons to blind him to the fact that he had the main enemy force before him. Nelson remarked that had Calder found himself alongside the same enemy fleet he would have beaten them soundly even against superior numbers.

The Strategic Reversal

Villeneuve, no more eager than Calder to renew the action, put into Vigo on 28 July and then into Coruña on 1 August, where he received Napoleon’s dispatches. The emperor, who had been at Boulogne awaiting news, learned of the action on 8 August. The failure of the Toulon fleet to appear in the Channel, combined with the damage suffered in Calder’s action and Villeneuve’s evident hesitation, forced Napoleon to confront the collapse of his invasion plan.

This was the hinge of the entire campaign. The Grande Armée, which had been training for the Channel crossing for two years, could not remain indefinitely in its coastal camps. Austria and Russia were forming the Third Coalition, and the strategic situation on the Continent demanded Napoleon’s attention. On 27 August 1805 — the pivotal date — the emperor ordered the army of Boulogne to break camp and march east. The invasion of England was abandoned. The Grande Armée was now to strike at Austria.

The destruction of the combined fleet made the Channel crossing impossible. Napoleon turned east not because he preferred to fight Austria but because the sea was closed to him. The campaign that ended at Austerlitz was thus determined by the naval campaign that ended at Trafalgar.

The Battle of Trafalgar

Villeneuve, after a period of indecision in Cadiz, put to sea on 19 October 1805 with the combined Franco-Spanish fleet of thirty-three ships-of-the-line. Nelson, who had returned to England in August and resumed command of the Mediterranean fleet at the end of September, was waiting off Cadiz with twenty-seven ships. The ensuing battle was the consummation of Nelson’s tactical thought.

Nelson’s plan was a departure from the conventional line-of-battle tactics that had governed fleet engagements for a century. Instead of forming his fleet in a single line parallel to the enemy and exchanging fire at long range, Nelson divided his force into two columns and drove them perpendicularly at the Franco-Spanish line, cutting it in two places. The idea was to concentrate overwhelming force on the enemy’s rear while leaving the van unable to turn back to support it. The risk was that the leading ships of each column would have to endure a concentrated fire from the enemy line for a prolonged period without being able to reply effectively.

The battle was fought on 21 October 1805, approximately fourteen miles off Cape Trafalgar. Nelson’s column, in the Victory, broke through the allied line astern of Villeneuve’s flagship Bucentaure; Collingwood’s lee column penetrated the rear. The engagement that followed was a confused melee in which the superior British gunnery and ship-handling proved decisive. By the end of the afternoon, nineteen Franco-Spanish ships had been captured or destroyed; the remainder were scattered. Nelson himself was shot by a marksman from the rigging of the Redoutable and died at four-thirty in the afternoon, having learned that the battle was won.

A violent storm followed the battle. The British fleet, with its shattered prizes in tow, was caught by a gale that raged for several days. Many of the captured ships were wrecked on the Spanish coast or had to be scuttled; only four of the nineteen prizes ever reached a British port. The storm cost the British much of the material fruits of the victory, but it could not undo the strategic result: the combined fleet had ceased to exist as a fighting force.

Strategic Consequences

The destruction of the Franco-Spanish fleet gave Great Britain command of the seas for the remainder of the Napoleonic Wars — command that was never again seriously challenged. Napoleon could still build ships, but he could not assemble a fleet capable of meeting the British in a general action. The naval war became a war of attrition and commerce raiding, a contest in which sea power, though less dramatically exercised than at Trafalgar, steadily drained the resources of the French Empire.

The more far-reaching consequence is that Trafalgar caused Austerlitz. The point is not merely chronological — that Trafalgar was fought on 21 October and Austerlitz on 2 December 1805 — but causal. By defeating Villeneuve, Nelson made the invasion of England impossible, and Napoleon was therefore compelled to turn his army against the only enemy he could reach: Austria and Russia. The campaign that culminated at Austerlitz was a strategic diversion forced upon Napoleon by the failure of his maritime design. The emperor had not chosen to fight the Third Coalition on the Danube; he had been driven to it because the sea had been closed to him. The Grande Armée that marched from Boulogne to Ulm and Austerlitz was the army that had been meant to conquer England, and it marched east only because Nelson had barred the way to the west.

Napoleon continued to hold the initiative on land, and Austerlitz was a victory of the first order. But the decisive defeat of the war had already been inflicted — not at Austerlitz, but at Trafalgar. Nelson’s victory did not end the war, but it established the condition under which all subsequent campaigns were fought: British command of the sea, and French confinement to the Continent.
