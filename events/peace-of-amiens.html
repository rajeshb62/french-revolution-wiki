The Peace of Amiens

The Collapse of the Second Coalition

By the autumn of 1801, Europe had fought itself to exhaustion. The war of the Second Coalition had begun in 1799 with high hopes: Russia, Austria, England, Turkey, Naples, and Portugal arrayed against a French Republic still reeling from the Directory’s corruption and military reverses. Suwarrow’s Russian armies had swept through northern Italy, Masséna had been besieged in Genoa, and the Republic itself seemed on the verge of collapse. But the tide turned. Masséna’s victory at Zurich saved Switzerland; Bonaparte returned from Egypt and seized power on 18 Brumaire; and in the spring of 1800 he crossed the Great Saint Bernard, fell upon the Austrian rear at Marengo, and shattered the Coalition’s prospects in a single afternoon. Moreau’s victory at Hohenlinden in December completed the work. By February 1801, Austria had signed the Treaty of Lunéville, confirming the French conquests of Campo Formio and extending French dominion to the left bank of the Rhine.

Great Britain alone remained at war. For two years she had sustained the Coalition with subsidies and her own maritime war; now she stood isolated, with no continental ally and the Armed Neutrality of the Northern Powers closing the Baltic to her shipping. The assassination of Tsar Paul I in March 1801 dissolved the Northern League before it could fully form, but it did not remove the underlying strategic problem: Britain had been fighting for eight years, her national debt had reached staggering proportions, and the war party led by William Pitt had given way to the more pacifically inclined ministry of Henry Addington. The country was weary. Pitt’s resignation in February 1801 over Catholic Emancipation had brought to power a government that wanted peace and needed peace.

The preliminaries were signed in London on the first of October, 1801. The very day after the signatures were dry, news reached London of the surrender of Alexandria to British arms — the final expulsion of the French from Egypt. It was believed that Bonaparte had possessed this intelligence at the time of signing but had concealed it, choosing to attribute to France the gracious act of restoring Egypt to the Porte. Whether or not this was true, it set the tone for everything that followed: a peace in which neither party trusted the other, and in which both believed they had been outmanoeuvred.

The Preliminaries of London

The preliminaries established the broad outlines that the definitive treaty would confirm. Great Britain agreed to restore most of her colonial conquests — the Cape of Good Hope to the Batavian Republic, the West Indian islands to France and Holland — retaining only Trinidad, ceded by Spain, and the Dutch possessions in Ceylon. France, for her part, recognised the independence of the Ionian Republic and agreed to evacuate Naples and the Papal States. The island of Malta was to be restored to the Knights of St. John under a guarantee of its independence and neutrality.

To contemporaries, these terms appeared generous to France — excessively so, in the eyes of the British Opposition. Pitt’s supporters had warned that any peace resting on the good faith of Bonaparte was a delusion. But the Addington ministry calculated that the relief of peace, the reduction of taxation, and the restoration of commercial intercourse were worth the sacrifice of conquests that would have been costly to defend in any case. The City of London received the news with jubilation. Illuminations, bonfires, and public subscriptions for the wounded marked the announcement. English tourists began packing their trunks for Paris.

The Negotiations at Amiens

The definitive treaty was to be negotiated at Amiens, a cathedral town in Picardy chosen for its neutrality and its proximity to both Paris and the Channel ports. The British government sent Lord Cornwallis — the same general who had surrendered at Yorktown twenty years earlier, now an elder statesman of unimpeachable integrity — as its plenipotentiary. He arrived in Amiens on the first of December, 1801, and found no one there to meet him.

The delays that followed were deliberate. The French and Dutch envoys trickled in, but the Spanish plenipotentiary failed to appear at all. On various pretexts the negotiations were spun out through the winter. The cause was not French procrastination alone; Cornwallis himself complained bitterly of the tergiversations of his interlocutors. But the chief beneficiary of delay was Bonaparte, who used the months of negotiation to consolidate his position in Europe by actions that had not been contemplated when the preliminaries were signed.

In December 1801, the Cisalpine Republic — whose independence had been guaranteed by the Treaty of Lunéville — adopted a new constitution drawn up under Bonaparte’s personal inspection. Delegates were summoned to Lyon, where the First Consul presided over a consulta that offered him the presidency of the republic. He accepted on the 26th of January, 1802. A few days later the name Cisalpine was changed to Italian, a modification whose aggressive implications were understood in every chancery in Europe. The same man who ruled France now wielded the power of four million Italians.

The news reached London and caused consternation. Many who had supported the peace now clamoured for war. The Addington ministry, however, chose to ignore what had passed so publicly and pressed forward with the negotiations. But by March, patience had expired. A number of ships were ordered into commission, extensive naval preparations begun, and an ultimatum dispatched to Amiens: Cornwallis was instructed to leave in eight days if the terms were not accepted.

Bonaparte had too much at stake on the seas to risk a rupture. The definitive treaty was signed on the 27th of March, 1802 — 6 Germinal, Year X of the French Republic.

The Treaty of Amiens

The treaty did not differ materially from the preliminaries, except in the article of Malta, which was elaborated at length. The Order of the Knights of St. John was to be restored to the island, with the provision that no citizen of either Great Britain or France could thereafter be a member. The neutrality and independence of the Order and the island were proclaimed. The British forces were to evacuate within three months of the exchange of ratifications, upon condition that a Grand Master was on the spot to receive possession and that two thousand Neapolitan troops — sent by the King of Naples, himself a neutral and a weak power — were present as a garrison. The independence of the island was placed under the guarantee of Great Britain, France, Austria, Spain, Russia, and Prussia — whose acquiescence had not been obtained in advance.

These elaborate provisions concealed an essential weakness: the entire structure depended on the active co-operation of powers who had not yet agreed to participate. When the moment came to execute the treaty, this clause would prove the lever by which Great Britain justified retaining Malta long after the stipulated period had elapsed.

Beyond the Maltese question, the treaty provided for the restoration of Porto Ferrajo in Elba (which Britain had held by force of arms), the evacuation of Egypt, and the exchange of prisoners. The cession of Louisiana by Spain to France — known to the British ministry at the time of the preliminaries but publicly unavowed — was not mentioned in the treaty. The boundary of French Guiana obtained from Portugal was pushed back off the Amazon, but no reference was made to the secret treaties by which Bonaparte had already secured commercial advantages from Turkey and territorial gains from Portugal.

The Peace in Europe

The Treaty of Amiens was received in both countries with profound relief. In France, the joy was unbounded. Paganel, writing from the perspective of 1815, observed that the peace was regarded by the French as a triumph wrung from the weakness of the Sea Power — a vindication of Bonaparte’s genius for war and diplomacy. The Legislature was convoked in extraordinary session to ratify the treaties and measures of the government, and the Treaty of Amiens was presented as the crowning work of the First Consul’s statesmanship. For his services, Bonaparte was proposed for a striking mark of public acknowledgment: the Consulate for life, which was approved by plebiscite in August 1802.

Yet even at the moment of celebration, Paganel noted that the public joy was mingled with sad presentiments. The advantages obtained by France at Amiens were regarded by the discerning as forced concessions, dictated by necessity. The contracting parties, in signing the peace, remained in hostile dispositions; and the Consul, more than any other, in a threatening attitude. ‘The Peace of Amiens,’ Paganel wrote, ‘was only a concerted truce between the enemy powers of France.’

For the English, the peace opened Paris to travellers who had not visited the Continent in a decade. The influx of British tourists was one of the most remarked phenomena of the period: they crowded the Palais-Royal, attended the Opéra, climbed the towers of Notre-Dame, and marvelled at the works of art that Bonaparte had plundered from Italy. The painter Benjamin West received permission to visit the Louvre; Charles James Fox, the great Whig statesman, made a celebrated journey to Paris and was received by Bonaparte himself. For fourteen months, the guns were silent and the Channel was open.

The Breakdown

The peace was never allowed to settle. From the moment the treaty was signed, Bonaparte continued the restless political activity that had characterised the negotiation itself. He seemed incapable of treating the peace as final; for him, it was merely a breathing-space in which to consolidate French domination of the Continent.

The first shock came in September 1802, when Bonaparte formally incorporated Piedmont and Elba into France. Piedmont had been organised as a French military department since April 1801, but the decree of annexation changed a de facto condition into a de jure one — a permanent encroachment that menaced Austria’s Italian possessions and destroyed the territorial integrity of the Kingdom of Sardinia. No compensation was given to the Sardinian king, and both Great Britain and Russia had sought earnestly to obtain some reparation for him.

Next came Switzerland. In the spring of 1802, Bonaparte had detached the Valais from the Helvetic Confederation and proclaimed it independent, securing the Simplon route into Italy. He then withdrew French troops, leaving the Swiss cantons to settle their own affairs. The opposing parties — Unitarians who favoured the French Revolution and Aristocrats who sought to return to the old constitution — fell into civil conflict. The aristocratic party gained the upper hand. Bonaparte, declaring that he would not ‘deliver the formidable bastions of the Alps to fifteen hundred mercenaries paid by England,’ sent thirty thousand French soldiers under General Ney into Switzerland. All opposition ceased.

The emotion of Europe was profound. The British ministry, which had acquiesced in the annexation of Piedmont in silence, finally spoke. On the 10th of October 1802, the British cabinet dispatched a vigorous remonstrance to Paris and, on the same day, sent a special envoy to the Swiss frontiers with instructions to offer the Swiss pecuniary succour if they would resist. The ministry then took a further step: orders were sent to the West Indies, Dutch Guiana, and the Cape of Good Hope directing that the colonies ordered to be restored should be retained until further instructions.

Bonaparte received the British remonstrance with fury. He dictated instructions to his minister in London that were characterised even by the Bonapartist historian Thiers as ‘truly extraordinary.’ He threatened that if war arose, the coasts of Europe from Hanover to Taranto would be closed to British commerce; that Liguria, Lombardy, Switzerland, and Holland would be converted into French provinces; that Great Britain herself would be invaded by a hundred thousand soldiers. ‘The first consul was but thirty-three,’ he said. ‘He had as yet destroyed only states of the second order. Who knows how long it would take him, if forced thereto, to change again the face of Europe and revive the Empire of the West?’

The crisis over Switzerland passed without immediate rupture — the Swiss had not in fact risen, and the British envoy was recalled — but the damage was irreparable. From that moment, both sides began preparing for war while still nominally at peace. The British ministry recalled its orders to retain the colonies, but the second vessel carrying the countermand might or might not overtake the first. The uncertainty poisoned the atmosphere.

The Question of Malta

Bonaparte, having failed to overawe the British cabinet, now pressed for the evacuation of Malta. The ministry had its own reasons for delay. No Grand Master had yet accepted the sovereignty of the island; Spain, under Bonaparte’s influence, had suppressed the revenues of the Order within her limits; and doubt existed whether the Order’s income would suffice to maintain the island’s defences. More fundamentally, the British public had never reconciled itself to surrendering a position so vital to the control of the Mediterranean and the approaches to India.

The ministry took broader ground. Lord Whitworth, the new British ambassador in Paris, was instructed to affirm Great Britain’s right to interfere in continental affairs whenever her interests required it, and to point out that the annexation of Piedmont, the invasion of Switzerland, and the renunciation of the Grand Duke of Parma had materially altered the conditions since the treaty. These changes, the ministry argued, entitled Great Britain to compensations — of which the retention of Malta was the most obvious.

Bonaparte responded by publishing, on the 30th of January 1803, Colonel Sébastiani’s report on his mission to the Levant. Sébastiani, dispatched the previous September to visit Tripoli, Egypt, Syria, and the Ionian islands, reported that the Eastern peoples revered Bonaparte, that the Ionian islands were ready to declare themselves French at the first opportunity, and — in the passage that caused a storm in London — that ‘six thousand French troops would now suffice to conquer Egypt.’

The publication of this report in the official Moniteur was a deliberate provocation. It was intended to intimidate the British ministry into compliance on Malta; it achieved the opposite. The British government replied that, in view of Sébastiani’s report, the king would not evacuate Malta until substantial security was provided against the purposes therein revealed.

The Rupture

From this point, the slide to war was inexorable. On the 20th of February 1803, Bonaparte sent a message to the Legislature in which he declared that ‘two parties strive for power’ in England — one for peace, one for war — and concluded with the words: ‘England, alone, cannot to-day contend against France.’ The British government responded on the 8th of March by sending a message to Parliament that, in consequence of military preparations in the ports of France and Holland, the king judged it expedient to adopt additional measures of precaution.

The final negotiations were conducted in an atmosphere of mutual ultimatum. Great Britain offered to cede Malta in perpetuity to Britain (Bonaparte replied that he would sooner see the British on the heights of Montmartre), or alternatively to hold the island for ten years as security. Bonaparte refused. On the 12th of May 1803, Lord Whitworth demanded his passports and left Paris.

On the 16th of May, Great Britain declared war against France. The following day, Admiral Cornwallis sailed from Plymouth with ten ships-of-the-line and resumed the watch off Brest. On the 18th, Nelson hoisted his flag on board the Victory at Portsmouth and sailed for the Mediterranean.

The Peace of Amiens had lasted fourteen months.

The Anatomy of Failure

Mahan’s analysis of the failure of Amiens is an anatomy of the fundamental incompatibility between Bonaparte’s ambition and the security of the British state. The peace was conceived, Mahan argues, under a double illusion: that Bonaparte would be content with the territorial arrangements of Lunéville, and that Great Britain could surrender her maritime conquests without receiving in return a stable continental equilibrium.

The first illusion was shattered within weeks of the signature. The second persisted longer but was no less delusive. Mahan observes that the peace was never anything but an ‘armed truce,’ a condition in which both parties maintained large military establishments while pretending to have laid down their arms. The fundamental cause of the rupture was Bonaparte’s conviction that ‘in the existing state of Europe England cannot reasonably make war, alone, against us’ — a conviction he stated openly and acted upon in his interventions in Italy, Switzerland, and Germany.

From the naval perspective, the peace was a disaster for France. Mahan notes that the respite did not restore French sea power; it merely revealed how thoroughly that sea power had been annihilated. The French navy had lost Egypt, Malta, and its best colonial possessions. The expedition to Saint-Domingue — Bonaparte’s grand project to reassert French authority in the Caribbean — was consuming armies whole in the yellow fever epidemic. By the time war resumed, France was no stronger at sea than she had been in 1801, while British naval preparations had been driven forward by the very tensions that preceded the rupture.

Paganel’s analysis, written from the French side in the immediate aftermath of Waterloo, is more sombre. He sees the Peace of Amiens as the apogee of the Consulate — the moment when Bonaparte stood highest in European estimation — and simultaneously as the beginning of the descent. The peace was not genuinely European; it was a bilateral arrangement between two powers that distrusted each other, imposed upon a continent that had not been consulted. ‘The advantages that France obtained by the Treaty of Amiens,’ Paganel writes, ‘were regarded with some reason as forced concessions, dictated by necessity. The contracting parties, in signing the peace, remained in hostile dispositions; and the Consul, more than any other, in a threatening attitude.’

The war that followed was longer, harder, and more terrible than anything that had preceded it. The Peace of Amiens was the last chance for a general settlement in Europe for a generation. It failed not because the terms were inadequate but because the peace rested on no foundation of trust and no balance of power that Bonaparte was willing to respect. In Mahan’s concluding judgment, the rupture demonstrated that a maritime power and a continental power could not coexist in equilibrium so long as one of them was led by a man who ‘would never accept the independence of others as a final condition of the European system.‘
