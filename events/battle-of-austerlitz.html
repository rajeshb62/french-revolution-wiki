The Battle of Austerlitz

The Army from the Sea

On the 27th of August 1805, the emperor gave the order that changed the direction of the war. The Grande Armée, which for two years had been encamped on the heights above Boulogne, watching the white cliffs of England across the grey Channel waters, was to break camp and march east. One hundred and thirty thousand of the most brilliant soldiers of the age — the men who had fought in Italy, in Egypt, in Germany — turned their backs on the sea and began the movement that would carry them, in three months, from the shores of the English Channel to the field of Austerlitz.

The decision was not a choice but a necessity. The invasion of England, the great design that had occupied Napoleon since the rupture of the Peace of Amiens in May 1803, had collapsed at sea. Vice-Admiral Villeneuve, returning from the West Indies with the combined Franco-Spanish fleet, had been intercepted by Calder off Cape Finisterre on 22 July; the action was inconclusive, but Villeneuve had taken refuge in Coruña and then in Cadiz, his will broken. The Toulon fleet that was to have covered the crossing of the Grand Army was scattered and contained. Nelson was returning to his command. The Channel remained closed.

Napoleon understood the meaning of the failure with perfect clarity. “If I am master of the Straits for six hours,” he had said, “I shall be master of the world.” The six hours never came. The navy had failed him, and the invasion was impossible. But the army that could not cross the sea could still march across a continent. Austria and Russia, encouraged by British gold and British diplomacy, had formed the Third Coalition; they believed Napoleon was fixed on the Channel coast, his army tied to its flat-bottomed boats. They were about to learn how quickly an emperor who could not sail could still strike.

The Wheel of the Grande Armée

The movement that followed was one of the most extraordinary in the history of war. The Grande Armée, organized into seven corps under the ablest marshals of the age — Bernadotte, Davout, Soult, Lannes, Ney, Marmont, Augereau — was marched not directly toward Austria but eastward along the Channel coast, then southward through France, crossing the Rhine on a broad front between Strasbourg and Mannheim. The speed was phenomenal: the corps marched separately but converged with the precision of a machine, each commander knowing only his own part but the whole co-ordinated from a single mind.

Napoleon had not waited for his army to assemble before acting. On 25 September, while the Austrian army under General Mack was advancing into Bavaria, the French corps began to cross the Rhine. The plan was Napoleonic in its audacity: instead of advancing directly on the Austrian positions, the Grande Armée would wheel to the south-east, passing north of the Austrian army, crossing the Danube behind it, and cutting its lines of communication with Vienna. The Austrian commander, an able theorist who had never commanded an army in the field, found himself beset by an enemy that seemed to be everywhere at once. On 6 October, the French crossed the Danube at Donauwörth; by the 11th, the Austrian army, which had advanced confidently into Germany, was isolated and surrounded in the fortress of Ulm.

The encirclement of Ulm was a masterpiece of what the nineteenth century would call strategy of annihilation. Mack, with some sixty thousand men, waited for a Russian army that was still marching through Moravia, too far away to help. On 20 October, with his supplies exhausted and his escape routes blocked, Mack surrendered his entire army to Napoleon. Twenty-seven thousand prisoners, sixty guns, and the whole of the Austrian field army in Germany were lost. The soldiers of the Grande Armée had not fought a pitched battle; they had marched the enemy into the ground.

The news reached Napoleon on the same day that Nelson, far away off Cape Trafalgar, was breaking the line of the combined fleet and receiving the mortal shot that would kill him. The two events, separated by a continent and an ocean, were not unrelated. Napoleon’s decision to turn east had been forced upon him by the failure of his naval combinations; but once he had turned, he moved with a speed and decisiveness that made the Third Coalition seem to be fighting in slow motion.

Vienna

The surrender at Ulm left the road to Vienna open. The remnants of the Austrian army and the approaching Russian forces under General Kutuzov retreated eastward, keeping the Danube between themselves and the French. Napoleon advanced without pause: on 13 November, the Grande Armée entered Vienna unopposed. The imperial capital was abandoned by the court, deserted by the government; the emperor Francis had fled to Brünn, into the arms of his Russian allies.

Napoleon established himself at Schönbrunn and for a moment seemed to be master of everything that the Hapsburg monarchy had built over three centuries. But the occupation of Vienna was a strategic complication as much as a triumph. The Grande Armée had marched five hundred miles in two months. Its supply lines were stretched across the breadth of Germany; the harvest of 1805 had been poor, and the army, which had been provisioned for a campaign of weeks, was now entering the winter months far from its magazines. The Russian army, under the overall command of the Tsar Alexander and the immediate direction of Kutuzov, had not been destroyed. It was retreating eastward, drawing the French deeper into the frozen landscape of central Europe, husbanding its strength for a battle on ground of its own choosing.

The allied plan was as old as war itself: trade space for time. The Prussians were arming in the north; the winter was advancing; if the Austrian and Russian armies could hold out without fighting a decisive action, Napoleon would be forced either to retreat or to fight at a disadvantage. The emperor knew this perfectly. He had perhaps a month — six weeks at most — in which to force a battle before the weather and the converging armies of the Coalition would make his position untenable.

This was the argument that Paganel would later make, and Mahan would make it with even greater force: Trafalgar caused Austerlitz. The naval defeat of 21 October did not merely make the invasion of England impossible; it made a safe retreat impossible. The British blockade had cut the French Empire’s maritime commerce; the harvest of 1805 could not feed an army indefinitely. Napoleon could not winter in Vienna; he could not return to France with his work unfinished; he could not wait for the Russian and Austrian armies to double their strength. He had to fight, and he had to fight soon. The battle, when it came, would be imposed by necessity as much as by design.

The Pratzen Heights

The allied army — approximately eighty-five thousand Russians and Austrians under the nominal command of Kutuzov but the effective direction of the Tsar Alexander, who had joined the army in person — took up its position on the Pratzen Heights, a gently sloping plateau that dominated the landscape around the little Moravian town of Austerlitz, some sixty miles north of Vienna. The position was strong: the heights commanded the approach from the west, and the allied army was concentrated on the high ground, with the Austrian contingent on the right and the Russians on the left and centre.

Napoleon’s army, perhaps seventy-three thousand men, arrived before the allied position and — by deliberate design — appeared to be in a state of dangerous weakness. The emperor had marched his corps into positions that suggested he was overextended: the French right, on the Goldbach stream, was held by a thin line of troops; the left, anchored on the Santon hill, was more strongly held but still seemed dangerously stretched. Napoleon was offering a trap, and he relied on the Tsar’s inexperience and the allied eagerness to accept the bait.

He did not rely in vain. The allied war council, dominated by the young Tsar and the Austrian chief of staff Weyrother, against the experienced Kutuzov’s prudent advice, adopted a plan as bold as it was misguided. They would turn the French right, cutting Napoleon’s line of retreat to Vienna, rolling up the army from south to north, and crowding the French against the frozen marshes and lakes that lay at the southern end of the battlefield. To accomplish this, the allied centre would abandon the Pratzen Heights and march south, overwhelming the thin French right wing with a crushing superiority.

It was precisely what Napoleon had hoped for. If the allies abandoned the Pratzen Heights to attack his right, the heights themselves would be exposed. Soult’s corps, concealed from view by the morning mist that rose from the Goldbach valley, was ordered to be ready to take them the moment the allied centre moved away.

The Sun of Austerlitz

The 2nd of December 1805 — the first anniversary of Napoleon’s coronation — dawned cold and foggy. The allied army had marched through the night, executing its great turning movement. At first light, the attack on the French right began in earnest. The village of Telnitz fell; the outnumbered French defenders under Davout — who had marched his corps sixty miles from Vienna in forty-eight hours to reinforce the threatened wing — fought a desperate holding action, falling back from position to position, buying time with the lives of his men.

At approximately eight o’clock, Napoleon rode along the front of the centre. The mist was beginning to clear. Through the thinning fog, Soult’s division commanders could see that the Pratzen Heights, which had been crowded with allied troops the night before, were now emptier. The allied centre had indeed abandoned the plateau to join the attack on the French south.

Napoleon turned to Soult. “How long will it take you to reach the heights?”

“Sire,” Soult replied, “they will be at the summit in twenty minutes, if the enemy does not obstruct us.”

“Then wait a quarter of an hour,” said the emperor.

At nine o’clock, Soult’s corps — twenty thousand men who had been lying in the valley, invisible to the allies behind the fog and the folds of the ground — rose and advanced. The sun, which had been obscured all morning, broke through the mist at the same moment, shining full in the eyes of the allied troops on the heights. It was the famous “sun of Austerlitz” — a thing of legend to the soldiers who remembered it, an omen that convinced the army that destiny was with them.

Soult’s advance rolled up the thin crust of allied troops still holding the Pratzen. The allied centre collapsed within two hours. Kutuzov, unable to persuade the Tsar to react in time, watched the destruction of his army’s centre from the heights as the French poured onto the plateau in irresistible force.

The battle then broke into two halves. On the allied left, the Russian Imperial Guard attacked the French centre north of the Pratzen in a desperate counterattack that came closer to success than any other part of the allied effort that day. The Russian Guard cavalry, splendidly mounted and magnificently brave, broke through the French infantry and threatened Napoleon’s own headquarters. Rapp counter-charged with the Guard cavalry — the famous Mamelukes of the Imperial Guard, the grenadiers à cheval — and a ferocious mêlée developed on the heights. The French Guard eventually held, but only after a bitter struggle that left the Russian Guard shattered.

On the allied right — the southern flank, which had been the scene of the original allied attack — the turn of events transformed the Austrian-Russian effort from an offensive to a desperate retreat. The French right, reinforced by Davout’s hard-marching corps, was no longer merely holding its ground but pressing forward. The allies who had attacked in the morning found themselves attacked in turn, driven southward across the battlefield, their retreat blocked by the frozen lakes — the ponds of Menitz and Satschan.

What happened on the frozen lakes has passed into legend. Thousands of allied soldiers, trying to escape across the ice, found their path blocked. French artillery fired on the ice, which broke under the weight of horses, guns, and men. The official story — that the ice opened and swallowed whole battalions — is almost certainly exaggerated; but the image of the cold, dark waters of the Satschan pond receiving the drowned bodies of the Tsar’s soldiers became one of the fixed memories of the Napoleonic wars. The allied army disintegrated. By four in the afternoon, the battle was over.

The numbers tell part of the story. Fifteen thousand allied dead and wounded; twelve thousand prisoners; a hundred and eighty guns captured. French losses were perhaps eight thousand. But the numbers do not capture the completeness of the victory. The allied army that had marched confidently toward Austerlitz on the morning of 2 December had ceased to exist as a fighting force. The Russians retreated eastward in disorder; the Austrians had no army left in the field. The Third Coalition had been shattered in a single day.

The Treaty of Pressburg

The Tsar Alexander, his army broken, retreated into Poland and refused further operations. The emperor of Austria, Francis II, had no such luxury. His capital was occupied; his army was destroyed; he could not continue the war. On 4 December, he met Napoleon in person at the Spaleny Mill, near the battlefield, and agreed to an armistice. The negotiations that followed were swift; Napoleon dictated the terms.

The Treaty of Pressburg, signed on 26 December 1805, was the most sweeping settlement Napoleon had yet imposed. Austria recognized Napoleon as King of Italy; ceded Venetia, Istria, and Dalmatia to the French kingdom of Italy; surrendered the Tyrol to Bavaria and the Vorarlberg and several Swabian territories to Württemberg; paid an immense indemnity; and acknowledged the complete independence of the sovereigns of Bavaria, Württemberg, and Baden, who were elevated to kingdoms and bound by treaty to Napoleon. Austria was reduced from a great power to a secondary state; it had lost three million subjects and large portions of its revenue and territory.

The Treaty of Pressburg, in Paganel’s judgment, was not a peace but a suspension of arms — like Campo Formio and Lunéville before it. Napoleon treated the treaty as he treated a plan of campaign: he commanded it. The pride of the House of Austria was humiliated, but not destroyed, and Paganel saw in this humiliation the seeds of further wars. “He did not suffer the council,” Paganel wrote, “when he negotiated peace, any more than when he planned or made war.” The consequences were not long in coming.

The Confederation of the Rhine and the End of the Holy Roman Empire

The reorganization of Germany that followed Pressburg was more far-reaching than the territorial settlement with Austria. In July 1806, sixteen German states — Bavaria, Württemberg, Baden, Hesse-Darmstadt, and others — formally withdrew from the Holy Roman Empire and entered into a new Confederation of the Rhine, with Napoleon as its Protector. The Confederation committed its members to provide sixty-three thousand troops to the French Emperor in the event of war; in return, they received territorial gains, sovereign status, and the protection of the French Empire.

The Holy Roman Empire, which had existed in one form or another for a thousand years, was effectively dissolved. In August 1806, the Emperor Francis II — who had also been Holy Roman Emperor Francis II — laid down the imperial crown. The ancient structure that had claimed to be the secular arm of Christendom, that had survived the Reformation, the Thirty Years’ War, and the armies of Louis XIV, was ended by a stroke of Napoleon’s pen.

The Victory That Cost Everything

The victory at Austerlitz was Napoleon’s most brilliant achievement as a commander — the battle that above all others confirmed his claim to genius. The combination of strategic audacity (the march from Boulogne), operational speed (the encirclement at Ulm), tactical deception (the deliberately weakened right wing, the abandonment of the Pratzen), and the perfect timing of the execution created a victory that became the model for everything that followed.

But Paganel — writing from the perspective of a man who had been silenced by the Empire and who now spoke freely from its ruins — saw Austerlitz as the moment when Napoleon’s ambition crossed a threshold from which there was no return. “The battle of Austerlitz,” he wrote, “was decisive against the united powers and against ourselves. It swelled the pride and the ambitious pretensions of Napoleon.” The emperor who had been content to be First Consul of the French Republic was now the master of Europe; the emperor who had crossed the Pratzen Heights that December morning had destroyed the Third Coalition, but he had also destroyed any possibility of moderation in his own soul.

The deeper analysis belongs to Mahan, who saw in the campaign of 1805 the proof of his great thesis about sea power. Trafalgar, fought six weeks before Austerlitz, had made the victory on land possible only by making the victory at sea impossible. Napoleon had not chosen to fight at Austerlitz; he had been driven there by the failure of his maritime design, by the British blockade that choked his supplies, by the harvest crisis that forced him to seek a decision before winter closed the roads. The Grande Armée that marched from Boulogne to the Pratzen was the army that had been meant to conquer England, and it marched east only because Nelson had barred the western passage. Austerlitz was a victory of the first order, but it was a victory won on ground that Napoleon had not chosen, under conditions that he had not desired, against enemies who had forced his hand.

The structure of the European war after Austerlitz reflected this paradox. Napoleon was supreme on land; no continental power could stand against him in the field. But he was confined to the continent. The sea belonged to England; the commerce of the world flowed through English ports; the colonial empires of France and Spain and Holland had been stripped away or blockaded into ruin. The emperor who had conquered Europe could not command the ocean that surrounded it.

In the years that followed, Napoleon would try to break the English hold by economic warfare — the Continental System, the Berlin Decree, the Milan Decree — closing every European port to British goods, attempting to starve the island into submission. It was the strategy of a land power trying to do what it could not do at sea. It would lead him into Spain, into Portugal, into Russia; it would drain the Grande Armée in a hundred small wars and one great catastrophe; it would bring him finally to Waterloo and to Saint Helena.

But all of that was still in the future on the evening of 2 December 1805, when Napoleon rode across the Pratzen Heights under the fading winter sun, past the bodies of fifteen thousand dead and dying men, past the shattered remnants of the Russian Guard, past the guns that had been captured from the emperor of Austria. He was thirty-six years old. He had won the greatest victory of the age. He was already, in the precise sense that Mahan would later define, a prisoner of the sea.
