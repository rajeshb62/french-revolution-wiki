9 Thermidor

I. The Law of 22 Prairial

By the summer of 1794, the Revolution had turned inward. The foreign armies had been pushed back from the frontiers — the victory of Fleurus in June had secured the Belgian frontier, and the Coalition no longer threatened Paris — but the Terror, far from slackening with the passing of the external danger, had reached a new and terrible intensity. On the twenty-second of Prairial, Year II (the tenth of June, 1794), the Convention, at the insistence of Robespierre and the Committee of Public Safety, passed the law that would give its name to the final phase of the revolutionary government.

The Law of 22 Prairial transformed the Revolutionary Tribunal into an engine of condemnation without appeal. It suppressed the preliminary examination of accused persons; it allowed the judges to convict on moral proof — on the inner conviction of the jurors — without the need for material evidence; it denied the accused the assistance of counsel; it reduced the trial itself to a single question: Did the prisoner appear before the Tribunal as an enemy of the people? Those who answered in the affirmative were sentenced to death. There was no other possible verdict.

What followed has been called the Great Terror. In the forty-nine days between the passage of the Law of 22 Prairial and the fall of Robespierre, the Revolutionary Tribunal condemned more than thirteen hundred persons to death — a rate of execution that exceeded anything that had come before. The prisons of Paris were crowded with suspects; the tumbrils rolled daily from the Conciergerie to the Place de la Révolution; the guillotine, which had once been reserved for the great enemies of the Revolution, now fell upon the humblest of citizens — servants who had spoken ill of the Republic, peasants who had evaded the requisitions, old women who had prayed for the King. The blade was never dry.

The deputies of the Convention watched this acceleration with a fear that was personal and immediate. The Law of 22 Prairial had not specified who might be brought before the Tribunal; it had only removed every obstacle to conviction. Any deputy could be denounced; any deputy could be arrested; any deputy could be sent to the guillotine within days of accusation. The Revolutionary Tribunal had already consumed the Hébertists in March, the Dantonists in April; the Committee of Public Safety had purged the Cordeliers, the Indulgents, and all who stood between. There was no reason to suppose that the Convention itself — those deputies who had voted for the execution of the King, who had voted for the proscription of the Girondins, who had given the Committee its powers — would be spared. On the contrary: the more the Terror accelerated, the more the list of the denounced grew, and the more the deputies saw their own names whispered in the corridors of the Tuileries.

II. The Conspiracy

The conspiracy that formed against Robespierre in the weeks before Thermidor was not a conspiracy of principles but of fears. It was a coalition of the Convenient, as Pagès called it — an alliance of men who had nothing in common but their terror of the guillotine. The Mountain had been Robespierre’s instrument; the Terror had been his policy; and those who had served him most faithfully now had the most reason to dread him.

The centre of the plot was Tallien, a deputy who had been sent on mission to Bordeaux and who, having enriched himself there and having married the beautiful Thérésia Cabarrus, had returned to Paris to find that the Committee of Public Safety was preparing his arrest. Tallien had been one of the fiercest Jacobins; he had voted for the death of the King and for the proscription of the Girondins. But he was marked. His mistress had been arrested; his own head was in question. He had nothing to lose.

With Tallien came Barras — the former nobleman, the man of pleasure and violence, who had commanded the army of the South and had returned to the capital to watch the Terror consume its authors. Fréron, who had been sent to Marseilles and had distinguished himself by his brutality, now found that the same violence that had served the Committee might be used against him. Fouché, the former oratorian, the man of cold calculations and unshakeable nerve, had been recalled from Lyon, where he had presided over the fusillades and the noyades in the name of revolutionary justice; Robespierre had denounced him publicly, and Fouché knew that his life depended on the destruction of the man who had made him.

And there was Carnot — the great organiser of victory, the man who had raised the armies of the Republic — who had quarrelled with Robespierre over the conduct of the war, who had watched the Committee’s political domination encroach upon his military authority. The Committee itself, which had once been the solid instrument of the revolutionary government, was now divided: Billaud-Varenne and Collot d’Herbois, who had been responsible for the Terror in Lyon and feared that Robespierre would make them its scapegoats; Saint-Just, who remained loyal to Robespierre to the end; and Barère, who trimmed his sails to every wind.

The conspirators met in secret. They dared not speak openly; Robespierre still commanded the loyalty of the Jacobin Club, the sections of Paris, and the Commune. But they met — in the corridors of the Tuileries, in the houses of the deputies, in the gardens of the Palais-Royal — and they laid their plans. They knew that Robespierre was preparing a speech to the Convention, a speech in which he would denounce his enemies by name. They knew that he was waiting for the right moment. And they knew that they must strike before he did.

III. The Speech of 8 Thermidor

On the eighth of Thermidor — the twenty-sixth of July, 1794 — Robespierre rose in the Convention to deliver the speech that would seal his fate.

He spoke for two hours. It was a long, rambling, terrible speech — not the clear denunciation his friends had expected, but a vague and menacing accusation against unnamed conspirators. Robespierre denounced a plot against the Republic, a conspiracy of the corrupt and the ambitious who sought to destroy the revolutionary government. He spoke of deputies who had enriched themselves, of false patriots who had committed crimes in the name of the Revolution and now sought to turn the public hatred against the Committee. He called for the purification of the Convention, the punishment of the wicked, the defence of virtue against the assaults of vice.

But he would not name names.

The Convention listened in silence — a cold, hostile silence that Robespierre mistook for agreement. He demanded that his enemies be punished; he refused to say who they were. He invoked the Committee of Public Safety; he attacked the Committee of General Security; he called for the reconstruction of both, but would not say which of their members he accused. The deputies sat frozen, each man wondering whether his name was among the denounced, each man waiting for the blow that would fall from a hand he could not see.

When Robespierre finished, there was a long pause. Then Cambon — the financier of the Revolution, the man who had raised the armies and fed the nation — rose to speak. He was a member of the Committee of Public Safety; he had worked with Robespierre for months. Now he turned on him with a fury that shocked the Assembly. “It is time to tell the truth,” Cambon said. “One man alone paralyzes the will of the Convention. That man is Robespierre.”

Others followed. The silence broke into a storm of accusation. Robespierre, pale and trembling, tried to speak again, but his voice was drowned by the cries of the deputies. He had come to the Convention expecting to destroy his enemies; he left it with the knowledge that he had given them the weapon they needed.

That evening, at the Jacobin Club, Robespierre recovered his voice. He read the same speech to the assembled Jacobins, and this time the reception was very different. The Jacobins applauded him; they embraced him; they swore to defend him against the traitors of the Convention. Billaud-Varenne and Collot d’Herbois, who had followed him from the Convention into the Club, were driven from the tribune; they barely escaped the fury of the Jacobins, who would have torn them to pieces. The Club voted to expel all who had spoken against Robespierre; the Commune of Paris prepared to support him.

The lines were drawn. That night, Paris was a city divided against itself, waiting for the morning.

IV. The Ninth of Thermidor

The twenty-seventh of July, 1794 — the ninth of Thermidor, Year II of the Republic — began in confusion. The Convention assembled in the morning with a tension that could be felt in the air. The galleries were packed; the deputies were armed; the streets outside were filled with the patrols of the Commune and the loyal battalions of the revolutionary sections.

Saint-Just rose to speak. He was Robespierre’s closest ally, the most brilliant orator of the Mountain, the man who had written the most terrible decrees of the revolutionary government. He had prepared a speech in defence of Robespierre — a calm, measured, devastating indictment of the conspirators who had plotted against the Incorruptible. He began to read.

He was not allowed to finish.

Tallien interrupted him. The deputy from the Indre-et-Loire, who had been waiting for this moment, who had prepared his own speech, who had nerved himself for the act that would either save his life or end it, rose from his bench and demanded the floor. “I ask the permission of the Assembly to speak a few words,” he said, “and I hope that I shall be allowed to do so.”

Saint-Just hesitated. Tallien began to speak, and his voice was the voice of all the deputies who had trembled through the Great Terror. He had armed himself with a dagger — he brandished it before the Assembly. “I have armed myself with a dagger,” he cried, “to pierce the heart of the new Cromwell if the Convention does not have the courage to decree his accusation!” The words were a signal. The Mountain, which had been Robespierre’s Mountain, broke apart. The deputies rose from their benches; the hall filled with shouting; the President’s bell was drowned by the uproar.

Tallien demanded the arrest of the Robespierre’s creatures — Henriot, the commandant of the National Guard of Paris; Dumas, the President of the Revolutionary Tribunal; the members of the Committee of General Security who had served the Terror. The Convention, caught in the current of accusation, voted the arrests without debate. Then Tallien, seconded by Billaud-Varenne and Collot d’Herbois, turned on Robespierre himself.

“I demand the arrest of Maximilien Robespierre!” Tallien shouted.

The Convention roared its approval. Robespierre tried to speak — tried to mount the tribune, tried to make himself heard above the din. His voice was drowned. He turned to the deputies of the Right, the Plain, the men who had never spoken against him, who had voted as he wished, who had survived by silence. “For the last time,” he cried, “will you hear me?” No one answered. The President, Thuriot, who had been chosen by the conspirators, rang his bell and refused him the floor.

Then the decrees were passed. The Convention ordered the arrest of Maximilien Robespierre, his brother Augustin Robespierre, Louis-Antoine Saint-Just, Georges Couthon, Philippe Le Bas, and François Hanriot. The deputies of the Mountain, who had been Robespierre’s followers, watched in silence as the men who had commanded the Revolution were led from the hall. Robespierre was taken to the Luxembourg prison; Saint-Just to the Écossais; Couthon to the Bourbe; the others to their separate prisons.

V. The Rising of the Commune

But the Convention had not reckoned with the Commune of Paris.

Word of the arrests reached the Hôtel de Ville within hours. The Commune, which had been Robespierre’s most faithful instrument, did not accept the authority of the Convention. It ordered the tocsin to be rung; it ordered the sections to rise; it ordered the prisons to be opened and the arrested deputies to be liberated.

The revolution of the Commune against the Convention began in the afternoon and continued through the night. The sections of the Faubourg Saint-Antoine and the Faubourg Saint-Marceau, which had marched on the Tuileries on the tenth of August, rose again. The National Guard of Paris, which had been commanded by Hanriot, answered the call of the Commune. The gates of the Luxembourg were forced; Robespierre was taken from his cell and carried in triumph to the Hôtel de Ville.

But Robespierre, released from prison, hesitated. He had been a prisoner; he had seen the Convention turn against him; he had felt the hand of power slip from his grasp. The Commune urged him to lead the insurrection, to march on the Convention, to crush the conspirators who had arrested him. But Robespierre could not act. He was exhausted; he was uncertain; he had spent his life in the service of the Revolution, and he could not bring himself to attack the Convention that embodied it. He sat in the Hôtel de Ville, waiting, while the hours passed and the night deepened.

The Commune, meanwhile, worked with desperate energy. It issued decrees in Robespierre’s name; it arrested the commissioners of the Convention; it summoned the sections to arms; it prepared the barricades. The tocsin rang from the church towers; the drums beat the générale; the streets of Paris filled with armed men. By nine in the evening, the Hôtel de Ville was a fortress, surrounded by the battalions of the loyal sections, guarded by the gunners of the National Guard. The Commune believed that it held Paris.

VI. The Defection of the Sections

But the Commune had overestimated its power.

The Convention, which had wavered through the afternoon, recovered its nerve. It appointed Barras to command the armed force of the Convention; it declared the Commune outlawed; it sent commissioners to the sections of Paris — not to demand submission, but to argue, to persuade, to remind the citizens of Paris that the Convention was the sovereign power of the nation. And the sections, one by one, began to abandon the Commune.

The National Guard, which had marched to the Hôtel de Ville in the afternoon, began to melt away in the night. The men were tired; they had been under arms since morning; they had no clear orders; they saw the Convention arrayed against the Commune and did not know whom to follow. Barras, who had commanded armies in the field, moved through the streets with the calm authority of a man who had long since passed beyond fear. He spoke to the commanders of the sections; he promised amnesty; he threatened the guillotine. By midnight, the battalions that had surrounded the Hôtel de Ville had begun to disperse.

The Convention decreed the outlawry of Robespierre and his allies. The decree had a single, terrible consequence: any man outlawed by the Convention could be executed on sight, without trial, without appeal. The price of Robespierre’s head was set at the stroke of a pen.

VII. The Second Arrest

At the Hôtel de Ville, the night was passing in confusion. Robespierre sat in the great hall of the municipal building, surrounded by the revolutionary officials of the Commune, waiting for a decision that would not come. He had been urged to march; he had refused. He had been urged to issue a proclamation; he had hesitated. The Commune, which had risen for him, now began to see that he would not lead it.

At two in the morning of the tenth of Thermidor, the forces of the Convention arrived at the Hôtel de Ville. The doors were forced; the guards dispersed; the hall filled with the men of the Convention. Robespierre, hearing the noise, rose from his chair. He knew what was coming.

He tried to take his own life. He had a pistol; he put it to his mouth and pulled the trigger. The shot shattered his jaw but did not kill him. He fell to the ground, covered in blood, his face a ruin of bone and flesh. The soldiers of the Convention found him lying on the floor, moaning, his teeth scattered across the stones.

The others died or were captured. Couthon, whose legs had been paralysed from the waist down by a spinal disease, was thrown from a window by the soldiers; he broke his body on the pavement below and was taken up for execution. Le Bas, the deputy who had been Robespierre’s most faithful disciple, was more successful in his suicide; he shot himself dead in the chamber of the Hôtel de Ville and was not taken alive. The younger Robespierre — Augustin, who had followed his brother to the end — threw himself from a window and survived, broken, to be executed with the others. Hanriot, the commander of the National Guard, was dragged from a sewer where he had tried to hide.

The prisoners were taken to the Conciergerie. Robespierre, his jaw shattered, his face bound in a bloody cloth, lay on a table in the antechamber of the prison, waiting for the morning. He was not given a trial; he was not given a bed; he was not given water. The men who had arrested him did not wish to hear him speak.

VIII. The Tenth of Thermidor

The morning of the tenth of Thermidor was clear and hot. The tumbrils were prepared at the Conciergerie, as they had been prepared every day for months, but this time the prisoners were different. This time, the men who had made the Terror were going to die by the instrument they had perfected.

Robespierre was lifted onto the tumbril. He could not stand; he could not speak; his shattered jaw hung open, bound by a cloth that was soaked through with blood. The crowd that lined the streets did not jeer at him; they did not cheer; they watched in a silence that was more terrible than any cry. Some said that he looked like a corpse already — that the face that had dominated the Revolution, that had spoken from the tribune of the Convention, that had sent thousands to the guillotine, was no longer a face at all but a mask of suffering.

The tumbrils passed through the streets of Paris, along the same route that the condemned had taken every day since the Terror began. The crowd grew as the procession approached the Place de la Révolution. The guillotine stood where it had always stood — at the foot of the statue of Liberty, on the spot where the King had died eighteen months before. The blade was ready.

Robespierre was carried to the scaffold. The executioner tore the bandage from his face; the shattered jaw gaped open; a cry of pain and rage escaped from the man who could no longer speak. The crowd saw the face of the Incorruptible — the face of the man who had sent Danton to death, who had purged the Hébertists, who had made the Law of 22 Prairial — and for a moment, there was silence. Then the blade fell.

The head of Maximilien Robespierre was lifted by the executioner and shown to the people. A great cry rose from the crowd — not the “Vive la République” that had greeted the death of the King, but a cry of relief, of joy, of a nation that had been liberated from a nightmare. The people danced in the streets. The prisons of Paris, which had been filled with suspects, opened their gates. The news spread through France: the Terror was over.

IX. The Thermidorian Reaction

The fall of Robespierre was the end of the Terror, but it was not the end of the Revolution. The Thermidorian Reaction — the period that followed the execution of the Incorruptible — was a slow and chaotic unwinding of the revolutionary government. The Committees of Public Safety and General Security were stripped of their powers; the Revolutionary Tribunal was reformed; the Jacobin Club was closed; the Law of 22 Prairial was repealed. The prisoners of the Terror, who had filled the prisons of France, were released by the thousands. The men who had conspired against Robespierre — Tallien, Barras, Fréron, Fouché — became the masters of the Convention.

But the Reaction had its own violence. The Jacobins were hunted through the streets of Paris; the sections that had supported Robespierre were disarmed; the memory of the Terror was punished in the bodies of those who had served it. The jeunesse dorée — the gilded youth of the Reaction — patrolled the boulevards with clubs and cudgels, beating the Jacobins who dared to show themselves. The White Terror in the provinces surpassed in some places the Red Terror it replaced.

Louvet, who had been proscribed with the Girondins and who returned from hiding after Thermidor, found a city transformed. The men who had denounced him were now the men in power; the men who had sent him into exile were now the men in the prisons. He walked the streets of Paris — the same streets he had walked as a deputy of the Convention, as a proscript, as a fugitive — and he saw the same faces, the same fears, the same shifting allegiances that had marked the Revolution from its beginning. The fall of Robespierre did not bring the Republic that Louvet had dreamed of; it brought only a change of masters.

X. The Meaning of Thermidor

Benjamin Constant, writing two years after the event, gave the Thermidorian coup its most penetrating analysis. The fall of Robespierre, he argued, was not merely the destruction of a tyrant but the demonstration of a fundamental truth about the nature of revolutionary government. The Terror had been based on the principle of arbitrary power — the power of a committee to arrest, to judge, to execute without appeal. That power had been turned against the very men who had wielded it, and the Revolution had discovered, in the most terrible way, that arbitrary power is a weapon that can be used by any hand.

“The government that had claimed the right to strike down all its enemies,” Constant wrote, “had itself been struck down by a faction of its own creation. The men who had proscribed the Girondins, who had sent Danton to the scaffold, who had filled the prisons with suspects, now found themselves the objects of the same justice they had invented. They had built a machine of destruction that could not be controlled; and when they tried to stop it, it devoured them.”

The lesson of Thermidor, for Constant, was that a government founded on force alone carries within itself the seeds of its own destruction. The revolutionary government had no foundation but the will of those who wielded it; and that will could change, could divide, could turn against itself. The Terror had consumed its creators; the Revolution had devoured its own children. And France, exhausted, bleeding, and uncertain, stood at the beginning of a new phase of its history — a phase in which the forms of liberty would be slowly restored, and the substance of power would pass into other hands.

The ninth of Thermidor did not end the Revolution. But it ended the Terror. And in doing so, it ended the Revolution as it had been known — the Revolution of the guillotine, of the Committees, of the Law of Suspects, of the revolutionary tribunals that had judged and condemned without appeal. What followed would be slower, more complex, and in some ways more terrible. But the heart of the Terror had been cut out on the Place de la Révolution, and the man who had personified it lay in the common grave of the executed, his shattered jaw bound in a bloody cloth, his name cursed by the people he had tried to save.

  • Pierre Pagès, Histoire secrète de la Révolution française (6 vols., 1801). Pagès provides the primary narrative of the events of 8–10 Thermidor, Year II: the Law of 22 Prairial, the formation of the conspiracy in the Convention, the speeches of 8 and 9 Thermidor, the arrest of Robespierre, the rising of the Commune, the second arrest at the Hôtel de Ville, the execution, and the Thermidorian Reaction.
  • Jean-Baptiste Louvet, Mémoires de Louvet (1823). Louvet, a Girondin deputy who had been proscribed and forced into hiding, returned to Paris after Thermidor; his account captures the atmosphere of the days following the fall of Robespierre — the release of prisoners, the joy of the city, the changing of the political guard.
  • Benjamin Constant, De la force du gouvernement actuel et de la nécessité de s’y rallier (1796). Constant analyzes Thermidor as a theoretical demonstration of the instability of arbitrary government: a revolutionary power founded on force alone must eventually turn against its own agents, proving that no faction can safely wield a power that has no foundation but will.
