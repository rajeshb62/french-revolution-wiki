The Battle of Copenhagen

The Baltic Expedition

Great Britain, by the spring of 1801, stood alone against all Europe. The Armed Neutrality of the Northern powers—Russia, Sweden, Denmark, and Prussia—had closed the Baltic to British shipping and threatened the supply of naval stores upon which the Royal Navy depended. To break this combination, a fleet of eighteen sail-of-the-line and thirty-five smaller vessels was assembled at Yarmouth on the east coast of England.

The chief command was given to Sir Hyde Parker, a gallant and respectable officer of the old school, but one whose caution was ill-suited to a campaign demanding audacity and speed. As his second-in-command, the government sent Horatio Nelson—the hero of the Nile, the most aggressive and brilliant fighting admiral in the British service. The choice of Parker over Nelson was a mistake that would have been avoided had Lord St. Vincent, who succeeded Earl Spencer as First Lord a few weeks later, been in office when the expedition was planned. Nelson was still among the junior flag-officers, and his conduct in the Mediterranean under the influence of Lady Hamilton had perhaps raised doubts about his fitness for so delicate a command. Whatever the reason, the withholding of the chief command from Nelson weakened the expedition from the start.

The fleet sailed from Yarmouth on the 12th of March, 1801. By the 19th, despite a heavy gale that had scattered the ships, nearly all were collected off the Skaw, the northern point of Jutland at the entrance to the Kattegat. The wind was fair for Copenhagen, and Nelson chafed at delay. “While the negotiation is going on,” he said, “the Dane should see our flag waving every moment he lifted his head.” But Parker sent the envoy forward in a single frigate while the fleet waited.

On the 21st the fleet was off Elsinore. The envoy rejoined them: Denmark had rejected the British terms. The time for negotiation had passed; it only remained to act, and the wind was favourable—an advantage that at any moment might be lost. Nelson immediately addressed a letter to Parker summing up the situation with luminous clarity. “Not a moment should be lost in attacking,” he wrote. “We shall never be so good a match for them as at this moment.” He pointed out that the fleet should have been off Copenhagen when the negotiation failed; then the Danish ships could have been destroyed at once. “Almost the safety, certainly the honor, of England is more entrusted to you than ever yet fell to the lot of any British officer,” he concluded.

The Plan of Attack

Copenhagen lies on the east coast of the island of Zealand, fronting Sweden across the Sound. Before the city, shallow flats extend for half a mile from the shore, and in the Sound itself, little more than a mile out, lies a long shoal called the Middle Ground. Between these two bodies of shallow water runs the King’s Channel, through which heavy ships could sail. At the northern end of this channel, a deep pocket stretches toward the harbour, defended by powerful works rising on piles out of the shoal water—the formidable Three-Crown Batteries. The Danes had moored a line of armed hulks and floating batteries along the inner edge of the King’s Channel, covering the front of the city.

The natural point of attack appeared to be from the north, but Nelson rejected this approach, which he dismissed as “taking the bull by the horns.” Not only was the head of the Danish line exceedingly strong, but the wind that was fair to attack would be foul to return. A disabled ship would have no escape but by running the gauntlet of the entire Danish line. More importantly, so long as the British fleet remained in the Sound without entering the Baltic, the way was left open for the Swedes and Russians to join the Danes. Nelson therefore advised that a sufficiently strong force should attack the southern end of the Danish line, interposing between Copenhagen and the Baltic.

Nelson’s letter of March 24, 1801, was a masterpiece of military reasoning. He concluded with a suggestion worthy of Bonaparte himself: a detachment to Revel to destroy the Russian squadron there. “The measure may be thought bold,” he wrote, “but I am of opinion the boldest are the safest; and our country demands a most vigorous exertion of her force, directed with judgment.” He saw the Northern League as a tree, with Paul of Russia as the trunk and Sweden and Denmark as the branches. “If I can get at the trunk and hew it down, the branches fall of course; but I may lop the branches and yet not be able to fell the tree, and my power must be weaker when its greatest strength is required.”

At Revel lay twelve Russian ships-of-the-line—fully half their Baltic fleet. Their destruction would have paralyzed the naval power of the empire. But Parker was not the man for such a stroke. He adopted Nelson’s plan for the attack on Copenhagen but not the broader strategic conception. Nelson was given twelve ships-of-the-line—two more than he had asked—and the task of destroying the Danish floating defences.

The Sounding of the King’s Deep

The nights of the 30th and 31st of March were employed sounding the King’s Channel. It was perilous work. The pilots were mostly mates of merchantmen trading with the Baltic, and their experience in vessels of three or four hundred tons did not fit them for the charge of heavy battleships. They betrayed throughout a great indecision that would contribute to the principal mishaps of the day.

On the 1st of April the fleet moved up to the north end of the Middle Ground, about four miles from the city. That afternoon, Nelson’s division got under way, passed through the outer channel, and anchored towards sundown off the south-east end of the shoal, two miles from the head of the Danish line. Nelson announced his purpose to attack as soon as the wind served. The night was passed in arranging the order of battle.

The Danish line stretched in a north-west to south-east direction for somewhat over a mile. It was composed of hulks and floating batteries, eighteen to twenty in number, mounting six hundred and twenty-eight guns, of which about three hundred and seventy-five would bear on the engaged side. The southern flank was partly supported by works on shore, but from the intervening shoal water these were too distant for thoroughly efficient fire. It was this southern extremity that Nelson had chosen as his point of assault—the weakest part of the Danish defence, and the one that would interpose his ships between the Danes and their allies in the Baltic.

The Battle of 2 April 1801

The morning of the 2nd of April brought the wind fair from the south-south-east. At eight o’clock the British captains were summoned to the flagship for their final instructions. At half-past nine the signal was made to weigh.

The ships were soon under sail, but the difficulties of pilotage were immediately apparent. The Agamemnon, of sixty-four guns, was unable to weather the point of the Middle Ground and had to anchor out of range, taking no share in the battle. Worse followed: the Bellona and the Russell, seventy-fours both, the fourth and fifth ships in the order of battle, entered the King’s Channel but kept too far to the eastward and ran ashore on the farther side—upon the Middle Ground itself. They were not entirely out of action, but they were beyond the range of efficient gunnery. Nelson’s flagship, the Elephant, passed clear, as did the rest of the heavy ships, but the loss of these three vessels from the line exposed the northern part of the British order to a weight of fire quite disproportioned to its strength. A body of frigates very gallantly undertook to fill the gap.

Thus, from the very beginning, Nelson fought with a third of his force crippled or missing. The battle that followed was not the neat tactical victory he had planned; it was a slogging match, a test of sheer endurance and gunnery against an enemy fighting under the eyes of his own capital.

The battle was at its height by half-past eleven. There was no more manoeuvring—only the simple question of efficient gunnery and endurance. Around two in the afternoon, a great part of the Danish line had ceased to fire, and the flagship Dannebrog was in flames. But the Danish defence was stubborn and resourceful. Throughout the action, fresh crews were frequently sent from the shore to replace the men who had fallen. In several cases, new arrivals reached ships that had already struck their colours and renewed the fight, either through ignorance or indifference. The land batteries fired on British boats attempting to take possession of their prizes.

The Signal and the Blind Eye

It was at this moment that Sir Hyde Parker, watching from the London some four miles to the north, saw the smoke and confusion and drew the worst conclusions. The Agamemnon, Bellona, and Russell were aground; the frigates were taking punishment they were never designed to bear; and the whole British line seemed to be struggling in a trap from which escape would be difficult. Parker raised the signal for recall—Signal No. 39—ordering his ships to discontinue the action.

On the deck of the Elephant, Nelson saw the signal as it rose to the masthead of the commander-in-chief. He understood perfectly what it meant: Parker feared the fleet was being destroyed and wished to withdraw before the losses became catastrophic. Nelson also understood that withdrawal was impossible—his crippled ships could not beat their way out of the King’s Channel against the wind without being raked by the undamaged Crown Batteries. To retreat was to invite destruction. To press the attack was the only hope.

He turned to his flag-captain, Thomas Foley. “You know, Foley,” he said, “I have only one eye. I have a right to be blind sometimes.” Then, raising his telescope to his blind eye, he looked deliberately toward the London and said: “I really do not see the signal.”

Whether he meant the signal was hidden by the smoke or simply chose to ignore it matters little. The gesture has become the emblem of his genius: the deliberate disobedience, the calculated insubordination, the willingness to stake his reputation and his life on a judgment that contradicted the orders of his superior. He ordered his own signal for closer action to be kept flying, and the battle continued.

The Flag of Truce

Nelson seized on the circumstance of the reinforced Danish crews to bring the affair to a conclusion. He wrote a letter addressed “To the brothers of Englishmen, the Danes,” and sent it under a flag of truce to the Crown Prince, who was in the city:

“Lord Nelson has directions to spare Denmark when no longer resisting; but if the firing is continued on the part of Denmark, Lord Nelson will be obliged to set on fire all the floating batteries he has taken, without having the power of saving the brave Danes who have defended them.”

The letter was sent ashore by a British officer who had served in the Russian navy and spoke Danish. The engagement continued until about three o’clock, when the whole line of floating defences south-east of the Crown Batteries had either struck or been destroyed. The Crown Prince demanded the precise purport of the first message. Nelson took a high hand. He had destroyed the part of the enemy’s line he had attacked, but it was now essential to withdraw his crippled ships—and with the existing wind, that could only be done by passing the Crown Batteries, which had been but little injured. He wrote a second letter, politicly ignoring this danger:

“Lord Nelson, with humble duty to His Royal Highness, begs leave to say that he will ever esteem it the greatest victory he ever gained, if this flag of truce may be the happy forerunner of a lasting and happy union between my most gracious Sovereign and His Majesty the King of Denmark.”

Having written the letter, he referred the bearer for definite action to Sir Hyde Parker, four miles off—foreseeing that the long pull there and back would give time for the leading ships, much crippled, to clear the shoals, though their course lay close under the Crown Batteries. Thus the exposed part of the British fleet was successfully withdrawn. Three of the five ships-of-the-line that had borne the heaviest fighting were so damaged that they grounded in the shallows and had to be towed off; one of them, the Monarch, lost her rudder and was forced to steer with sails alone. Desperate measures were required to bring them round the shoals, but by nightfall the squadron had rejoined Parker to the north of the Middle Ground. The fleet was safe.

The Armistice

The result of the battle was to uncover the front of Copenhagen and lay it, with its dockyards and arsenals, open to bombardment. The bomb vessels could now be placed safely in the King’s Channel. Denmark had to choose whether fear of her powerful allies and zeal for neutral claims should lead her to undergo further punishment, or whether the suffering already endured was excuse enough for abandoning the coalition.

Nelson conducted the negotiations himself. He insisted upon an armistice of fourteen weeks, after which hostilities could be resumed on fourteen days’ notice. This would assure Parker complete immunity for his communications and leave Nelson free to act against the Russian fleet—the object he had never lost sight of. During the discussions, the Crown Prince received news that changed everything: the Czar Paul I had been murdered in his palace at St. Petersburg on the night of the 23rd of March—the night after the battle. The Danish government concealed the tidings, but the departure of the soul of the confederacy relieved their worst fears and encouraged them to yield to Nelson’s demands.

The armistice was signed. Denmark’s part in the Armed Neutrality was suspended.

The Aftermath

Among the first measures of the new Czar, Alexander I, was the release of the British seamen imprisoned by his father. On the 12th of April the British ships entered the Baltic—much to the surprise of the Northern powers, who had thought their heavy draught would prevent their passage. Parker anchored in Kioge Bay and there awaited further instructions, but Nelson entirely disapproved of this inactivity. Russia might yield, but she would be more likely to do so if the British fleet lay off Revel.

The British ministry, however, had finally taken the measure of Parker. Four days after news of the victory reached London, orders were issued relieving him and leaving Nelson in command. Taken only a week after a victory, this could scarcely be construed as anything but an implied censure. “They are not Sir Hyde Parker’s real friends who wish for an inquiry,” Nelson wrote. “His friends in the fleet wish everything of this fleet to be forgot, for we all respect and love Sir Hyde; but the dearer his friends, the more uneasy they have been at his idleness, for that is the truth—no criminality.”

Nelson’s first signal upon receiving command was to hoist the boats aboard and prepare to weigh. On the 7th of May the fleet left Kioge Bay, and on the 12th appeared off Revel. But the Russian division had sailed three days before and was now safe under the guns of Cronstadt. The bird was flown. Nelson remained in the Baltic, awaiting the issue of negotiations. Russia meant peace: on the 17th of May the Czar ordered the release of the embargoed British ships. On the 4th of June Great Britain released the Danes and Swedes detained in her ports.

On the 17th of June 1801, a convention was signed at St. Petersburg between Russia and Great Britain, settling the points in dispute. The Armed Neutrality had dissolved.

The Tactical Significance

The Battle of Copenhagen was in many respects Nelson’s most difficult action. Unlike the Nile, where he had struck a fleet at anchor with the advantage of surprise and a clear tactical plan, Copenhagen was a battle fought against a prepared defensive line in waters so treacherous that a third of his attacking force was neutralized before firing a shot. The victory was won not by manoeuvre but by the sheer weight of British gunnery and the iron determination of the man in command.

The action demonstrated the offensive spirit in its most extreme form. Nelson’s refusal to obey Parker’s signal of recall was an act of calculated insubordination that has been debated by naval thinkers ever since. Was it justified? The answer lies in the result: had Nelson obeyed, his crippled ships would have been destroyed in attempting to withdraw, the Danish line would have remained intact, and the Armed Neutrality would have held. By disobeying, he won the battle, broke the Northern League, and secured the Baltic for British commerce.

Yet the campaign was not what Nelson had wished it to be. He had wanted to strike at Revel before Copenhagen, to destroy the Russian fleet at its anchorage and shatter the Northern League at its source. He was overruled by his cautious superior, and the Russian ships escaped destruction. The opportunity never came again. It was the great lost chance of the Baltic campaign—one that Nelson would always regard as the true object of the expedition.

Still, the battle itself stands as a monument to Nelson’s genius. The navigation of the King’s Channel in the face of an armed enemy, the coolness with which he accepted the loss of a third of his force and fought on, the timing and wording of the flag of truce, the deception by which he gained time for his crippled ships to escape—these were not the actions of a mere fire-eater. They were the calculations of a commander who understood that in war, the boldest course is often the safest, and that a successful disobedience is its own justification.
