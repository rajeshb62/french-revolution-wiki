Sources Map

This page provides a guided overview of every source page in the wiki — 22 source pages covering 27 distinct works (counting multi-volume and multi-title collections). They are grouped by type to help you navigate the material efficiently. Each entry describes the author’s perspective, the date range covered, and the key events the source illuminates.

Memoirs & Eyewitness Accounts

The memoirists are the wiki’s most vivid windows into the Revolution. Most wrote in extremis — from prison, in hiding, or looking back at lives upended — which gives their testimony an intensity no later historian can match. The Girondin cluster (Roland, Buzot, Louvet) covers the same events from different angles and can be read as a single overlapping testimony.

Madame Roland — Mémoires

roland-memoires-vol1-1823 and roland-memoires-vol2-1823

Date range: Childhood (1754) to November 1793

Madame Roland, the intellectual centre of the Girondin circle, wrote these memoirs in the Abbaye and Sainte-Pélagie prisons between August and November 1793, knowing she would face the Revolutionary Tribunal. Volume I is autobiographical — her childhood, convent education, the reading of Plutarch and Rousseau, her marriage, and her husband’s first ministry. Volume II is political: the second Roland ministry, both imprisonments, devastatingly sharp portraits of every major figure (Robespierre, Danton, Brissot, Buzot), and the trial documents. Her perspective is Girondin, republican, fiercely anti-Montagnard. She writes with the moral clarity of someone with nothing left to lose. The two volumes together are the single most important primary source for the Girondin experience of the Terror.

Buzot — Mémoires sur la Révolution Française

buzot-memoires-revolution-1823

Date range: c. 1789 – June 1794

François-Nicolas-Léonard Buzot (1760–1794), Girondin deputy for Eure and the man Madame Roland loved, wrote these memoirs while proscribed and in hiding after the coup of 31 May – 2 June 1793. He hid for nearly a year at Saint-Émilion before being found dead in a field. The memoirs cover the Convention, the factional struggle with the Mountain, the proscription, and his flight through Normandy and Brittany. He includes portraits of Marat, Robespierre, Danton, and Pache, and a sustained refutation of the charge that the Girondins were federalists. His perspective is Girondin, defensive, embittered — a testament written by a man who knew he was already condemned.

Louvet de Couvray — Mémoires

louvet-memoires-1823

Date range: October 1792 – July 1794

Jean-Baptiste Louvet de Couvray, Girondin deputy and author of the novel Les Amours du chevalier de Faublas, wrote these memoirs in April 1794 from a cave in the Jura, expecting to die. He survived and returned to the Convention after Thermidor. The memoirs are deliberately unpolished — “I throw down notes, and that is all,” he says. They cover the May–June 1793 coup, the Girondin resistance at Caen, Charlotte Corday’s mission to Paris, and his extraordinary clandestine flight across France. He is the author of the famous “Je t’accuse, Robespierre!” speech of 29 October 1792. His perspective is Girondin, dramatic, fugitive. The rawness is the value — this is not retrospective literary memoir but a record written under mortal danger.

Madame Campan — Mémoires sur la vie privée de Marie-Antoinette

campan-memoires-marie-antoinette-3vols-1823

Date range: c. 1770 – August 1792

Jeanne Louise Henriette Campan was première femme de chambre to Queen Marie-Antoinette. Her three-volume memoir is the most intimate royalist source in the wiki. Volume I covers the court of Louis XV and the early years of Louis XVI’s reign. Volume II runs from the Diamond Necklace Affair through the storming of the Tuileries on 10 August 1792. Volume III collects historical anecdotes and correspondence. She writes from inside the royal household, with access to the queen’s private conversations and emotions. Her perspective is royalist, loyalist, court-insider — but she is not uncritical of the queen or the court, and her testimony on the October Days and 10 August is indispensable.

Gouverneur Morris — A Diary of the French Revolution

morris-diary-french-revolution-vol1-1789 and morris-diary-french-revolution-vol2-1790-1793

Date range: March 1789 – January 1793

Gouverneur Morris — American statesman, drafter of the U.S. Constitution, and United States Minister to France from 1792 — kept a diary beginning March 1789 that forms the most sustained foreign eyewitness account of the French Revolution in English. Volume I covers the States-General, the fall of the Bastille, the October Days, and Morris’s informal diplomatic mission to London. Volume II covers the Constitution of 1791, the Legislative Assembly, the Flight to Varennes, the storming of the Tuileries on 10 August, and the September Massacres. Morris was privately a royalist sympathiser (he secretly organised a 5,000-louis fund for the king) and publicly a cold-eyed analyst. His perspective is American constitutionalist, aristocratic, skeptical of revolutionary idealism. He provides the best outside view of the Revolution’s political mechanics.

Marmontel — Mémoires d’un père

marmontel-memoires-3vols-1804

Date range: 1723 – c. 1795

Jean-François Marmontel (1723–1799) was a leading philosophe, contributor to the Encyclopédie, and Secrétaire Perpétuel of the Académie Française. His posthumous memoirs (20 books across 3 volumes) cover his entire life. The first two volumes deal with the ancien régime — his provincial origins, literary career, the salons, his academic life. The third volume (Books XII–XX) covers the Revolution from the pre-1787 crisis through the Terror, written by a man who lost nearly everything in the revolutionary crisis. His perspective is moderate-constitutionalist, Enlightenment liberal, anti-Jacobin. He writes from inside the pre-revolutionary intellectual establishment, watching it be destroyed by the forces it helped unleash.

Baron de Besenval — Mémoires

besenval-memoires-4vols-1805

Date range: c. 1735 – July 1789

Pierre-Victor, baron de Besenval (1721–1791), was a Swiss-born lieutenant-general of the royal armies and intimate of the French court under Louis XV and Louis XVI. His four-volume posthumous memoir is structured as a series of topical articles rather than a continuous narrative. The volumes cover his Swiss origins, the Seven Years’ War, court intrigues under the Choiseul and Maupeau ministries, and — crucially — his own command of the troops around Paris during the July 1789 crisis. He was the senior royal commander on the ground when the Bastille fell, and was subsequently arrested and tried as a scapegoat. His perspective is royalist, military, courtier — from the inside of the Bourbon military establishment at the moment of its catastrophic failure.

Ernest Daudet — Three Works on the Counter-Revolution

police-chouans-consulat-empire-daudet-1895, conspirations-royalistes-midi-daudet-1881, and histoire-emigration-daudet-1908

Date ranges: 1790–1793, 1800–1815, and 1789–1814 respectively

Ernest Daudet (1837–1921), elder brother of Alphonse Daudet, was a professional historian who specialised in the diplomatic and political history of the Counter-Revolution. The three works form a trilogy covering the royalist resistance across the entire Revolutionary and Napoleonic period.

  • conspirations-royalistes-midi-daudet-1881 covers the early royalist conspiracies in southern France (1790–1793): the Camp de Jalès, the Saillans conspiracy, the Charrier uprising, set against the Catholic–Protestant conflict in the Gard and Ardèche. Perspective: archival historian, sympathetic to royalist subjects but methodologically rigorous.

  • police-chouans-consulat-empire-daudet-1895 covers Chouan resistance under Napoleon (1800–1815): the Clément de Ris kidnapping, the murder of Audrein, English spy networks in Bordeaux and Jersey. Perspective: police-and-conspiracy historian, drawing on official records.

  • histoire-emigration-daudet-1908 is Daudet’s synthetic masterpiece on the Bourbon emigration as a whole (1789–1814): Coblentz, Quiberon, the Fructidor deportations, the courts in exile at Hamm, Verona, Mitau, and Warsaw. He draws on French, Russian, Austrian, English, and Prussian archives, including the papers of the duc de Blacas and unpublished correspondence of Louis XVIII. Perspective: scholarly, royalist-leaning, but archivally exhaustive — the definitive secondary work on the emigration.

Hector Fleischmann — Les Filles Publiques sous la Terreur

filles-publiques-fleischmann-1908

Date range: 1793–1794

Hector Fleischmann (1882–1914) was a French historian who specialised in the social and sexual history of the Terror. This work examines the world of prostitution in Paris during the Terror, drawing extensively on police surveillance reports, archival documents, and contemporary pamphlets. It covers the Palais-Royal brothel culture, the Sainte-Amaranthe case, the Marquis de Sade, gambling, and erotic literature. Fleischmann’s perspective is social historian of the “underbelly,” deliberately sensational in subject but documentary in method. He refuses to generalise from the exceptional behaviour of prostitutes to conclusions about French society as a whole. This is the wiki’s primary source for the histoire des mentalités approach to the Terror.

Historical Works

These are secondary accounts written during or shortly after the Revolution, blending narrative history with the political commitments of their authors. They are themselves primary documents of how the Revolution was understood by its contemporaries and near-contemporaries.

Alfred Thayer Mahan — The Influence of Sea Power upon the French Revolution and Empire

mahan-sea-power-french-revolution-vol1 and mahan-sea-power-french-revolution-vol2

Date range: 1783–1812

Rear Admiral Alfred Thayer Mahan’s two-volume work (1892) applies his famous sea-power thesis to the Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars. Volume I covers 1783–1801 (the destruction of the French navy, the Nile, Cape St. Vincent, the Irish expeditions); Volume II covers the Second Coalition through Trafalgar, the Continental System, and Napoleon’s Russian invasion (Copenhagen, Amiens, the commerce war). Mahan’s argument is that Britain’s ultimate victory was determined less by Wellington’s armies than by the Royal Navy’s control of the seas — and that this control was the product of sustained national commitment to maritime strength. His perspective is American naval historian, strategic analyst, Anglo-Atlanticist. He writes to draw lessons for American naval policy.

François Pagès — Histoire secrète de la révolution française

pages-histoire-secrete-revolution-6vols-1801

Date range: Merovingian origins to Marengo (June 1800)

François Pagès (1745–1802) produced this ambitious six-volume synthetic history between 1799 and 1802 (second edition, revised just before his death). The title “secret history” signals his aim to reveal the hidden intrigues and conspiracies behind public events. He opens with a compressed constitutional history from the Merovingians through Louis XV (arguing that the estates-general were an ancient institution suppressed by absolutism), then covers the Revolution month by month through the Estates-General, the Bastille, the October Days, Varennes, the Convention, the Terror, Thermidor, the Directory, Bonaparte’s Italian and Egyptian campaigns, 18 Brumaire, and Marengo. His perspective is anti-Jacobin, moderate republican, pro-Bonaparte-as-restorer-of-order. He is scathing about Robespierre and Marat, admiring of Bonaparte, and deeply suspicious of both aristocratic reaction and popular extremism. The work includes embedded documents — letters, treaty texts, official reports — and lengthy character portraits of every major figure.

Fantin-Desodoards — Histoire Philosophique de la Révolution de France

fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-vol7-1801

Date range: 1796 – September 1797 (Volume VII)

Antoine Fantin-Desodoards (1738–1820) wrote a multi-volume synthetic history of the Revolution as it was still unfolding. Volume VII (4th edition, 1801) covers the Italian campaign of 1796–97 (Castiglione, Arcola, Rivoli, Mantua), the Babeuf Conspiracy, Moreau’s retreat, the Irish expedition, and the Fructidor coup of September 1797. His “philosophical” method means he pauses regularly to assess causes, moralise on consequences, and reflect on the relationship between constitutional forms and political reality. His perspective is moderately conservative republican — critical of the Terror, respectful of the Directory, suspicious of both Jacobin extremism and royalist reaction, and admiring of Bonaparte’s military genius.

Pierre Paganel — Histoire de Napoléon Bonaparte

paganel-histoire-napoleon-1815

Date range: Egyptian expedition (1798) to Saint Helena (1815)

Pierre Paganel, a former member of the legislature and former secretary-general of the Ministry of Foreign Relations, wrote this political and moral history of Napoleon’s career in the weeks immediately after Waterloo. The book is itself a primary document: Paganel had published a historical essay in 1810; Napoleon read it, was offended by the words “souveraineté du peuple” and “représentation nationale,” and ordered the edition seized. The preface frames everything that follows as a judgment delivered by a man who was silenced under the regime and now speaks freely. His perspective is liberal republican, anti-imperial, freshly liberated from censorship. He covers Napoleon’s career from Egypt through the Consulate, the Empire, the Russian campaign, the fall, and the Hundred Days, with the moral arc of a man watching a republic become a tyranny.

Brissot — À ses Commettans

brissot-a-ses-commettans-1793

Date range: August 1792 – May 1793

Jacques-Pierre Brissot de Warville, the leading Girondin journalist and politician, wrote the texts collected in this volume between August 1792 and May 1793 — just before his arrest, condemnation, and execution on 31 October 1793. The volume (published in London as a document of an enemy of Britain) contains four texts: a declaration to foreign powers on the king’s suspension, two reports on British hostility and the case for declaring war, and the May 1793 address to his constituents on anarchist domination of the Convention. Brissot’s perspective is Girondin, war-party, anti-Montagnard — arguing that the Republic can only be saved by crushing the anarchist faction within. The London publisher’s framing (“Faction Brissotine”) makes the English reception as interesting as the text itself.

Statistical & Reference Works

These are not narrative histories but systematic surveys — the bones of the revolutionary state, laid out in numbers and names.

Herbin de Halle et al. — Statistique Générale et Particulière de la France

herbin-statistique-france-1803

Date range: Circa 1803 (Consulate)

Volume VII of a collaborative statistical survey of France and its colonies, published in the year after the Treaty of Amiens. The volume covers exclusively the French colonial empire — Saint-Domingue, Guadeloupe, Martinique, Louisiana, Africa, and the Indies — with contributions from several named authors: Peuchet on commerce and colonies, Sonnini on climate and geography, Delalauze on agriculture, Gorsse on mineral productions, and Herbin on topography and population. The perspective is Consular-state statistical, encyclopaedic, practical — a snapshot of French imperial ambitions at their momentary post-Amiens peak, before the disaster of Saint-Domingue and the renewal of war with Britain.

Testu — Almanach Impérial, 1808

testu-almanach-imperial-1808

Date range: 1808 (single year)

The official annual directory of the French Empire for the leap year 1808, presented to the Emperor and King. Published by Testu, the Emperor’s own printer, it lists every named office-holder in the imperial state — ministers, councillors of state, prefects, generals, judges, university functionaries, diplomatic corps, and decorations — with their ranks, addresses, and appointments. The perspective is imperial-state self-description, administrative, official. It represents the Empire’s organisation at its most confident and complete, after Austerlitz and Tilsit but before the Spanish catastrophe and the Russian campaign.

Contemporary Political Analysis

Benjamin Constant — De la force du gouvernement actuel; Des réactions politiques; Des effets de la terreur

constant-de-la-force-gouvernement-1796

Date range: 1796–1797 (written under the Directory)

Benjamin Constant’s three pamphlets — his first sustained political writings — constitute the founding texts of French liberal political thought. De la force du gouvernement actuel (1796) argues for rallying to the Directory as the only viable Republic. Des réactions politiques (1797) and Des effets de la terreur (1797) respond to the Thermidorian and royalist backlash. Together they address the central problem of post-Terror politics: how to stabilise a republic without collapsing into either renewed Jacobinism or royalist reaction. Constant’s perspective is liberal, republican, constitutionalist — the first systematic attempt to found a French liberalism on the experience of revolutionary failure.

How to Use This Map

  • For the Girondin experience, read the Roland, Buzot, Louvet, and Brissot cluster together — they cover the same events (the 31 May coup, the proscription, the federalist revolt) from different personal angles.
  • For the court and royalist perspective, read Campan, Besenval, and Marmontel alongside Morris’s external commentary.
  • For military strategy, Mahan (naval) and Paganel (Napoleonic) complement each other; Pagès and Fantin provide contemporary campaign narratives.
  • For the Counter-Revolution, Daudet’s three works form the most complete archival survey available in the wiki.
  • For the social history of the Terror, Fleischmann is the only source that systematically examines non-political life under revolutionary government.
  • For institutional and quantitative data, Herbin and Testu provide the numbers and names that the narrative sources lack.
  • For political theory, Constant’s pamphlets are the wiki’s only dedicated liberal-theoretical source.
