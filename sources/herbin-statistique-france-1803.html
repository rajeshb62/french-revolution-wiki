P. E. Herbin de Halle et al., Statistique Générale et Particulière de la France et de ses Colonies, Tome VII. Paris: F. Buisson, An XII (1803). The extracted raw text runs to approximately 26,220 lines and 1.14 MB of OCR from a Princeton University Library copy. This is Volume VII of a collaborative statistical survey of France, with atlas, produced by a team of specialists. Volume VII covers exclusively the French colonial empire and an agricultural appendix, published in the year after the Treaty of Amiens (1802) momentarily restored French possessions abroad and made a comprehensive colonial account both possible and politically timely. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

Contents at a Glance

The Statistique Générale is a planned, systematic survey rather than a narrative. Volume VII brings together contributions from several named authors — Peuchet handled commerce and colonies; Sonnini handled climate, geography, and natural history; Delalauze handled agriculture; Gorsse handled mineral productions; and Herbin himself handled topography, population, rivers, canals, administrative divisions, routes, commerce, and navigation. The atlas that accompanied the full work contained nineteen tables and nine colored maps of France, its internal navigation, and its colonies across the four parts of the world, engraved by Tardieu after Poirson.

The volume treats the French colonial empire as it stood at the Consulate — at what Herbin presents as a moment of recovery. The preface traces the losses of 1713 (Treaty of Utrecht), 1763, and the Revolutionary wars, then notes how the Treaty of Amiens of 1802 restored to France its possessions, restoring her to a position from which she might again rival the other European powers in colonial commerce. The volume then proceeds colony by colony, section by section, with statistical tables of products, trade, population, and administration embedded throughout.

The structure is three major colonial sections followed by an appendix on French agricultural vegetable productions — the appendix placed here because the volume of material forced it out of its original position in Volume I.

Preliminary: Treaty Context and Colonial Government

  • Historical overview of French colonial losses and recoveries — From the Treaty of Utrecht (1713) through the Peace of 1763, through the partial recovery at the 1783 treaty, through the Amiens settlement of 1802: each treaty’s specific effect on the colonial empire is traced. The 1783 treaty restored Senegal, Gorée, Saint-Pierre and Miquelon, and French Indian possessions; Amiens restored everything taken during the Revolutionary wars. This framing positions the statistical survey as a document of imperial restoration rather than of contraction. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Colonial government under the Consulate — The administrative apparatus established by the consular arrêté of 6 Prairial an X (1802), described through the Martinique, Sainte-Lucie, and Tabago model: three magistrates govern each colony — a captain-general (military command, defense, passports, fortification), a colonial prefect (civil administration, agriculture, commerce), and a grand judge (justice). The captain-general holds immediate command of land and sea forces, the national guard, and the gendarmerie; he has exclusive charge of the colony’s interior and exterior defense; he provides provisionally for all military appointments and posts through to the rank of battalion or squadron commander, proposing higher replacements to the minister; he determines and arrests each year, with the colonial prefect, the works to be done for fortifications and roads. The authorities of colonial prefect and grand judge over civil, commercial, and judicial matters are similarly itemized. All communicate with each other through defined protocols and report to the minister of the navy and colonies in Paris. This governmental model is presented before the colony-by-colony survey because it was applied — with local variations — across the entire French American colonial empire as of 1802. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

Section I — French Colonies and Possessions in America

  • Commercial regime of French American colonies — The arrêté of 3 Thermidor an X regulating colonial trade: which goods can be brought in and out, on what terms. Tables give the customs duty tariffs on colonial French products and on foreign colonial products entering France; lists of the colonial produce subject to these duties. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Saint-Domingue — The most extensive single entry in the volume, treating the island that was France’s most valuable colonial possession before the Revolution. The geographic description covers general situation, size, climate, and natural resources. The departmental organization of the French (western) part is detailed through five departments: Département du Sud (covering the southern peninsula, including Jacmel and Les Cayes, sugar and coffee country); Département de l’Ouest (Port-au-Prince and the Cul-de-Sac plain, the richest sugar district of the world); Département du Nord (Le Cap, the colony’s commercial capital and finest harbor, indigo and sugar); Département de Samaná (the northeastern peninsula and bay, important for its harbor); Département de l’Inganne (the interior districts). Each department receives a topographic description, principal towns, port facilities, and note of its principal agricultural products. A table gives the state of exports from the French part in 1788 — the last full pre-revolutionary year — providing the baseline against which the post-revolutionary collapse can be measured: sugar, coffee, cotton, indigo, cacao, and hides in the quantities that had made Saint-Domingue the single most productive colonial territory in the Atlantic world. The Spanish part of the island is then described, covering its interior commerce (cattle, tobacco, hides, mahogany) and noting the fundamentally different character of the Spanish colony (ranching and subsistence agriculture rather than plantation monoculture). The article’s presence in a volume dedicated to the Consulate reflects the ongoing French military effort to reassert sovereignty under Leclerc — an effort that would fail completely within two years of publication. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • La Martinique — Brief statistical description of Martinique: situation, extent, principal towns, agricultural products, commercial value. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • La Guadeloupe — Extended treatment with full geographic description: the island’s two parts (Basse-Terre and Grande-Terre), the channel separating them, the dependent islands. Agricultural products and commerce covered. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Sainte-Lucie — Statistical description of Sainte-Lucie, returned to France by the Treaty of Amiens. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Tabago (Tobago) — Statistical description, similarly restored by Amiens. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Marie-Galante — Brief description of this dependency of Guadeloupe. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • La Désirade — Brief description. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Les Saintes — Brief description of this island group. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Saint-Martin — Brief description of the French part of this shared island. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Saint-Pierre et Miquelon — Description of these fishing islands off Newfoundland, restored in 1783, with their fisheries rights and their dependence on the Newfoundland and northern waters fishing economy. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Cayenne et Guiane Française — Extended geographic description of French Guiana: coastline, rivers, interior territory, indigenous peoples, agricultural potential, European settlements. Commerce and administration covered. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Louisiane (Louisiana) — Description of Louisiana as a French possession, with its geography, the Mississippi basin, the city of New Orleans, its population, and its commerce. This entry was written before the Louisiana Purchase (the sale to the United States occurred in April 1803, the same year as publication), so it reflects the state of French Louisiana in the immediate post-Amiens moment. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

Section II — French Possessions in Africa

  • Slave trade merchandise table (1789) — A table listing the types and values of merchandise used in the slave trade at the African coast in 1789 — the last year before the Revolution disrupted the trade — and a note on the price of a captive of choice in that year. The merchandise listed includes textiles (Indian cottons and European woolens in specified lengths and colors, each type valued for different coastal markets), alcohol (brandy and rum in specific quantities), firearms and powder, iron bars, copper basins, tobacco, and assorted trade goods. The price of a prime captive is given in a unit of account combining the merchandise types. This table is the volume’s most direct statistical statement of the French slave trade before the Revolutionary abolition of 1794 (which the Consulate had effectively reversed for the colonies, though this is not stated explicitly in the volume). ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • French establishments on the western coast of Africa — Survey of each post in geographic order from north to south:

    • Arguin — The island off the Mauritanian coast, France’s oldest African trading post, described for its history and its current subordinate status to Senegal. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]
    • Sénégal — The principal West African establishment, centered on the island of Saint-Louis at the mouth of the Senegal River. Herbin describes the island’s fort, the mixed population of French officers and merchants, African and métis habitants and signares, and the commerce: gum arabic from the interior (the dominant commodity, used in French textile printing), slaves and captives (still the foundation of the trade’s profitability), gold dust, ivory, and hides. The river posts up-river — Podor (trade and communication with the middle Senegal valley) and Galam (the furthest French penetration, at the junction of the Falémé, with access to Bambara gold country) — are described separately with their trade volumes. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]
    • Gorée — The island off the Cape Verde peninsula, described for its strategic position (the finest natural harbor in West Africa south of Morocco), its history of repeated Anglo-French seizure and restitution, its population (French garrison, African grumetes, liberated slaves, and the mixed-race signare merchant class), and its importance as a slave-trade entrepôt and revictualing station. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]
    • Gambia — The French trading presence on the Gambia River, described for its secondary status relative to British competition in the same river system. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]
    • Barbarie (Barbary Coast) — Brief notes on the North African littoral in relation to French commercial and diplomatic interests, including the treaty relations with the Barbary states that protected French shipping from corsairs. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

Section III — French Establishments in the Indies

  • Eastern coast of Africa — Description of French interest and trading presence along the Mozambique Channel coast. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Cape of Good Hope, Mozambique, Madagascar — Brief notes on these way-stations and their relation to French Indian Ocean navigation. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Île de la Réunion (ci-devant Bourbon) — Description of the island: physical geography, climate, agriculture (coffee, spices, sugar), population, and administration under the Consulate’s new colonial government (captain-general, colonial prefect, commissaire de justice). ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Île de France (Mauritius) — Extended description: situation, extent, the capital Port-Louis, harbor, commerce, population (European, African, Indian), agricultural products (sugar, coffee, spices), and importance as the hub of French Indian Ocean operations. The government of the Île de France is described alongside that of Réunion since the two islands were administered together under a shared captain-general and colonial prefect. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Île Rodrigue — Brief description of this smaller Indian Ocean island dependency. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Îles Seychelles et Praslin — Description of the Seychelles archipelago as a French possession. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Diego Garcia — Brief description of this atoll in the Chagos group. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Coromandel Coast establishments — Survey of French trading posts on the eastern Indian coast:

    • Pondichéry — The principal French establishment in India: its geography, fortifications, European town and Indian town (White Town and Black Town), population, commerce, the period of British occupation and the condition of the settlement at restoration. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]
    • Karikal — The secondary Coromandel establishment south of Pondichéry, with its dependent magans (rural districts). ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]
    • Chandernagor — The French factory on the Hooghly River near Calcutta, its special position within Bengal, and its commerce. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Malabar Coast establishments — Survey of French posts on the southwestern Indian coast:

    • Mahé — The French settlement on the Malabar Coast, its commerce, and its condition after British occupation and restoration. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

Appendix — Vegetable Productions of France

This appendix was displaced from its intended location in Volume I by the volume of material. It covers the major categories of French agricultural vegetable production in statistical detail:

  • Grains — Wheat (froment), rye, barley, oats: volumes, regional distribution, and price tables. The centrepiece is a table giving the mean annual price of a setier of wheat (240 livres weight, equivalent to 117.6 kg) at the Rosoy market near Paris from 1643 to 1788 — 145 years of pre-Revolutionary prices, the longest continuous price series in the wiki’s source collection. The series shows the secular price trends, harvest failures (the sharp spikes of 1693–94 and 1709 visible as mortality crises in the historical literature), and the gradual rise through the 18th century to the pre-Revolutionary peak. Supplementing this long series are: mean myriagramme prices of wheat by department for Year IX (1800–01) and Year X (1801–02), allowing comparison across the Republic’s 98 departments; a month-by-month series for Year X showing seasonal price variation; a departmental series for the Aude from Prairial Year VIII through Prairial Year IX; a table of grain exports through Dunkirk; and Halles de Paris price means for Years VIII and X. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Major industrial crops — Flax and hemp (linen textile and naval cordage supply — critical for wartime France, both as domestic textile and as naval rigging material); colza, navettes, and poppies (oil crops for lighting, cooking, and textile finishing); maize and buckwheat (subsistence crops in southern and western departments); madder (garance — the principal red dye, concentrated in Avignon and the Comtat Venaissin, essential for military uniforms); saffron, weld (gaude — yellow dye), and woad (pastel — the traditional blue dye largely supplanted by indigo from the colonies); anise and coriander (pharmaceutical and food flavoring); tobacco (production by department, given that tobacco was a state monopoly with controlled cultivation zones); hops (with coverage of the beer economy in northern and eastern departments where viticulture was impossible). Each crop is treated with production geography, regional specialization, and commercial uses. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

  • Vegetables as garden and fodder plants — Forage and kitchen garden vegetables treated more briefly, closing the agricultural appendix. ^[source: raw/herbin-statistique-france-1803.txt]

Coverage Guide

  • French colonial empire at the Consulate — The Statistique is the most systematic source for the French colonial world as reconstituted after Amiens: what France held, where, under what administrative arrangement, and what commercial value each possession was expected to generate. It records the colonial empire at the precise moment before Louisiana was sold and before Saint-Domingue was definitively lost.

  • Saint-Domingue before and after the Revolution — The 1788 export figures and the departmental description give the pre-Revolutionary baseline; the article’s structure reveals how France still conceived of the colony in administrative terms even as Haitian independence was being fought out.

  • Louisiana — The article on Louisiana as a French possession, written and published in 1803 before the sale, is a document of what France expected to recover and what it then yielded.

  • West African slave trade — The 1789 merchandise table and price of a captive entry are the volume’s most direct statement of the French slave trade before abolition. The descriptions of Gorée, Sénégal, and Gambia provide geographic and commercial context.

  • French Indian Ocean empire — Réunion, Île de France, Rodrigue, Seychelles, Diego Garcia: the full French presence in the Indian Ocean is described in the Consulate period, when these islands served as the support network for French naval and commercial operations in Asian waters.

  • French India — Pondichéry, Karikal, Chandernagor, Mahé: the restored Coromandel and Malabar establishments, their geography, and their condition after the Revolutionary war’s interruptions.

  • French agricultural prices (grain, 1643–1802) — The appendix’s long wheat-price series at Rosoy near Paris runs from 1643 to 1788 and is supplemented by Consulate-era departmental tables. This is one of the few places in the wiki’s source collection with pre-Revolutionary price data over a long run.

  • Colonial administrative structures under the Consulate — The captain-general / colonial prefect / grand judge triad, with its specific competencies, is laid out in the preliminary government section before the colony-by-colony survey begins.

Quality

Princeton University Library OCR from a scanned copy. The text is approximately 1.14 MB of mixed prose and tabular material. Prose sections are readable with the usual OCR artifacts (word splits, garbled proper names). The statistical tables — customs tariffs, commodity prices, export values — have suffered significant column-collapse in the OCR: figures are often detached from their labels or run together. The geographic descriptions are more reliably extracted than the numerical tables. For any precise price or volume figure, verification against the page images is necessary. Place names in Africa and India are spelled according to 1803 French conventions and may require matching against later standardized forms.
