Ernest Daudet, Histoire de l’Émigration pendant la Révolution Française: d’après les précédentes publications de l’auteur et de nouveaux documents inédits. Paris: Ancienne Librairie Germer Baillière et Cie., Félix Alcan, Éditeur, 1905–1908. The extracted raw text runs to approximately 9,284 lines and 3.2 MB, from a library copy bearing the Princeton University Library stamp.

Ernest Daudet (1837–1921) was a French historian, journalist, and novelist, the elder brother of Alphonse Daudet. He specialized in diplomatic and political history of the Revolutionary and Napoleonic periods, publishing extensively on the Bourbon emigration, Franco-Russian relations, and the Counter-Revolution. This work is his synthetic masterpiece: as he explains in the Avertissement, it draws on and recasts his earlier studies — Les Bourbons et la Russie, Les Émigrés et la Seconde Coalition, Coblentz — adding extensive new material from previously unavailable archival sources, particularly the papers of the duc de Blacas (Louis XVIII’s minister and confidant). The work covers the entire history of the emigration from July 1789 through the Restoration of 1814, drawing on French, Russian, Austrian, English, and Prussian archives as well as unpublished correspondence of Louis XVIII, the comte d’Artois, the comte d’Avaray, and Joseph de Maistre. ^[source: raw/histoire-emigration-1908.txt]

Contents at a Glance

The work is organized into 15 Livres covering the emigration from its origins through the Restoration. The author states in the preface that his goal was to produce a “tableau complet de la politique des émigrés” — a complete picture of émigré politics — and that “quel que soit l’effort des historiens qui tenteront après moi de faire revivre les mêmes personnages et de raconter les mêmes événements, ils ne trouveront que de rares épis à glaner dans mon sillon.” This confident claim is supported by the extraordinary range of archival sources on which the work rests. The material is treated as straight narrative history rather than as memoir or contemporary analysis. The text includes 107 numbered footnote annotations, many of which reproduce entire documents — letters from Louis XVIII, the comte d’Avaray, Joseph de Maistre, the duchesse d’Orléans, Mme Moreau, and others. ^[source: raw/histoire-emigration-1908.txt]

Full Contents Map

Livre Premier — Les Premières Étapes (1789–1791)

Covers the beginning of the emigration from the fall of the Bastille. The comte d’Artois flees Paris on the night of July 16–17, 1789, followed by Condé, Bourbon, Enghien, and the court nobility. The émigrés gather at Turin under the protection of the king of Sardinia. The early chapters cover the arrival at Turin, the growing dissensions among the émigré factions, and the political agitation that develops around the princes’ court-in-exile.

  • I. Les Débuts de l’Émigration — The flight of Artois and the first wave of émigrés after July 14, 1789; the court’s reaction; the spread of emigration across Europe.
  • II. L’Arrivée à Turin — The establishment of the émigré court at Turin under the protection of the king of Sardinia; the roles of the comte d’Artois, the prince de Condé, and their entourages; Calonne’s arrival and his influence.
  • III. Les Dissentiments et les Conflits — Internal divisions among the émigrés: conflicts between Artois and Condé, between the nobility and the clergy, between moderates and intransigents.
  • IV. L’Agitation à Turin — Political intrigues at Turin; relations with the king of Sardinia; the question of armed intervention; the role of the court of Vienna.
  • VII. Sur la Route de Coblentz — The shift of the émigré center from Turin to Coblentz on the Rhine, closer to the Austrian and Prussian courts; the departure of Artois and Condé for Germany.

Livre Second — Coblentz (1791)

The émigrés establish their principal center at Coblentz on the Rhine, under the joint protection of the Elector of Trier and the Holy Roman Emperor. Daudet describes the diplomatic efforts of the princes to coordinate a coalition of European powers against revolutionary France.

  • I. La Diplomatie des Princes — The princes’ efforts to build a European coalition; the mission of Fersen to Vienna; Calonne’s negotiations in London; Leopold II’s reluctance; the shifting Austrian position.
  • III. Une Cour d’Exilés — Life at the émigré court in Coblentz: the composition of the court, daily routines, rivalries, and the growing gap between the princes’ illusions and reality.
  • IV. Autour de la Constitution de 1791 — The émigré reaction to the Constitutional Monarchy; their refusal to accept the Constitution; their campaign to discredit Louis XVI’s acceptance of it.
  • V. La France Menacée — The escalating war of words between the émigrés and the revolutionary government; the Legislative Assembly’s decrees against the émigrés.
  • VI. L’Émigration en 1792 — The growth of the emigration as the Revolution radicalizes; the émigré military preparations; the formation of the Army of the Princes.
  • VII. Missions Multipliées — Émigré diplomatic missions to European courts; efforts to win support from Russia, Sweden, Spain, and the German states.

Livre Troisième — La Campagne de 1792

The military campaign of the Duke of Brunswick’s allied army and the émigré forces against revolutionary France. Covers the period from the declaration of war (April 1792) through the French victory at Valmy and the retreat.

  • I. À l’Approche de la Guerre — The allied preparations; the Brunswick Manifesto; émigré hopes and plans.
  • III. Les Derniers Jours de Coblentz — The collapse of Coblentz as an émigré center after Valmy; the dissolution of the Army of the Princes; the dispersion of the émigrés.
  • IV. La Retraite de Brunswick — The allied retreat after Valmy; the émigré despair; the beginning of the long exile.

Livre Quatrième — Hamm et Vérone (1792–1795)

After the collapse of Coblentz, the comte de Provence (the future Louis XVIII) settles first at Hamm in Westphalia and then at Verona on Venetian territory. He proclaims himself Regent after Louis XVI’s execution and then King after the death of Louis XVII.

  • I. La Régence de Monsieur — The comte de Provence proclaims himself Regent of France after Louis XVI’s execution (January 21, 1793); his establishment at Hamm; his diplomatic correspondence.
  • II. Le Comte d’Artois à Saint-Pétersbourg — Artois’s mission to the court of Catherine the Great; Russian policy toward the émigrés.
  • V. Monsieur Devient Roi — The death of Louis XVII in the Temple (June 1795); the comte de Provence proclaims himself King Louis XVIII; the shift in his diplomatic and political position.

Livre Cinquième — Quiberon (1795)

The disastrous British-supported landing of émigré forces at Quiberon Bay in Brittany (June–July 1795). Daudet covers the planning, the landing, the hopes of the Breton Chouans, the military operations, and the catastrophic defeat and execution of the émigré prisoners.

  • I. Préparatifs Défectueux — The planning of the Quiberon expedition: British support under Pitt’s government, the Puisaye leadership, the role of the émigré military.
  • II. Émigrés et Chouans — The landing at Quiberon; the junction with the Chouan forces; the military operations under Hoche’s republican counter-offensive.
  • V. Le Roi Expulsé de Vérone — The French Directory’s diplomatic pressure forces the Venetian Republic to expel Louis XVIII from Verona; his expulsion and its consequences.

Livre Sixième — Blanckenberg (1795–1797)

Louis XVIII establishes his court at Blanckenberg in the Duchy of Brunswick. Daudet covers the king’s efforts to reorganize the royalist movement and the growing internal conflicts within the émigré community.

  • I. Le Comte de Saint-Priest à Saint-Pétersbourg — Saint-Priest’s mission to the Russian court; the shifting policy of Paul I.
  • II. Louis XVIII à l’Armée de Condé — The king’s relations with the Condé army, the last organized émigré military force.
  • IV. Saint-Priest, Premier Ministre — The brief ministry of Saint-Priest in the émigré government; the conflicts between the king’s ministers.

Livre Septième — Les Émigrés et le XVIII Fructidor (1797)

The coup of 18 Fructidor Year V (September 4, 1797) and its devastating effects on the royalist cause in both France and the emigration. The royalist party had made significant gains in the 1797 elections; the Directory’s coup annulled these gains, purged royalist deputies, and reignited the persecution of émigrés.

  • I. Regard en Arrière — Retrospective on the state of the royalist movement between 1795 and 1797.
  • II. Le Parti Royaliste en 1796–1797 — The growth of royalist sentiment in France; the Clichy club; the elections of Year V; the royalist revival.
  • IV. Le Plan des Agents de Paris — The royalist agents’ plans for a peaceful restoration; their negotiations with the Directors.
  • V. La Catastrophe du 31 Janvier 1797 — The abortive royalist coup attempt and the republican response; the prelude to Fructidor.
  • IX. Les Débuts d’une Intrigue — New intrigues within the émigré leadership.
  • X. Le Lendemain de Fructidor — Aftermath of the coup; the proscription of royalists; the hardening of the Directory’s anti-émigré policy.

Livre Huitième — Louis XVIII et Madame Royale (1795–1799)

The negotiations between Louis XVIII and his niece Marie-Thérèse-Charlotte (Madame Royale, daughter of Louis XVI), who was released from the Temple in December 1795 and exchanged for French prisoners in Austria. Daudet covers the protracted negotiations over her marriage, her arrival at the émigré court, and the tensions surrounding her position.

  • I. La Délivrance — The release of Madame Royale from the Temple; the negotiations for her exchange.
  • II. Le Consentement de Madame Royale — Her agreement to join her uncle’s court; the political significance of her arrival.
  • III. L’Abbé Edgeworth à Blanckenberg — The arrival of the abbé Edgeworth de Firmont (Louis XVI’s confessor) at Blanckenberg; his role in the émigré court.
  • V. Dissentiments Passagers — Friction between Madame Royale and the émigré court; her independent position.
  • VI. Le Roi Chassé de Blanckenberg — Prussian diplomatic pressure forces Louis XVIII to leave Blanckenberg.
  • VII. En Route pour Mitau — The king’s journey to Mitau in Courland (now Jelgava, Latvia), under the protection of Tsar Paul I of Russia.

Livre Neuvième — Agitations et Intrigues (1797–1799)

The émigré court at Mitau; the intense political intrigues within the royalist leadership as the Directory’s power weakens and the prospect of a second coalition against France takes shape.

  • I. Le Roi à Mitau — The establishment of the émigré court at Mitau; Paul I’s patronage; the conditions of exile in Courland.
  • III. Fauche-Borel à Londres — The Swiss royalist agent Fauche-Borel’s missions to London; his role in émigré intelligence and diplomacy.
  • IV. Les Lettres Patentes — Louis XVIII’s Letters Patent of 1798–1799; his political program for a restoration.
  • VI. Dumouriez Royaliste — The former revolutionary general Dumouriez, now a royalist agent; his schemes and proposals.
  • VII. La Seconde Coalition — The formation of the Second Coalition (Russia, Austria, Britain, Naples, Ottoman Empire) against France; émigré hopes for a restoration through allied victory.

Livre Dixième — À la Veille et au Lendemain du Dix-Huit Brumaire (1799–1800)

The coup of 18 Brumaire Year VIII (November 9–10, 1799) and the establishment of the Consulate under Bonaparte. Daudet covers the émigré response to Brumaire, the initial confusion, and the first tentative contacts between Louis XVIII and Bonaparte.

  • I. Le Roi et son Neveu — Louis XVIII and the duc d’Angoulême (son of Artois); the question of the royal succession.
  • II. De la Coupe aux Lèvres — The émigré hopes raised by the Second Coalition’s early victories; the disappointment as Bonaparte defeats the coalition.
  • III. Les Préparatifs — Émigré military preparations for a landing in France; coordination with the Chouans.
  • IV. Le Mariage — The marriage of the duc d’Angoulême and Madame Royale; its political significance.
  • V. Nouvel Essai de Recours à Bonaparte — Louis XVIII’s famous letter to Bonaparte (February 1800), proposing a restoration in exchange for Bonaparte’s support; Bonaparte’s cold reply.
  • VII. Les Plans de Dumouriez et de Willot — Dumouriez’s and General Willot’s plans for a restoration through military action.
  • VIII. Le Conseil Royal et sa Mission — The reorganization of the royal council; missions to European courts.
  • IX. Autour de Bonaparte — Émigré efforts to sound out Bonaparte’s intentions; the shifting political situation.
  • X. Les Dessous d’une Négociation — The secret negotiations between the royalists and Bonaparte; their failure.

Livre Onzième — La Cour de Mitau en 1800

Daily life and politics at the Mitau court during the year 1800, as the émigré leadership struggles with internal divisions and the growing power of Bonaparte.

  • I. L’Église et la Royauté — Church-state relations within the émigré community; the question of ecclesiastical authority in exile.
  • II. Les Divisions de l’Épiscopat — Conflicts among the exiled French bishops; their competing claims to authority.
  • III. La Rentrée des Évêques — The gradual return of bishops to France under the Consulate’s tolerance.
  • IV. Les Préliminaires du Concordat — The negotiations leading to the Concordat of 1801; the émigré reaction to Bonaparte’s religious policy.
  • V. Les Princes d’Orléans en Angleterre — The Orléans branch of the royal family in English exile; their separate position.
  • VI. Réconciliés — Attempts at reconciliation between the various émigré factions.
  • VII. Les Dissentiments Entre le Roi et son Frère — The growing rift between Louis XVIII and the comte d’Artois over policy and authority.
  • VIII. Aggravation des Conflits — The escalation of the king-brother conflict.
  • IX. Querelles Apaisées — Temporary resolution of some conflicts.
  • X. Négociations en Vue d’un Mariage — Marriage negotiations for the duc de Berry.
  • XI. Misères de Prince — The material and psychological hardships of the exiled princes.
  • XII. Le Duc de Berry et les Bourbons de Naples — The duc de Berry’s relations with the Neapolitan Bourbons.

Livre Douzième — Louis XVIII et Paul Ier (1799–1801)

The relationship between Louis XVIII and Tsar Paul I of Russia, who had granted the émigré court asylum at Mitau. Daudet covers the warm initial reception, the tsar’s erratic policy shifts, and the deterioration of the relationship as Paul moves toward rapprochement with Bonaparte.

  • I. L’Abbé Edgeworth et le Comte de Caraman à Saint-Pétersbourg — The missions of Edgeworth and Caraman to the Russian court.
  • II. Le Voyage du Général Dumouriez — Dumouriez’s journey to Russia; his efforts to influence Paul I.
  • III. Bonaparte et Paul Ier — The diplomatic rapprochement between Bonaparte’s Consulate and Paul I.
  • IV. Fin du Rôle de Dumouricz — The end of Dumouriez’s political career as a royalist agent.
  • V. L’Expédition de Willot — Willot’s projected expedition to France.
  • VIII. Le Roi Chassé de Mitau — Paul I’s abrupt order expelling Louis XVIII from Mitau (January 1801); the collapse of the Russian patronage.

Livre Treizième — Varsovie (1801–1804)

After his expulsion from Russia, Louis XVIII settles in Warsaw, then under Prussian control. The period covers the Peace of Amiens, the brief respite for émigrés who could return to France, and the hardening of Bonaparte’s regime after the assassination attempt of 1803 and the execution of the duc d’Enghien in 1804.

Livre Quatorzième — Avant et Après l’Entrevue de Calmar (1804–1807)

The new international situation after the formation of the Third Coalition; Louis XVIII’s journey to Sweden and the famous interview at Calmar with King Gustav IV Adolf; the failure of the coalition and the Austerlitz campaign; the Treaty of Tilsit and the Franco-Russian alliance, which destroys émigré hopes for a restoration through Russian power.

Livre Quinzième — Les Dernières Années de l’Émigration (1807–1814)

The final years of the emigration as Bonaparte’s empire reaches its height and then collapses. Louis XVIII’s wanderings through Sweden, Prussia, and finally England; the émigré community in London; the Russian campaign of 1812 and the destruction of the Grande Armée; the formation of the Sixth Coalition; the allied invasion of France; the abdication of Napoleon; the restoration of Louis XVIII. ^[source: raw/histoire-emigration-1908.txt]

Coverage Guide

  • The origins of the emigration, 1789–1791 — Livre Premier gives the most detailed single account of how and why the emigration began, who left first, the choice of Turin, and the role of the king of Sardinia. The emphasis is on the nobility and the princes’ immediate circle rather than on the lesser nobility or clergy who emigrated later. Cross-reference paganel-histoire-napoleon-1815 for the republican perspective on the same events.

  • Coblentz and the Army of the Princes, 1791–1792 — Livre Second provides a comprehensive account of the émigré political and diplomatic establishment on the Rhine. Daudet draws heavily on diplomatic correspondence to reconstruct the princes’ negotiations with Leopold II, Frederick William II, Catherine the Great, and Gustav III of Sweden. The portrait of Calonne as an endlessly optimistic but ultimately deluded advisor is a recurring thread.

  • The military campaigns, 1792–1793 — Livre Troisième covers the Brunswick campaign and the émigré military participation. The focus is less on the military operations themselves than on the émigré political leadership’s strategic illusions and the diplomatic context that produced them.

  • Louis XVIII’s court in exile, 1793–1807 — Livres Quatrième, Sixième, Huitième, Neuvième, Onzième, Douzième, and Treizième provide a continuous narrative of Louis XVIII’s successive residences: Hamm, Verona, Blanckenberg, Mitau, and Warsaw. Daudet’s access to the Blacas papers gives these sections unusual depth — he reproduces extensive correspondence between the king, his ministers, and his agents across Europe. The treatment of the king’s character is generally sympathetic but not uncritical: Daudet presents him as a genuine politician whose natural moderation was warped by exile, by the intransigence of his brother Artois, and by the relentless pressure of circumstances.

  • Quiberon, 1795 — Livre Cinquième covers the Quiberon expedition in detail, from the planning stages through the landing, the military campaign, and the execution of the prisoners. Daudet’s account is notable for its clear-eyed assessment of the planning failures, the political conflicts between Puisaye and the other émigré leaders, and the British government’s ambiguous commitment to the enterprise.

  • 18 Fructidor Year V (1797) and its consequences — Livre Septième covers the royalist revival in France, the elections of Year V, the coup of 18 Fructidor, and its devastating effect on both the émigrés and the internal royalist party. This is one of the most detailed sections of the work, reflecting Daudet’s extensive use of police and diplomatic archives.

  • The émigrés and Bonaparte, 1799–1814 — Livres Dixième, Quatorzième, and Quinzième cover the complex relationship between the émigrés and Bonaparte: from Louis XVIII’s letter offering reconciliation, through the Cold War of the Consulate and Empire periods, to the final restoration in 1814. The treatment of the Concordat negotiations (Livre Onzième, chapters III–IV) from the émigré perspective is particularly useful.

  • Émigré internal politics — Throughout the work, Daudet emphasizes the émigrés’ political divisions: between Artois and Provence/Louis XVIII, between the military faction (Condé) and the diplomatic faction (the king’s ministers), between the intransigents who refused any compromise with revolutionary France and the moderates who sought accommodation. This internal political history is one of the work’s principal contributions.

Quality

The OCR quality is generally good, with clear section headers and well-preserved French text. The text was produced from a printed edition with careful typography — footnotes are numbered and the note markers in the text are preserved. There are approximately 107 numbered notes, some of which run to considerable length as they reproduce entire documents. The OCR has handled diacritical marks and special characters well. Total length approximately 9,284 lines / 3.2 MB.

The work dates from the early 20th century (first published 1905–1908) and reflects the historiographical conventions of its time: narrative-driven, diplomatically focused, with a generally sympathetic but not uncritical view of the Bourbon monarchy. Daudet’s archival research is extraordinary for its breadth; his access to the Blacas papers — the private archive of Louis XVIII’s most trusted minister — gives the work a documentary depth unusual for royalist historiography. The tone is consistently professed as impartial (“Je me suis efforcé d’être indulgent et impartial”), though the work is clearly oriented toward a readership sympathetic to the monarchy. The treatment of the revolutionary government and its leaders is factual rather than polemical.
