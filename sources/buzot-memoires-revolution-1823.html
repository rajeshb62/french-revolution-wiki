François-Nicolas-Léonard Buzot, Mémoires sur la Révolution Française. Paris: Béchet Aîné, 1823. The extracted raw text runs to approximately 16,280 lines and 776 KB, from a Harvard/Widener Library copy.

Buzot (1760–1794) was a Girondin deputy to the National Convention from Eure, one of the closest colleagues of Jean-Marie Roland and the man Madame Roland loved. He was proscribed after the coup of May 31–June 2, 1793, fled through Normandy and Brittany, and hid for nearly a year at Saint-Émilion before being found dead in a field in June 1794.

His memoirs were written in hiding (1793–1794) and published posthumously in 1823, edited by Joseph Guadet, who added a biographical notice, a historical essay on the Girondins, and editorial footnotes. ^[source: raw/buzot-memoires-revolution-1823.txt]

The memoirs are direct and unpolished — written without the leisure to revise, addressed to posterity and to his constituents. Buzot writes in two phases: the 1793 memoirs, analytical, dissecting Montagnard domination of the Convention and its means; and the 1794 memoirs, more personal, written in the most desperate months of his flight — his last wishes, his judgment on what constitution France needed, and his final assessments of Robespierre, Saint-Just, Barrère, and Danton. ^[source: raw/buzot-memoires-revolution-1823.txt]

Mémoires — Première Partie (written 1793)

Chapter I — Disposition of minds; the true causes of Girondin proscription. Buzot opens by examining the political atmosphere in the Convention’s early months. He identifies the “true causes” of the proscription: not the public charges (royalism, federalism, counter-revolution, corruption by foreign gold), but the Girondin insistence on order, legality, and constitutional procedure at a moment when the Mountain’s power depended on maintaining popular agitation. He writes: since the monarchy had been annihilated, the insurrectional movement had to cease; where there is no tyranny to overthrow there should be no force in insurrection; where one is only building, one needs order and reason.

Chapter II — Examination of the charges against the Girondins. Buzot takes each accusation in turn. Were they counter-revolutionaries? He traces the Girondin record from the States-General through the Convention. Were they agents of foreign powers? He challenges accusers to produce evidence, then turns the charge: “the anarchists who accuse others of theft to divert attention from their own — corrupt the French people to conceal their own corruption — render your accounts, render account of your scandalous fortunes, brigands! I wait for you there.” Were they royalists? He notes most Girondins voted for the king’s death, himself included. Were they federalists? He finds this the most absurd charge — the Girondins never proposed dissolving the Republic into independent parts. The word “federalism” had been deployed to stigmatize any resistance to Parisian sectional power. He quotes Charron on the credulity of the people: “In 1791, they were told: Brissot, Pétion, and Buzot are republicans, good to hang; and everywhere the people repeated that they were republicans and would have hanged them willingly. Today they are told: Brissot, Pétion, Buzot and their friends are royalists who must be chased, pursued, put to death; and the people repeat that they are royalists, and chase them, pursue them, kill them. The people in all cases did its job, even in assassinating us.” ^[source: raw/buzot-memoires-revolution-1823.txt]

Chapter III — Means used to change public opinion against the Girondins. Buzot examines how the Mountain managed popular opinion: through the press, the sections, and deliberate misrepresentation of the federalist resistance after May 31 — casting republican resistance to an illegal coup as royalist counter-revolution. ^[source: raw/buzot-memoires-revolution-1823.txt]

Mémoires — Deuxième Partie (written 1793)

Chapter I — Means used to dominate the people. The Jacobin Club as a parallel power structure outside constitutional institutions. Buzot describes how the affiliated clubs in the departments let the Mountain mobilize public opinion against the Convention itself. He also describes the radical women’s sections that provided street pressure at key moments.

Chapter II — Means used to dominate the Convention: portraits of Marat, Robespierre, Danton, and Cambon. The most mined section of Buzot’s memoirs — sustained character studies of the four men he identifies as principal instruments of Montagnard domination.

Marat: Buzot gives Madame Roland’s portrait of Marat (physical repulsiveness and moral monstrousness) before adding his own assessment: a man whose denunciations served as the Mountain’s instrument for destroying individual reputations; whose “acquittal” by the Revolutionary Tribunal (after Louvet’s accusation) made the Convention permanently subject to factional terror.

Robespierre: Buzot traces Robespierre’s rise from the Jacobin Club through the Convention, his tactical patience while Marat was the visible extremist, his move toward dictatorship once Marat’s assassination removed his cover.

Danton: Described through his conduct as Minister of Justice after August 10, his role in the September Massacres, his management of the Committee of Public Safety as an instrument of factional power rather than national defense. Buzot writes: “Danton and Robespierre are the sole authors of the afflictions that have fallen on France.”

Cambon: The Convention’s finance man — “an honest scoundrel who, always speaking of economizing the people’s funds, knew only how to economize his own.” Cambon’s power over the assignats, his management of financial decisions without accountability, his fury at contradiction: “his ignorance was so bold that one did not even dare reproach him with it.” ^[source: raw/buzot-memoires-revolution-1823.txt]

Chapter III — Means used to dominate the ministry: Roland, Garat, Pache. Buzot traces how the Mountain worked to discredit and destroy each minister. Roland is described with the “rigid virtues of the finest ages of the Roman Republic.” Garat he calls a man of letters who flattered each faction in turn as each rose — “with much wit, incapable of all good; without soul, without energy, without character.” Pache is the most detailed portrait: Buzot recounts how he recommended Pache for the War Ministry on Roland’s advice, and then Pache’s systematic betrayal — his false simplicity (arriving at the ministry at seven in the morning with a piece of bread in his pocket, refusing salary and title), and his destruction of the army’s capacity: “all those without morals and without credentials as without capacity were sure to be welcomed” if they affected extreme Jacobinism; armies lacked everything; generals’ campaign plans were undermined. Buzot calls Pache and Garat “names of horror, execrable memory! how many regrets, how many remorse they cause me! It is to you that I partly owe your sudden elevation, and I am well punished for it.” ^[source: raw/buzot-memoires-revolution-1823.txt]

Chapter IV — Conduct of the anarchists toward the army and departments; Conclusion. Montagnard representatives-on-mission used their powers to purge capable officers and convert military appointments into instruments of factional control. The conclusion surveys France’s state at the time of writing: the Republic nominally established, the constitution not yet applied, the arbitrary power of the Paris sections replacing constitutional government. ^[source: raw/buzot-memoires-revolution-1823.txt]

Mémoires — Année 1794

Written when Buzot had been a year in hiding and the situation in France had worsened.

Chapter I — Response to the charge they did not prevent the state’s ills. Buzot answers the implicit accusation that the Girondins should have done more. He gives the honest answer: the cowardice of the departments after May 31 left the fugitive deputies isolated; the federalist resistance in Calvados and the Gironde was real but insufficient; the departments that rose were outmaneuvered, intimidated, and then submitted. France’s current state is the consequence.

Chapter II — What constitution France needed; anecdotes on Robespierre, Barrère, Saint-Just, Danton. Buzot examines the constitutional question from hiding. He distinguishes his actual position (departments should have equal weight; no city should dictate to the whole Republic) from the “federalism” caricature. He examines the Directory constitution of 1795, correctly anticipating its defects before it existed. The anecdotes on surviving Montagnard leaders give his final assessments: Robespierre’s ascendancy, Barrère’s servility, Saint-Just’s fanaticism, Danton’s corruption.

Chapter III — Who can judge us; trial of Brissot and friends; last wishes. His account of the Revolutionary Tribunal’s trial of Brissot and the other proscribed Girondins — conducted by judges who were instruments of the faction that had overthrown them. His last wishes: addressed to posterity, to his constituents. He ends on Rousseau’s line: “One who falls asleep in the bosom of a father is not anxious about the waking.” ^[source: raw/buzot-memoires-revolution-1823.txt]

Morceaux Détachés — Fragments from Buzot’s Manuscripts

Scattered passages from Buzot’s papers, published posthumously:

  • Anecdotes on Valazé, Lacaze, and Brissot — Personal recollections of three Girondin colleagues: Valazé’s suicide at the bar of the Revolutionary Tribunal when his verdict was announced; Lacaze’s conduct in hiding; Brissot’s character and final period.

  • Anecdotes on Dubois-Crancé and Collot-d’Herbois — Two of the most notorious Montagnard representatives-on-mission, both implicated in the massacres at Lyon.

  • New developments on federalism — Additional arguments distinguishing the Girondin position from the federalism charge.

  • Trial of Danton; new judgment on Robespierre, Saint-Just, and Barrère — Written after news of Danton’s trial reached the fugitives, Buzot revises some of his earlier judgments in light of the factional devourings unfolding in Paris. ^[source: raw/buzot-memoires-revolution-1823.txt]

Première and Seconde Lettres de Buzot à ses Commettans (January 11 and 21, 1793)

Two formal letters to his constituents in Eure-et-Loir, written at the height of the Louis XVI trial debates. The first gives his account of the Convention’s political situation; the second covers the question of the king’s judgment — he voted for death with appeal to the primary assemblies for confirmation. His full speech on this question, preserved in the Pièces Justificatives, argues: the Convention has the power to judge; it should pronounce death; but it should also call the departments to confirm, both to exercise national sovereignty and to give the judgment greater legitimacy than a Paris-dominated Convention alone could confer. ^[source: raw/buzot-memoires-revolution-1823.txt]

Précis des Évènements qui ont eu lieu à Évreux après le 31 mai

An account of what happened in Buzot’s home department (Eure) after the coup: the initial resistance to the coerced Convention decrees, the arrival of armed forces from Calvados and Brittany under Puissaye, the brief alignment with the federalist resistance, and the rapid collapse when military defeat at Brécourt made clear the resistance could not be sustained. ^[source: raw/buzot-memoires-revolution-1823.txt]

Notice sur les Derniers Jours des Députés Réfugiés dans le Département de la Gironde

The most harrowing section of the volume: an account of the fugitive deputies’ last months at Saint-Émilion.

The fugitives — Buzot, Pétion, Barbaroux, Salles, and others — made their way to the Gironde after Caen’s resistance collapsed, sheltered by sympathetic republicans in the wine country. At Saint-Émilion they were “buried alive in this dwelling of death”: the man who sheltered them later described exactly which men slept where — Pétion occupied this bed, Barbaroux lay on a mattress there, Buzot sat in the old armchair, together they cooked their meals in that corner.

A first search of Saint-Émilion found no one. A second caught them: Barbaroux shot himself through the head — he survived the wound but was arrested and guillotined. Pétion and Buzot were found dead in a field, their bodies partly consumed by wolves. Whether from self-administered poison, starvation, exposure, or some combination is not established with certainty. ^[source: raw/buzot-memoires-revolution-1823.txt]

Pièces Justificatives

Supporting documents:

  • Buzot’s speech on the trial of Louis XVI — his full statement of position: Louis is guilty, the Convention should judge him, the death penalty is deserved, the appeal to the primary assemblies should confirm. He argues methodically against both acquittal and immediate execution without appeal.

  • His account of his own conduct on May 31 — a minute-by-minute account of the coup: how he learned of the planned arrest lists the night before, how he went at dawn to warn Grangeneuve, how he held his position in the Convention through the morning’s violence, how he escaped.

  • Documents relating to the petition of May 31 and the mechanics of the coup — the addresses to the Convention from the sections, the armed encirclement, the debate inside. ^[source: raw/buzot-memoires-revolution-1823.txt]

Coverage Guide

  • Girondin self-portrait from the inside — Buzot’s memoirs are written from the position of a man present at every key moment, giving the Girondin reading without diplomatic filter. His account of the Convention’s internal politics complements roland-memoires-vol2-1823, louvet-memoires-1823, and brissot-a-ses-commettans-1793 from the same perspective.

  • Portraits of Marat, Robespierre, Danton, Cambon — Chapter II of Part II gives Buzot’s character studies of the four key Montagnard figures. Cross-reference with roland-memoires-vol2-1823 and louvet-memoires-1823.

  • The Roland ministry: Garat and Pache — The detailed portraits in Chapter III, including Buzot’s personal remorse at having recommended both men. Cross-reference roland-memoires-vol2-1823 for Roland’s side.

  • Buzot’s vote on Louis XVI — His two letters to his constituents and full speech in the Pièces Justificatives give the complete Girondin constitutional argument for death with appeal to the people.

  • The federalism charge — Buzot’s most extended refutation of the “federalist” label occurs across Chapters II and III of Part I and Chapter II of the 1794 memoirs.

  • The last days at Saint-Émilion — The Notice sur les Derniers Jours is the primary narrative for the fugitive deputies’ concealment and deaths. Cross-referenced in louvet-memoires-1823.

  • Évreux and the federalist resistance — The Précis des Évènements à Évreux gives the only detailed account of the resistance in Buzot’s home department, its collapse, and submission.
