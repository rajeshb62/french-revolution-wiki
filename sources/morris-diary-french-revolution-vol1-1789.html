Gouverneur Morris, A Diary of the French Revolution, Volume I. Edited by Beatrix Cary Davenport. Boston: Houghton Mifflin / The Riverside Press, 1939. The extracted raw text runs to approximately 27,590 lines and 1.53 MB, from a University of Michigan library copy (Bequest of Arthur Lyon Cross). Morris (1752–1816) was an American statesman and lawyer, drafter of much of the final text of the United States Constitution, who arrived in Paris in February 1789 on a private commercial mission and remained through 1794 — first as a private citizen, then as United States Minister to France. Volume I covers March 1, 1789 through late September 1790, spanning the States-General, the fall of the Bastille, the October Days, and Morris’s informal diplomatic mission to London. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol1-1789-1790.txt]

Contents at a Glance

Morris bought a blank-book bound in apple-green vellum at a shop called À l’Espérance, rue Neuve des Petits Champs, and made his first diary entry on March 1, 1789. He was in Paris on commercial business for Robert Morris (a leading American merchant-financier, no relation): to settle the tobacco contract with the French Farmers-General, to extract payment from the reluctant Farm, and to attempt to raise loans from Parisian and Dutch bankers for the Delaware ironworks at Trenton — all of this amid a political crisis he had not anticipated and could not ignore. The business negotiations run continuously through the diary, interleaved with observations on the Revolution happening around him.

Morris moves in the highest social and political circles. He dines at Versailles, visits the salons of the great ladies of the ancien régime, attends the National Assembly debates, and converses regularly with Jefferson (American Minister), Lafayette (commander of the National Guard), Talleyrand (then Bishop of Autun), Montmorin (Foreign Minister), Necker, and the leading constitutional politicians of both the aristocratic and the reforming parties. He is received at court. He is consulted by Lafayette on constitutional design; he advises against giving the legislature too much power and the executive too little; he predicts the constitution that emerges will fail because it lacks adequate executive authority.

The diary is written in Morris’s characteristic style: terse, precise about times and names, economical with political judgment, ironic about human weakness, and laconic about violence. He records the great events of 1789 — the States-General, the Tennis Court Oath, the fall of the Bastille, the October march to Versailles — not as theatrical spectacles but as data about a political process he is observing with skeptical attention. The editor Beatrix Cary Davenport has woven extensive editorial notes throughout, bracketed in square brackets, providing biographical sketches of individuals mentioned, linking diary entries to the broader historical literature, and occasionally supplying context from letters Morris wrote at the same time.

Format and Edition

The Davenport edition is not a verbatim transcript of the diary but a shaped literary production: the editor selects, brackets editorial commentary, provides thematic section headings in the running text (printed in the original as chapter-like titles such as “First Impressions,” “Sightseeing in the Bastille,” “Augean Stable of Versailles”), and supplies the letters Morris wrote during the same period when the diary is thin or has gaps. The diary entries themselves begin with the day — “Monday 20” — followed by continuous prose; no paragraph breaks between sentences, all records of time, weather, business, and politics in the same breath. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol1-1789-1790.txt]

Prefatory Material

  • Introduction by the editor — Beatrix Cary Davenport provides a biographical introduction covering Morris’s background: born at Morrisania manor in New York, half-brother of Lewis Morris who signed the Declaration of Independence, educated at King’s College (Columbia), admitted to the bar at nineteen, member of the New York Provincial Congress, drafter of the New York Constitution of 1777, contributor to the Federalist arguments around the Constitution. His clubfoot — he lost a leg below the knee in a carriage accident in 1780 — was a permanent feature of his Paris life, and the irony of a physically distinctive American aristocrat moving through the most radical event in modern European history is present throughout. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol1-1789-1790.txt]

  • Preliminary letters — Before the diary proper, Davenport includes Morris’s letters to Franklin (from Paris, February 23, 1789, reporting on French interest in Franklin’s health and reputation), and the exchange between Morris and Washington (November–February 1788–89) in which Washington commissions a watch and provides introductory letters, and Morris promises to execute the commission and requests credentials. The Washington–Morris letters are remarkable: Washington writes with dry humor about the Chinese pigs he is sending as a gift; Morris responds with advice about the likelihood of Washington being compelled to accept the presidency (“I hope the choice will not fall upon me and, in the second, if it should and if I can with any degree of propriety decline, I shall certainly contrive to get rid of the acceptance”). ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol1-1789-1790.txt]

Part I — Fall of the Bastille (1 March to 31 July 1789)

States-General (March–May 1789). Morris’s first diary entries describe a Paris paralyzed between the pressure of events and the inertia of pleasure-seeking: “Paris is if possible a worse place for Business than Virginia. Every Body here has his Country Seat and comes to Town to do Business once in three or four Days and then works not to finish but to get Rid of Work that he may again get out of Town. Indeed Pleasure is the great Business.” He attends sessions at Versailles, delivers introductory letters, begins negotiations with Le Couteulx and the tobacco contractors, and observes the political atmosphere as the States-General opens in May.

He meets Lafayette — whose character becomes a running subject of the diary. Morris’s assessment, delivered across many entries: Lafayette’s ambition is of the vain rather than the proud kind; “he is the Lover of Freedom from Ambition, of which there are two Kinds, the one born of Pride, the other of Vanity and his partakes most of the latter.” He commands the National Guard, has marched the king through the streets, could have kept him prisoner — and yet claims to want to return to private life. Morris finds this both self-deception and attempt to deceive others.

He meets Madame de Flahaut at a dinner at the Comte d’Angivillier’s in Versailles: “She speaks English and is a pleasing Woman. If I might judge from Appearances, not a sworn Enemy to Intrigue.” (The editor’s footnote here provides a full portrait of Adèle de Flahaut — she has rooms in the Louvre, is the mother of Talleyrand’s son, and will become a novelist celebrated by Sainte-Beuve.) Their relationship becomes a central social thread of the diary, her apartment in the Louvre a fixed point in Morris’s Paris life. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol1-1789-1790.txt]

National Assembly (June 1789). Morris records the political events of June with the same compressed notation he gives his business meetings: “This Day the Majority of the Clergy and a Minority of the Noblesse have joined the Tiers. The National Assembly, or whatever else they may now choose to call themselves, have agreed on a Deputation to the King.” He notes Necker’s influence on finance debates, attends to the position of the various parties, and dispatches letters to Robert Morris in Philadelphia while continuing to press Le Couteulx and Le Normand on the tobacco settlement.

He converses with Lafayette and other constitutional politicians about executive power. His consistent advice is that the Assembly is making a structural error by failing to give the executive sufficient authority: “a constitution of a single Assembly without a Senate and without a real executive will fail.” He discusses the constitutional options with Mirabeau and with the Bishop of Autun (Talleyrand), noting that Talleyrand has not the right kind of head for a ministry — too visionary in his ideas — a judgment the Marquis de La Luzerne corroborates. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol1-1789-1790.txt]

Necker’s dismissal and the Bastille (12–14 July 1789). The entries for July 12–14 capture Paris moving from agitation to insurrection. On July 12, Morris is visiting Jefferson when word arrives that Necker has been dismissed by letter delivered by hand — he received it at dinner, proposed a walk to his wife, communicated the news on the road, and departed France that night. Montmorin resigned immediately. Morris records the city breaking open: armorers’ shops forced, armories raided, the Monastery of Saint-Lazare attacked and its grain distributed — each cart accompanied by a friar. He is turned back by guards in the Place Louis Quinze. He goes to his club, where a gentleman just arrived from Versailles reports on the new ministry. He puts a green cockade in his hat — “the fashion of the Day which every Body is obliged to comply with who means to march in Peace” — and walks to the Palais-Royal because no carriages are moving: “I apprehend nothing from the Populace.”

On July 14, he reports the fall of the Bastille indirectly through the mood of the city and what people say at dinner. On July 17, Lafayette takes him to the Hôtel de Ville and gives him a passport to visit the Bastille. Morris describes proposing to a painter that instead of his current subject (Love escaped from his cage) he should paint the storming of the Bastille — specifically, a guardsman who, having seized the gate and unable to pull it down, cried to his companions to pull on his legs, and held while a dozen pulled him like a rope, racking himself, until the gate came down: “To represent him drawn out of Joint with his Head turned round, encouraging them to draw still harder, must I think have a fine Effect.” The Bishop of Autun (Talleyrand), who is present, agrees entirely. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol1-1789-1790.txt]

Lafayette and the new order (July–August 1789). Morris visits Lafayette at the Hôtel de Ville and finds him exhausted by “a Variety of Attentions.” He tells Morris he has had the utmost power his heart could wish, has commanded absolutely a hundred thousand men, has marched his sovereign through the streets as he pleased, prescribed the degree of applause the king should receive, and could have kept him prisoner — “and is grown tired of it.” Morris does not believe the desire for private life is sincere. He suggests to Lafayette a plan for the Gardes Françaises — incorporating them into the City Guard with high pay. He records that the king confirmed Bailly as mayor, received the cockade of the city (red and blue), and only after putting it in his hat received the general shouts of Vive le Roi! — “This Day I think will prove a useful Lesson to him for the Rest of his Life, but he is so weak that unless he be kept out of bad Company it is impossible that he should not act wrongly.” ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol1-1789-1790.txt]

Part II — London and Loyalists (1 August to 10 September 1789)

Morris departs for London on business — the tobacco negotiations require him to work with English bankers and creditors of Robert Morris — and the diary continues from England. He visits the Penn family (John Penn and his circle, English-born proprietors of Pennsylvania who had refused independence and were living on expectations), calls on bankers and merchants with letters of introduction, dines at the French Ambassador’s. He observes the English political scene during the early weeks of the Revolution’s English reception and comments on Necker’s departure from France and the new ministry. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol1-1789-1790.txt]

Part III — Versailles Comes to Paris (11 September 1789 to 16 February 1790)

Morris returns to Paris in mid-September and resumes his daily round — business meetings with Le Couteulx and Le Normand, visits to Madame de Flahaut at the Louvre, attendance at the National Assembly debates, dinners with the diplomatic corps and the reforming nobility. This section covers the October Days and their aftermath.

The October Days (5–6 October 1789). The women’s march to Versailles and the forced return of the royal family to Paris is covered through what Morris hears and observes from Paris, not from Versailles where the events occurred. He notes the meaning of the transfer: the court at Versailles had been a buffer between the king and the Paris mob; Paris and the Tuileries place the king directly in the city’s hands. His entries in the following days record the changed atmosphere.

Conversations with the King (autumn 1789). Through intermediaries — principally the Abbé Bertrand and Madame de Flahaut — Morris is drawn into advising the royal household on the constitutional situation. He is consulted on the form of the king’s address to the Assembly, and he prepares drafts or revisions. He records that the king’s speech will contain “partly mine and partly other Materials.” He gives advice through channels he does not always name explicitly, including through the Comte de La Marck (a Belgian who had stayed in the king’s confidence since Marie-Antoinette’s arrival in France).

Mirabeau. The diary records meetings with Mirabeau — “one of those extraordinary Characters which Nature sometimes produces” — who moves between the constitutional politicians and the court, attempting to broker a settlement that would give the executive real authority while satisfying the Assembly. Morris respects his intelligence while noting his personal corruption and vanity. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol1-1789-1790.txt]

Constitutional advice. Morris’s consistent position through all conversations — with Lafayette, with Montmorin, with the king’s advisers — is that the Constitution being drafted gives the legislature too much and the executive too little. He draws on the American experience: “I preach incessantly Respect for the Constitution under which we live and for that which is forming here.” He argues that a single assembly without a senate and without genuine executive power will produce anarchy — a prediction the diary treats as confirmed by events visible in each week’s entries. He does not trust the enthusiasm for natural rights as a foundation for stable government: “Like the cut of a Coat and the Form of a Constitution. Like the English, they want what they know not.” ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol1-1789-1790.txt]

Part IV — Antwerp and Amsterdam (17 February to 26 March 1790)

Morris travels to Antwerp and Amsterdam to pursue the financial negotiations for Robert Morris’s account — trying to raise loans from Dutch bankers, settling the tobacco accounts with French agents in the Low Countries, and meeting with the banking houses of Van Staphorst and others. The diary continues with the same mixture of financial negotiation and political observation, now applied to the Dutch and Belgian political situation. He notes the impact of the French Revolution on banking confidence across northern Europe. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol1-1789-1790.txt]

Part V — Informal Mission to England (27 March to 24 September 1790)

Washington, through Secretary of State Jefferson, asks Morris to undertake a confidential probe of British intentions regarding the commercial treaty between the two countries and the posts still held by Britain on American territory in violation of the 1783 peace treaty. Morris conducts this inquiry in London — meeting with the Duke of Leeds and other British ministers — while continuing to manage Robert Morris’s commercial affairs. The diary in this section covers the London diplomatic world, English political society, news from France, and the business of the informal mission. Morris’s mission produces no formal result; the British are not ready to negotiate. He reports to Jefferson and returns to France at the end of September 1790. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol1-1789-1790.txt]

Key Figures as Morris Records Them

  • Lafayette — Described across many entries. Morris respects his military record and his commitment to liberty but finds him politically naive, vain rather than proud in his ambition, and unwilling to make the hard choices that constitutional stability required. He admires that Lafayette could have detained the king prisoner and chose not to. He doubts the sincerity of Lafayette’s desire for private life.

  • Talleyrand (Bishop of Autun) — A regular presence at Madame de Flahaut’s Louvre salon. Morris describes him as having ideas too visionary for administrative office, not the right kind of head for a ministry. The diary records their conversations on constitutional design, finance, and the colonial question. Talleyrand in this period is the ex-bishop turned administrator of the Department of Paris.

  • Jefferson — Morris visits Jefferson regularly at the American Legation. Their relationship is cordial but not close politically — Jefferson more sympathetic to the Revolution than Morris. Morris uses Jefferson’s letter-of-introduction network and consults him on French commercial law. Jefferson recommended a watchmaker who turned out to be a rogue; Morris found a better one.

  • Necker — Morris observes Necker’s position as finance minister — respected for his competence, undermined by his own fame and the political dynamics around him. Necker’s dismissal on July 12, 1789 is recorded through what Jefferson tells Morris: the letter delivered by hand, the walk, the secret departure.

  • Mirabeau — Encountered at dinners and in the Assembly. Morris records his physical presence, his eloquence, his corruption, and his attempts to broker a constitutional settlement that would preserve the monarchy’s effective power.

  • Madame de Flahaut — Her apartment in the Louvre is a fixed social coordinate for Morris throughout Volume I. She is described as speaking excellent English, having “chestnut hair curled under its powder” and “charmant face with the wittiest eyes in the world” (the editor’s note quotes Vigée-Le Brun’s portrait). She is Talleyrand’s intellectual companion and the mother of his acknowledged son; she is working on the novel Adèle de Sénanges during the same period the diary covers. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol1-1789-1790.txt]

Coverage Guide

  • The States-General and National Assembly, March–June 1789 — Morris observes the States-General from Paris and Versailles throughout the spring: the political maneuvering, the constitutional debate, the transformation into the National Assembly. His entries give the atmosphere of these weeks from the inside of the reforming and diplomatic circles.

  • The fall of the Bastille, July 1789 — Recorded through Morris’s direct experience: the green cockade in his hat, the city’s tumult, Necker’s dismissal as Morris heard it from Jefferson, his own walking through the streets on July 12–13 (no carriages moving), the post-Bastille political reconfiguration. He visits the Bastille after Lafayette provides a passport.

  • Lafayette’s character and the National Guard — One of the most sustained character studies in the volume. Morris’s assessments of Lafayette’s strengths and weaknesses run through every visit. He is the American observer who knew Lafayette best during this period and was most willing to be candid.

  • Constitutional advice to the court — The diary records Morris’s position as an informal constitutional adviser to the royal household through 1789–90. He drafted or revised texts for the king’s speeches. His consistent argument (single assembly without senate and without executive power will fail) is the organizing political opinion of the volume.

  • Talleyrand in the early Revolution — The diary records Talleyrand as a regular interlocutor before he became famous. The early assessments — “not the right kind of head” for a ministry — and the evolution of his position (from Bishop of Autun to administrator of Paris) are tracked across the entries.

  • Business negotiations in revolutionary Paris — The tobacco contract with the Farmers-General, the loans for the Delaware ironworks, the Dutch banking negotiations, the Paris credit markets — all are detailed through the diary’s business entries. These give a ground-level view of how American commercial interests in France operated while the Revolution was happening.

  • The London diplomatic probe, 1790 — Morris’s informal mission to sound British intentions on the commercial treaty and the frontier posts is documented through his meetings with the Duke of Leeds and other ministers. This is an early instance of American executive diplomatic practice.

Quality

University of Michigan library copy (Bequest of Arthur Lyon Cross), OCR from the 1939 Houghton Mifflin Riverside Press edition. The edition itself is a substantial editorial production: the diary text is clean, readable 1930s print quality, with editorial bracketed notes throughout. OCR is reliable; Morris’s abbreviations (M! for Monsieur, Mad! for Madame, Mess! for Messieurs) are consistent. French passages in the original are preserved and legible. Running headers give the month and a thematic phrase for each spread. The most challenging sections for OCR are the footnotes, which are keyed to superscript numbers in the text — these are generally readable but occasionally fragmented.
