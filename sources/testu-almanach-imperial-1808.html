Testu (publisher), Almanach Impérial, An Bissextil M. DCCC. VIII. Paris: Testu, Imprimeur de Sa Majesté, 1808. The extracted raw text runs to approximately 80,250 lines and 1.75 MB of OCR from a Princeton University Library copy. It is the wiki’s primary reference document for the personnel and institutional structure of the Napoleonic Empire at its height, listing virtually every named office-holder in the French imperial state in 1808. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

Contents at a Glance

The Almanach Impérial is the official annual directory of the French Empire for the leap year 1808, presented to the Emperor and King. It is less a book to be read than a book to be consulted: a systematic enumeration of every branch of the imperial administration, its officers by name, their ranks, addresses, and decorations. Because it was compiled by the Emperor’s own printer and presented formally to the court, it represents the state’s self-description at a moment — mid-Napoleonic reign, after Austerlitz and Tilsit but before the Spanish catastrophe — when the Empire’s organization was at its most confident and most complete.

The volume opens with calendrical and astronomical material for 1808, then moves through foreign courts and ambassadors, through the household of the Emperor and Empress and their families, through every branch of central government (Senate, Council of State, Legislature, High Court), through the military establishment, through the administrative geography of the Empire department by department, through the courts and tribunals, through commerce and finance, and finally through the institutions of Paris itself. The result is a snapshot of the imperial state in personnel terms: who held what office, where they sat, and what decorations they bore.

Calendar Section

  • Opening calendrical apparatus — Explains the astronomical calculations printed for each month: sunrise and sunset times, lunar phases, solar declination, and mean time at true noon. The Almanach distinguishes apparent from true sunrise (accounting for atmospheric refraction) and provides the equation of time for regulationof clocks against a sundial. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Epochs for 1808 — Lists the years elapsed since the first Olympiad of Iphitus, the foundation of Rome (Varronian reckoning), the epoch of Nabonassar, the birth of Christ, and the Hegira, fixing 1808 in multiple chronological systems simultaneously. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Ecclesiastical computus for 1808 — Provides the Golden Number, Epact (III), Solar Cycle, Roman Indiction, and Dominical Letters (C and B for the leap year), the technical data needed to determine moveable feast dates. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Zodiac signs, planetary symbols, and lunar phases — Standard reference table for the signs (Aries through Pisces), the planets then known (including Herschel, i.e. Uranus, discovered 1781), and the four lunar phases with their symbols. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Eclipses of 1808 — Five eclipses for the year: three solar (all invisible at Paris) and two lunar. The lunar eclipse of 3 November is noted as partly visible at Paris; full contact times and magnitude given for each. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Four seasons of 1808 — Precise times for the vernal equinox (20 March), summer solstice (21 June), autumnal equinox (23 September), and winter solstice (21 December). ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Monthly calendar pages (January through December) — Each month receives several pages: a civil and religious calendar of saints’ days and feast days (noting which feasts are transferred to Sundays under the new ecclesiastical regulations), followed by tables giving daily sunrise, sunset, lunar rise, lunar set, and solar declination, and a page aligning the Gregorian calendar with the Reformed (Protestant), Russian Orthodox, Hebrew, and Muslim (Hijri) calendars side by side for each day of the year. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

Chapter I — Foreign Courts and Diplomats

  • Births and alliances of European princes — Lists the ruling families of Europe: birth dates, marriages, and dynastic connections of sovereign and princely houses, including those now subordinated to or allied with Napoleon. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Cardinals — Names and sees of the College of Cardinals, relevant because of the imperial Concordat and Napoleon’s complex relations with Rome. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Foreign ministers and cabinet members of foreign powers — Names and posts of ministers of state and cabinet members of foreign sovereigns. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Ambassadors and ministers of foreign powers at the Emperor’s court — Foreign diplomatic representatives resident in Paris, with their titles and ranks. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • French ambassadors and ministers abroad — French diplomatic representatives at foreign courts, with the posts they hold. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

Chapter II — Holders of the Great Dignities; Ministers and Grand Officers

Lists by name the grands dignitaires of the Empire (Arch-Chancellor, Arch-Treasurer, Connétable, Grand Admiral, etc.) and the ministers and grand officers of the imperial household, with their Legion of Honor grades. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

Chapter III — Imperial Households and Military Household

  • Maison de l’Empereur — All officers of the Emperor’s civil household: grand chamberlain, grand equerry (Caulaincourt), grand master of ceremonies, chamberlains, prefects of the palace, pages, and the intendance-générale of the household. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Maison de l’Impératrice — Officers of Empress Joséphine’s household: ladies of honor, ladies of the palace, chamberlains, and household officials. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Maison de Madame Mère de l’Empereur — Household of Letizia Bonaparte (Madame Mère). ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Maisons des Princesses — Separate household listings for Princess Pauline and Princess Caroline. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Maison Militaire — The Emperor’s military household: the Imperial Guard in its full 1808 organization. The Guard at this date comprised the Old Guard (Grenadiers à pied and Chasseurs à pied, each formed into regiments with named colonels, chef de bataillon, captains, and lieutenants), the Middle Guard (in formation after Eylau and Friedland), cavalry of the Guard (Grenadiers à cheval, Chasseurs à cheval, Mamelukes, Dragoons, Lancers), horse artillery, foot artillery, train, and support services. Each formation is listed with its general or colonel-in-chief, then down through field-grade officers to company-level, with Legion of Honor grades noted. The Polish Chevau-légers (created 1807) appear. The total officer roster of the Guard as of 1808 — at the height of its power before the Spanish and Russian disasters — constitutes the most complete single snapshot of the Guard’s personnel in the wiki’s source collection. General staff of the Guard: Bessières as commander; the aide-de-camp staff of the Emperor (separate from the household proper) under Caulaincourt as Grand Equerry. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

Chapter IV — Legislative and High Judicial Bodies

  • Sénat Conservateur — All senators listed by name, with their addresses. The commission sénatoriale for individual liberty and the commission for freedom of the press are identified by name. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Conseil d’État — Councillors of state (conseillers d’état), masters of requests (maîtres des requêtes), and auditeurs, with their sections (legislation, interior, finance, war, navy). Includes the list of advocates (avocats) admitted to plead before the Council. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Corps Législatif — Members of the Legislative Body listed by department and electoral college. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Haute Cour Impériale — Members of the High Imperial Court. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Cour de Cassation — Judges of the Court of Cassation. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Cour des Comptes — Judges of the Court of Accounts (the supreme audit body). ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

Chapter VII — Organization of Religious Cults

  • Culte Catholique — The Catholic diocesan structure as reorganized by the Concordat: archbishops, bishops, cathedral chapters, diocesan seminaries. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Cultes Protestants — Reformed and Lutheran consistories under the imperial organization of non-Catholic worship. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

Chapter VIII — Military Organization

  • État-Major Général — The general staff of the army: marshals, generals of division, generals of brigade, named and ranked. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Divisions Militaires — The Empire’s military divisions, their commanders, and the commandants of places and fortresses within each division. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Inspecteurs-généraux et Chefs de Corps — Inspectors-general and commanding officers of the various arms (infantry, cavalry, artillery, engineers, etc.) and the veterans’ formations. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Gendarmerie Impériale — The imperial gendarmerie in its full 1808 departmental deployment, organized into fifteen legions. Each legion is commanded by a colonel and broken into escadrons, each escadron responsible for a group of departments, broken further into companies by department, then into brigades by canton. For every officer — from colonel down through chef d’escadron, capitaine, lieutenant, sous-lieutenant, and quartier-maître — the Almanach gives name, rank, and specific posting town. This is the most granular personnel record in the volume for any institution outside the Guard: hundreds of named officers with their precise departmental stations across France, the Belgian departments, the Rhine departments (Bas-Rhin, Haut-Rhin, Rhin-et-Moselle, Sambre-et-Meuse), the Italian departments, and the Dutch departments added by 1808. The gendarmerie’s presence in every corner of the Empire makes this section the closest thing the Almanach contains to a complete map of imperial administrative reach at the sub-prefectural level. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Inspecteurs aux Revues et Commissaires des Guerres — Review inspectors and war commissaries responsible for troop musters and supply certification. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

Chapter XV — Sciences, Letters, and Public Education

  • Institut des Sciences, Lettres et Arts — The Institut de France in its Napoleonic organization: four classes, each with its sections and named members. The Première Classe (Sciences physiques et mathématiques): sections for mathematics, mechanics, astronomy, experimental physics, chemistry, natural history and mineralogy, botany and plant physiology, anatomy and zoology, medicine and surgery, rural economy and veterinary art. The Deuxième Classe (Langue et littérature françaises): the reconstituted Académie Française with its forty members. The Troisième Classe (Histoire et littérature ancienne): the reconstituted Académie des Inscriptions et Belles-Lettres. The Quatrième Classe (Beaux-Arts): sections for painting, sculpture, architecture, engraving, and music. All members are named; foreign associates of each class (the Classe des Sciences alone had eight foreign associates including Volta and Davy) appear separately. The Academy in 1808 included figures such as Laplace, Monge, Berthollet, Cuvier, and Lacépède — the cream of Napoleonic scientific culture deployed as cultural instruments of the Empire. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Écoles d’Application — Specialist schools of public service (École Polytechnique, École des Mines, École des Ponts et Chaussées, military academies). ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

  • Lycées, Écoles Secondaires, Universités, Écoles Spéciales — The secondary and higher education system under imperial organization. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

Chapter XVI — Government of Paris

A complete directory for the capital: the prefect of the Seine and his administration, subprefectures, the prefecture of police and its services, the Paris guard, the judicial organization of the department of the Seine (courts, tribunals), financial administration, commercial tribunals and exchange, religious organization for Paris, public instruction, welfare and health services, and manufacturers, arts, and trades. ^[source: raw/almanach-imperial-1808.txt]

Coverage Guide

  • Imperial personnel and office-holders — The Almanach is the single richest source for named individuals occupying positions in the Empire in 1808: senators, councillors of state, prefects, judges, officers, gendarmes, diplomats, court officers, and household officials. Mine it for any individual whose position or rank in 1808 is relevant.

  • Imperial Guard — The military household section gives the most detailed personnel record of the Guard’s structure in this period: named officers down to sous-lieutenant level across all formations.

  • Gendarmerie — The fifteen gendarmerie legions are listed officer by officer with their department and town of posting. This is the primary source for gendarmerie organization across France and the annexed territories.

  • Departmental geography of the Empire in 1808 — The administrative chapter maps the full extent of the Empire as of that year, including the annexed territories beyond old France.

  • Legion of Honor — The Légion d’honneur chapter gives the organizational structure and its grade notation (used throughout the Almanach to mark decorated individuals).

  • Diplomatic corps — The foreign ambassadors resident in Paris and French representatives abroad give a full picture of the Empire’s diplomatic relations at the 1808 moment.

  • Scientific and educational institutions — The Institut de France chapter names every academician by class and section.

Quality

Princeton University Library OCR from a scanned copy. The raw text is 80,250 lines of heavily tabular and columnar content: the Almanach was typeset in narrow columns with abbreviated entries, and the OCR has degraded many of the column separations, producing run-together or split lines. Names are generally legible; department names and place names are reliable; but numerical data (addresses, distances) is often garbled by the column-formatting collapse. The text is best used to confirm who held an office and at what rank, not to recover exact numerical data. Page-image verification is recommended for any precise citation.
