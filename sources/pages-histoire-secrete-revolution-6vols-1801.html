Overview

This is the second edition of Pagès’ ambitious synthetic history of the French Revolution, expanded and revised by the author just before his death in 1802. François Pagès (1745–1802) was a French man of letters who published several historical, geographical and travel works. The work is titled Histoire secrète — “secret history” — because its aim is to reveal the hidden intrigues, conspiracies and personal machinations behind the public events of the Revolution, incorporating material Pagès claims is drawn from sources across France, Germany and England.

The work runs to 51 numbered books (Livres I–LI) across 6 volumes, covering the Revolution from its deep institutional origins in Merovingian France through the Consulate under Bonaparte and the battle of Marengo (June 1800). Pagès writes in a vivid, dramatic style with a sharp anti-Jacobin, moderate republican perspective: he is contemptuous of the aristocracy (the “party of honnêtes gens”), scathing toward the Jacobins and Robespierre, and broadly favourable to the Directory and Bonaparte as restorers of order. Each Livre is a self-contained narrative chapter covering weeks or months of events, with Pagès’ own personal judgments woven throughout.

Distinctive features:

  • Character portraits — lengthy personality sketches of key figures (Mirabeau, Marat, Robespierre, Bonaparte, the Directors) with sharp moral judgments
  • Documents embedded — includes letters (Marie-Antoinette’s farewell letter, Bonaparte’s proclamations), official reports (Courtois’ Thermidor commission report), treaty texts, and witness accounts
  • “Secret history” angle — emphasizes the Orléans faction, the “Comité autrichien,” hidden conciliatory attempts between courts and revolutionaries, and the role of foreign agents
  • Military narrative — detailed campaign histories (Italian, Egyptian, Swiss, German) with troop movements, logistics, and tactical decisions
  • International scope — covers the effect of the Revolution on England, Prussia, Austria, Russia, the Italian states, Switzerland, the Ottoman Empire, and the United States
  • Analytical summaries — several Livres are synthetic tableaux drawing wider lessons from events

Volume Breakdown

Tome I (An IX/1801) — Livres I–XXIII: Origins to 10 August 1792

This is the longest volume, covering a vast temporal sweep. The first four Livres are a compressed constitutional history of France from the Merovingians through Louis XV, establishing Pagès’ thesis that the estates-general were an ancient institution whose suppression under absolutism created the tensions that exploded in 1789. From Livre V onward the narrative follows the Revolution month by month.

LivreDescription
IMerovingian origins of French government. Traces the estates-general to the earliest Frankish institutions. Pagès opens with a philosophical reflection: since the origin of societies, despotism has weighed on the universe; the history of human revolutions is the story of usurpations of power, the claims of reason, and vengeance. Describes the four castes of the ancient Franks (nobles, freemen, serfs, clergy).
IICarolingian government and estates-general. Charlemagne overshadows Pepin and Charles Martel, acquiring rights to the throne and proving himself worthy by restoring tranquillity. Pagès notes that within seven years seven kings of this house perished, comparing their misfortunes to those of the Stuarts.
IIIThird dynasty (Capetian) through Louis XIV. Hugh Capet received the crown from the peers. Pagès reflects that an intermediate body between king and people is less dangerous, even useful under kings — but Louis XI, Richelieu and Louis XIV proved otherwise.
IVLouis XIV, the Regency, Louis XV. Louis XIV appeared on the throne when civil wars had carried energy to its highest pitch; his reign became an epoch in the annals of the universe. Covers Choiseul, Madame Dubarri, and the Duke d’Aiguillon.
VReign of Louis XVI: ministries of Maurepas, Vergennes, Turgot, Necker, Calonne, Brienne, Lamoignon. Convocation of the notables, exile of the parlement, the royal session (cour plénière), massacres in the rues Mêlée and Grenelle. Pagès notes the exile of the Duke d’Orléans.
VIInfluence of writers on national opinion. The estates-general fixed for January 1789. New Assembly of Notables. The parlements, clergy and nobility demand the forms of 1614. Necker grants the Third Estate double representation. Pagès includes Sieyès’ famous pamphlet and the bourgeoisie assigning a guard of honour to their deputies.
VIIRéveillon riots. Motives and pretexts of this uprising. Bloody scene in the Faubourg Saint-Antoine. Opening of the estates-general at Versailles; their first session. Pronounced division of the three orders. Conferences on the king’s invitation; his arbitration plan and how it was received.
VIIIRoyal session of 23 June 1789. The national hall returned to the people’s representatives. Mirabeau’s “sublime reply” to the grand master of ceremonies (“we are here by the will of the people and will only leave by the force of bayonets”). Homage paid by the people to Necker. The king orders the first two orders to join the Third. Project to dissolve the National Assembly.
IXSiege and storming of the Bastille. Pagès provides the most remarkable particulars of this great event, including the fatal misunderstanding of popular vengeance and various acts of magnanimity. Describes the white flag raised on the towers and the crowd who came to speak with the governor.
XAftermath of the Bastille. De Flesselles convicted of perfidy and killed by the mob. News reaches Versailles; Liancourt convinces the king to throw himself into the arms of the Assembly. Bailly named mayor of Paris, Lafayette commander-general of the National Guard. Within hours over 150,000 citizens were under arms.
XIThe Quincey massacres and the night of 4 August. Horrific events at Quincey (masquerading patriots who turned out to be brigands). The memorable night session of 4 August: destruction of feudalism. Necker proposes a patriotic contribution of one quarter of each citizen’s income. Pagès comments acidly that the oppressive castes “sow for themselves.”
XIICharacter of the French people and political writers. Declaration of the Rights of Man with Pagès’ own reflections. Constitutional projects of Lafayette, Sieyès and Mounier. Debates on the royal veto and whether the Assembly should be divided into two chambers. Pagès notes that when reduced to its principles, the Rights of Man produces: “equal rights to justice and humanity.”
XIIIFamine in Paris and government monopolies. Plan to abduct the king to Metz. Threats about the royal veto. The king’s observations on the decrees of 4 August. Portrait of Mirabeau — Pagès assesses him as at least a good citizen, but his immorality and ambition limited him to playing a secondary role, that of a principal agitator.
XIVArrival of the Flanders Regiment at Versailles. Arrogance of the conspirators. The “orgy” of 1 October 1789 (the famous banquet where royal soldiers trampled the tricolour cockade). Indignation of Parisians. The journées of 5–6 October. Pagès portrays d’Estaing as a courtier by habit and ambition, royalist by prejudice.
XVEnd of the October days. The king fixes his residence in Paris. Pagès offers extended reflections on the conspiracies of these two days and identifies the “two first revolutions within the Revolution” — the first being 14 July, the second 5–6 October. Notes d’Estaing’s studied absence during the crisis.
XVITerror and emigration of deputies. Lally-Tolendal and Mounier flee. Proclamation of the rights of the French nation. Detailed table of the Assembly’s work on the new constitutional system: the entire internal organisation of the empire and all reforms of the new administration.
XVIINew plots and the “Comité autrichien.” Murder of a baker. Conspiracy and heroic death of the Marquis de Favras. Decree establishing equality of punishments for all classes. A noble act by the district of Saint-Honoré toward the family of the two Agasse brothers. Remarkable remark by the Bishop of…
XVIIIFinancial operations of the Assembly. Reflections on Necker. A centenarian from Mont-Jura presented to the Assembly. Rejection of the insidious motion to declare Catholicism the national religion. Mirabeau’s brilliant oratorical movement on this subject. Pagès includes the scene of Voltaire’s ashes being transferred to the Panthéon beside Descartes.
XIXForeign and colonial affairs. The Brabant Revolution (Belgium). Reunion of Corsica and Avignon with France. Colonial troubles. The Nancy mutiny and the heroic devotion of the young Desille. Honours rendered by the Assembly to the memory of Franklin. Mirabeau’s speech.
XXCivil Constitution of the Clergy. General preparations for the Federation of 14 July. Return of the Duke d’Orléans. The Festival of Federation. An eyewitness letter describing the event. The Camp de Jalès (royalist conspiracy in the Midi). European powers solicited to form a coalition against France. A tableau of Europe at this epoch. Retreat of the Duke d’Orléans from the Jacobins.
XXIFlight to Varennes (Livre XXI). Details of Louis XVI’s evasion. The queen’s letter to Mme d’Ossun (“I wish I could see you out of here… Leave this city”). Fersen’s central role. Paris’s calm and majestic response — portraits of the king and queen disappeared from signs, their names effaced. Overnight the word “national” replaced “royal.” Arrest at Varennes. Patriotic emulation of the départements. The Pilnitz Conference. Coalition of the powers. The king’s judgment after his return. The Champ-de-Mars affair. Schism of the Jacobins. Revision of the constitution; the king accepts it.
XXIILegislative Assembly. Opening session. Sharp division into two pronounced parties. The king vetoes decrees against émigrés and priests. Renewal of the ministry. Disfavour of the new ministers. Troubles in Avignon.
XXIIIWar declared and the fall of the monarchy. The Assembly votes war against the foreign powers almost unanimously. Lafayette’s letter to the Assembly. The journée of 20 June 1792. Coalition armies march toward the frontiers. 10 August and 2 September. Suspension of the king; he is transferred with the royal family to the Temple. The Swiss Guard (not more than 900) fought and were overwhelmed. Emigration of Lafayette.

Tome II (An IX/1801) — Livres XXIV–XXXI: Convention, Terror, Thermidor, Directory

The most dramatic volume, covering the Convention, the trial of the king, the Terror, the execution of the principal factions, Thermidor, and the early Directory. Pagès’ anti-Jacobin perspective is most evident here.

LivreDescription
XXIVConvention Nationale. First session. Abolition of royalty; establishment of the Republic. Detailed explanation of the divisions within the Convention and the emerging factions. Portraits of Marat and Robespierre — Pagès draws them as the twin architects of the Terror, each with distinct vices. Departmental guard established.
XXVCounter-revolutionary uprisings. Movement in the Lozère. Continuation of the Vendée war; Pagès analyses its principal causes and duration. Bombardment of Lyon with reflections on this event. Siege of Toulon and its capture. Tableau of all military operations to the north, west and south.
XXVIThe Orléans faction and 31 May. Proofs and interesting details concerning the Orléans faction and the causes of the journée of 31 May (fall of the Girondins). Charlotte Corday assassinates Marat; Pagès portrays her as a heroine, describing her courage and character. Condemnation to death of the proscribed deputies and circumstances of their execution.
XXVIICrimes of the decemvirate (Committee of Public Safety). Decrees of accusation against Camille Desmoulins, Hérault de Séchelles, Danton, Philippeaux, Lacroix, Delaunay, Julien de Toulouse, Chabot and Bazire. Their execution. Trial and death of the Duke d’Orléans. Execution of Marie-Antoinette. Incarceration of 73 deputies (the Girondins). Pagès notes the magic and ever-powerful words “patrie, liberté, salut public, conspiration” used to impose on the multitude.
XXVIIIRobespierre’s conspiracy. Details of the plot. Events of 8–9 Thermidor. Tallien’s “beau mouvement” at the Convention. Arrest and execution of the conspirators, including 71 members of the Paris Commune. Extract from Courtois’ report to the Thermidorian commission. Pagès mentions the infamous Lebon (“ce peintre affreux”) and Carrier (who disavowed the horrors of the drownings at Nantes).
XXIXThe Thermidorian Reaction. New terrorists or “égorgeurs” (throat-cutters). The Companies of Jesus and of the Sun (royalist youth gangs). “Green cravats and black collars.” Troubles over two hymns/songs — the Marseillaise and the Réveil du Peuple. The new Republican Calendar; Pagès proposes his own modifications. A new constitution drafted. Pagès digresses on the state of theatre and literature.
XXXEarly Directory. First works of the Council of Five Hundred and the Council of Ancients. Organisation of the Executive Directory. Finances. Debate on the law of 3 Brumaire regarding deputy eligibility (the case of J. Jacques or Job Aymé). Resolution of the Five Hundred approved by the Ancients.
XXXI (last of Tome II)Babeuf’s Conspiracy. The conspiracy of Babeuf and Drouet. The grenade camp affair (Camp de Grenelle). New troubles in the Midi; Isnard accused by Fréron. Isnard’s sublime reply to Fréron followed by Fréron’s letter proving he equalled the other proconsuls in ferocity. Republican festivals. Opening of the Italian campaign. Pagès notes that Bonaparte, “like Condé,” was self-taught as a general.

Tome III (An X/1802) — Livres XXXII–XXXVIII: Directory, Italian Campaigns, Treaties

This volume shifts focus from domestic French politics to the external wars, especially Bonaparte’s Italian campaigns of Years 4–5 (1796–97). Pagès includes the full text of several peace treaties.

LivreDescription
XXXIIThe 18 Fructidor coup (4 September 1797). Plan of the volume. Annulment of elections in several départements. Condemnation to deportation of two former directors (one of whom escapes), several deputies and writers. Results of the 18 Fructidor journée. Pagès observes that those who had regarded the places with indifference now aspired only to be masters of them.
XXXIIIBonaparte’s Italian campaigns (Years 4–5). Portrait of Bonaparte. Recapitulation of the campaigns of 1793, 1794, 1795, especially the last campaign on the Rhine and its connection to Italy. State of Italy and the respective armies at the opening of the campaign. Pagès includes Bonaparte’s proclamation to the Army of Italy (“Soldiers, you are naked, ill-fed…”).
XXXIVPavia, Mantua, Castiglione. Revolt of Pavia and its capture. Passage of the Mincio and combat at Borghetto. Capture of Verona. Investment of Mantua. Armistice granted to the Pope. Capture of Livorno. Capitulation of the Castle of Milan. Combats of Salo and Lonado; battle of Castiglione. A “trait of boldness” by Bonaparte.
XXXVTreaties and battles of Arcole. Treaty of peace between the French Republic and the King of the Two Sicilies. Treaty with the Duke of Parma. Combats of Saint-Michel and Segonzano. Combat of Caldero. Battle of Arcole. Combat of Saint-George. Battle of Rivoli (or of La Favorite).
XXXVIWar with Rome, the Roman Republic, Switzerland. War with the court of Rome; definitive peace. Assassination of General Duphot. Triumphal entry of the French into Rome. Establishment of the new Roman Republic. Events in Switzerland. Brilliant victories by the French. Establishment of the Helvetic Republic.
XXXVIISummary of victories (Pluviôse Year 3 – Ventôse Year 6). Pagès catalogues all advantages gained by Republican troops from February 1795 to March 1798, including the battles of Quiberon, Millesimo, Lodi, Arcole, Lonado, Rivoli. Throws in a rhetorical invocation — “they will live forever.” The Congress of Rastadt and its negotiations.
XXXVIIIOther peace treaties. Treaties with the King of Prussia. Those preferring to emigrate from Saint-Domingue to Prussian possessions with their property. Treaties with other German states.

Tome IV (An VIII/1799–1800) — Livres XXXIX–XLIII: Egypt, Rastadt, Campaign of Year 7

This volume turns to the Egyptian expedition and the renewed European war of Year 7 (1799). The Egyptian campaign includes extended geographical and ethnographic descriptions.

LivreDescription
XXXIXThe Egyptian expedition. General survey of the Revolution. Bonaparte charged with a maritime expedition whose first objective is a descent on Egypt. Savants and artists of all kinds accompany him (the famous “savants” of the Egyptian Institute). Arrival at Toulon; Bonaparte’s harangue to his army. Embarkation. Capture of Malta.
XLBattle of the Nile (Aboukir). Combat in Aboukir roads between the English and French fleets; its unfortunate outcome for the French navy. Pagès claims Bonaparte had foreseen this event. General description of Egypt: Cairo, its population and grandeur. Manners, customs, monuments. Bonaparte attends the festival of the Prophet Muhammad’s birth. Reflection on Egyptian mores.
XLIRupture of Rastadt and assassination of the envoys. Rupture of the Rastadt negotiations. Causes of the rupture and resumption of hostilities. The assassination of the French plenipotentiaries at Rastadt — one of the most notorious incidents of the Revolutionary Wars. Declarations and other documents related to this assassination. Pagès’ reflections on this “attentat.” Biographical notes on the members of the French legation.
XLIICampaign of Year 7: the opening. Reflections on this new war. State of the respective forces of the belligerent powers. Operations of the Army of Helvetia and the Army of the Danube. Jourdan loses the battle of Stockach. Masséna takes command of both armies. The French seize the Grisons.
XLIIIYear 7 campaign continued. English naval forces. Table of what England spends on the current war. State and distribution of the coalition armies and those of the French Republic. Capture of Lucien-Steig by the Austro-Russian army. Details of this memorable combat. Pagès notes Suvorov’s attempt to force the Adda crossing while Moreau’s retreat was already underway.

Tome V (An VIII/1799–1800) — Livres XLIV–XLVII: Moreau’s Retreat, 18 Brumaire

The shortest volume, covering the critical transition from Directory to Consulate. Pagès provides “new details” on the 18 Fructidor and 18 Brumaire coups not found in other histories.

LivreDescription
XLIVMoreau’s masterful retreat. Savant dispositions and retreat of General Moreau after Suvorov’s passage of the Adda. Capitulation of the commander of the Castle of Milan. Battle in Switzerland on the whole line: Prince Charles wins and seizes Zurich. The famous battle of the Trebia (San Giovanni) lost by Macdonald. Pagès notes that both sides used the forced truce to reorganise.
XLVNew details on 18 Fructidor Year 5 and the events of 28–30 Prairial Year 7 (the fall of the Directory). Grievances against the last directors; their means of defence. Decree rendered against them. Victories of the armies of Batavia and the Danube. “Three armies beaten and scattered, 20,000 prisoners, more than 10,000 dead, nine of their generals killed or taken.”
XLVI18 Brumaire (9 November 1799). Presentation of twenty captured flags to the Directory by Macdonald. The journée of 18 Brumaire. Creation of a Consulate and other provisional measures. Adjournment of the Legislative Body until 1 Ventôse. Modifications proposed to the current constitution. Pagès reflects on the obstacles that had stopped or favoured great captains during several campaigns being finally overcome.
XLVIISummary of the Year 7 and Year 8 campaigns. Battle of Zurich (Second Battle of Zurich, where Masséna defeated the Russo-Austrian army). The Dutch expedition (the Anglo-Russian invasion of Holland, defeated by Brune).

Tome VI (An IX/1801) — Livres XLVIII–LI: Summary, Europe, Egypt, Marengo

The synthetic capstone volume. Pagès steps back from narrative to offer analytical summaries: a tableau of the Revolution’s course, a geopolitical overview of Europe, and a detailed account of the last campaigns through Marengo.

LivreDescription
XLVIIITabular summary of the Revolution. Tableau or résumé of the principal political and military events from the first causes through the present day. History of the Directory government (continued from Tome V): public and private lives of the Directors, their character and that of the principal ministers. Pagès notes the extreme penury of the national treasury — needs so great and pressing that the Directory could not borrow at any interest.
XLIXPolitical overview of Europe at century’s end. A geopolitical survey of the principal states of Europe and particularly France. Analysis of the effects of the Revolution and the new offensive and defensive system it has introduced among all powers; the system France ought to adopt. Assessment of England’s resources: the attentive observer recognises they are the product of an established system of credit and commerce rather than inexhaustible in themselves.
LEgyptian and Syrian campaigns summarised. Résumé of the entire Egyptian and Syrian campaign with new details. Convention for the evacuation of Egypt. Total rout of the Grand Vizier’s army. New details on Bonaparte’s departure from Egypt and his return to France. Formation of a new army. Description of the Alpine snows.
LILast campaign on the Rhine and Danube, including Marengo. Battles of Enghen, Oberhausen, Dillingen, Moëskirch and Biberach. Combats of Memmingen and Erbach. Capture of Augsburg and Munich. General Lecourbe seizes the posts of Lucien-Steig and Feldkirch, the Valtelline, and all the Grisons. Battle of Montebello and the Battle of Marengo (14 June 1800). Pagès includes Bonaparte’s proclamation before Marengo: “The result of all our efforts will be: glory without cloud, solid peace.” Describes Lecchi’s extraordinary feat of transporting boats on chariots to cross the Adda. Surrender of Genoa due to absolute lack of food. Pagès’ concluding reflection: blind or ferocious leaders have all been consumed when their time came — Girondins, Maratists, Robespierrists, Hébertists, Dantonists — and he has outlived them all.

Pagès’ Perspective & Method

Pagès writes in the tradition of the histoire philosophique — narrative driven by moral judgment and the extraction of lessons. His distinctive voice emerges in several ways:

  • Black cockades and “good company”: He repeatedly mocks the aristocracy’s self-description as “honnêtes gens” or “gens comme il faut,” noting that their black cockade was “the symbol of mourning in which they prepared to plunge all France.”
  • The “secret” revealed: For Pagès, the secret of the Revolution lies in the interplay of factions (Orléanist, Jacobin, royalist) whose hidden machinations drove events far more than public debates. The Orléans faction is a particular target.
  • Foreign dimension: He consistently frames the Revolution within European power politics — Pitt’s supposed plans, English financial resources, Austrian and Russian military calculations — reflecting the international scope promised in his title.
  • Anti-Jacobin verdict: The Jacobins are “égorgeurs” (throat-cutters); their Terror is a system of criminal madness. Yet he is no royalist — he blames aristocratic arrogance and the “Comité autrichien” equally. His sympathies lie with the moderate republicans of the Directory and with Bonaparte as the restorer of order.
  • Personal as political: Pagès consistently reduces great events to the characters and motives of individuals — a “secret history” in the classical sense, where the public transcript conceals private ambition.

Known OCR Issues

  • Volume numbers for Tome IV and Tome V read as “AN HUITIEME” — likely an OCR misreading of “AN VIII” (Year VIII = 1799–1800). The Anna’s Archive metadata dates the full set to An IX–X (1800–1802).
  • Roman numeral book numbers occasionally split across lines (e.g., “X XI” for “XXI”, “X L” for “XL”).
  • Page headers and marginalia mixed into the main text stream.
  • Title page ornamentation and printer’s marks garbled by OCR.
  • Livre boundaries sometimes indicated only by bold subheadings rather than explicit “LIVRE” markers (e.g., Livre XXI appears as a section title rather than a book heading).
