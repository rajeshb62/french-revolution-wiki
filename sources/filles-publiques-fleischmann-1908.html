Hector Fleischmann, Les Filles Publiques sous la Terreur: d’après les rapports de la police secrète, des documents nouveaux et des pièces inédites tirées des Archives nationales. Paris: Albert Méricant, Éditeur, 1908. The extracted raw text runs to approximately 10,499 lines and 419 KB, from a Princeton University Library copy (Gustave Bord Collection).

Hector Fleischmann (1882–1914) was a French historian and journalist who specialized in the social and sexual history of the Revolutionary and Napoleonic periods. He published extensively on the “darker side” of the Revolution — women, prostitution, pornography, the mechanisms of the Terror, the psychology of its principal actors. His works under the series title “Les Dessous de la Terreur” (“The Underbelly of the Terror”) were deliberately provocative, aiming to expose the aspects of revolutionary society that conventional historiography had ignored or suppressed. Other works in the series include Les Femmes et la Terreur, Anecdotes secrètes de la Terreur, and planned but unfinished studies including an Apologie de Maximilien de Robespierre and a Réhabilitation de Fouquier-Tinville.

This work examines the world of prostitution and public sexuality in Paris during the Terror (1793–1794). Fleischmann draws extensively on police surveillance reports, archival records from the Archives nationales, contemporary pamphlets and newspapers, and other primary sources to reconstruct the lives of prostitutes and courtesans (“filles publiques”) under the revolutionary regime. The author’s stated methodology — elaborated in the Avant-Propos — distinguishes between two possible approaches: drawing general conclusions from particular events (following the method of Taine and Michelet) or presenting archival documents as particular facts, as scenes from daily life. He deliberately chose the latter, refusing to generalize from the exceptional behavior of prostitutes to conclusions about French society as a whole. The work is dedicated to Xavier Roux. ^[source: raw/filles-publiques-terreur-1908.txt]

Contents at a Glance

The work is organized into three Livres and one Appendice, each examining a different dimension of prostitution during the Terror. The Avant-Propos explains the author’s rationale: while much had been written about the political role of women in the Revolution (Mme Roland, Olympe de Gouges, Charlotte Corday), virtually nothing had been written about the everyday social reality of the thousands of women who lived by prostitution. Fleischmann argues that these women’s lives are as worthy of study as those of the revolutionary heroines, and that their fate — many were guillotined for trivial offenses — reveals something important about the revolutionary society that executed them. ^[source: raw/filles-publiques-terreur-1908.txt]

Avant-Propos (Preface)

Fleischmann explains the rationale for the work: the gap in revolutionary historiography concerning prostitution; the author’s choice of the factual/documentary method over the generalizing/analytical method; the argument that the lives of prostitutes and courtesans are as worthy of study as those of the revolutionary heroines. He draws on an epigraph from Michelet (“la multiplication des filles, l’énervation de l’homme était un vrai fléau”) and engages with arguments from Taine, the Goncourt brothers, Mercier de Compiègne, and Bonaparte’s youthful writings on gallantry. The preface is notable for its claim that “C’est une sœur de Mme Roland, que la prostituée menée à l’échafaud pour avoir crié, étant ivre: Vive le Roi!” — a deliberately provocative framing that equates the fates of the virtuous revolutionary heroine and the degraded public woman. ^[source: raw/filles-publiques-terreur-1908.txt]

Livre Premier — La Vénus des Carrefours

An overview of the world of street prostitution and public women in Paris during the Terror.

  • I. Les citoyennes “férosses”. — The opening chapter establishes the paradoxical situation: the women who had been passionate patriots in 1789 reverted to their true role during the Terror — “amoureuses de l’amour, et uniquement.” The chapter covers the “fierce” (férosses) prostitutes of the streets; the peculiar story of the gendarme of the Revolutionary Tribunal; the Longchamp promenades of 1793; the “great conspiracy of women at the Salpêtrière”; the campaigns for repopulation and free union; and the repression of prostitution under the Old Regime as context for the revolutionary period. Draws on Restif de la Bretonne’s Le Pornographe and other reform proposals.

  • II. Les ci-devant poissardes. — The former fishwives and market women of Paris; their transformation under the Revolution; their political roles; the persistence of their traditional behaviors and language.

  • III. Inconvénients de l’égalité. — The comic and tragic consequences of revolutionary egalitarianism applied to sexual relations; the role of pipe-smoking in outrages against the “beau sexe”; a new method of reprimanding guilty actors; “procureuses, satyres et fruits verts.”

  • IV. Le citoyen “Alphonse.” — Male pimps and procurers under the Terror; the police category of “individus qui paraissent déterminés”; a nocturnal adventure at the Hôtel de Bourgogne; thieves operating around the guillotine; proposals for radical measures against prostitution.

  • V. Court chapitre consacré aux agréments nocturnes. — A brief chapter on the nocturnal pleasures and dangers of Revolutionary Paris. ^[source: raw/filles-publiques-terreur-1908.txt]

Livre II — Le Palais-Égalité ou le Jardin des Plaisirs

An extended study of the Palais-Royal (renamed Palais-Égalité during the Revolution) as the principal center of organized prostitution, gambling, and erotic entertainment in revolutionary Paris.

  • I. Du grand cardinal au prince régicide: Palais, cirque, boutique, club. — The history of the Palais-Royal from Cardinal Richelieu’s original construction through its ownership by the duc d’Orléans (Philippe-Égalité); its transformation into a commercial and entertainment center; the “manifestation du roquet travesti” (a satirical incident involving a dressed-up dog); the atmosphere on July 14, 1790; the warning sign “Attention! il y a des filles et des filous ou avis à nos frères des départements!”

  • II. Le jardin-lupanar: Nymphes, odalisques et dames du monde; quatre femmes pour cinq livres! — The Palais-Royal gardens as a vast open-air brothel; the range of women — from elegant courtesans (“nymphes,” “odalisques”) to common prostitutes; the famous price of “four women for five livres”; from Sodom to Cythera; the Allée des Soupirs (Alley of Sighs); police raids; the political dimension of prostitution.

  • III. Les modes féminines au Palais-Égalité. — Women’s fashion and its relationship to prostitution; the prospectus of the citizen Lisfrand, a fashion merchant; the “redingotte à la Thessalie” as practical attire for hurried gentlemen; earrings “à la guillotine”; the elegances of the Terror.

  • IV. Le jeu sous la Monarchie: Les trente-deux maisons de jeu du Palais-Égalité. — Gambling at the Palais-Royal; the history of gambling under the monarchy; the queen’s gaming table; the ambassador as croupier; the Knights of Saint-Louis; the thirty-two gambling houses of the Palais-Égalité; the police agent Monti, enemy of gambling; “Avez-vous du pouvoir exécutif de pique?” — the revolutionary card game.

  • V. La littérature érotique au Palais-Égalité. — Erotic and pornographic literature available at the Palais-Royal; the role of the police as the “last refuge of public modesty”; the poet Florian; the libelles and pamphlets against Marie-Antoinette; the Revolutionary Tribunal’s treatment of erotic publishers.

  • VI. La “Science de la gueule” suivant Montaigne: Les traiteurs à la mode. — The restaurants and eating-houses of the Palais-Égalité; Méot, Beauvilliers, Very, Venua; the last dinner of a regicide; a terrorist’s menu; the “science of the gullet” as Montaigne defined it. ^[source: raw/filles-publiques-terreur-1908.txt]

Livre III — De la Luxure à la Guillotine

The fatal intersection of sexual license with the machinery of the Terror — how prostitutes and courtesans were caught up in the revolutionary judicial system.

  • I. Une courtisane de la Révolution; le 50 et ses habitués; la suave Émilie; un mari qui aime les actrices; une épouse qui aime les chanteurs; la conspiration de l’étranger; Fouquier-Tinville et les dames de Sainte-Amaranthe; la fournée du 29 prairial; au cimetière Sainte-Marguerite. — The central narrative of the book: the story of Madame de Sainte-Amaranthe and her daughter Émilie, a celebrated courtesan of the Revolution. Fleischmann traces her career from her origins (Jeanne-Françoise-Louise de Saint-Simon d’Arpajon, daughter of a governor of Besançon) through her marriage, her salon, her establishment in the fashionable demimonde, her involvement in the “foreign conspiracy” investigations of 1794, her arrest with her daughter, the trial before the Revolutionary Tribunal presided by Fouquier-Tinville, and her execution with her daughter in the “batch” (fournée) of 29 Prairial Year II (June 17, 1794). The chapter connects the world of prostitution with the world of counter-revolutionary conspiracy as constructed by the revolutionary tribunals.

  • II. Un professeur de débauche: M. le marquis de Sade; le roman de la femme dépecée vivante; l’aventure de la maison close de Marseille; satyrographomanie, érotomanie ou folie?; les prisons du marquis de Sade; sous la Terreur; de Justine à Juliette; la morale de l’auteur d’Aline et Valcour; la fin du divin marquis. — An extended study of the Marquis de Sade (1740–1814) during the revolutionary period. Fleischmann covers Sade’s presence in revolutionary Paris, his involvement in the political clubs (Section des Piques), his imprisonment under the Terror, his narrow escape from the guillotine, and the relationship between his literary works (Justine, Juliette, Aline et Valcour) and his own life. The chapter treats Sade as a case study in the intersection of sexual deviance, literary production, and political violence. ^[source: raw/filles-publiques-terreur-1908.txt]

Appendice — Les Scandales au Palais de Justice sous la Terreur

A supplementary chapter on the sexual scandals that occurred within the Palais de Justice itself during the Terror, involving judicial officials, clerks, prisoners, and prostitutes who operated within the precincts of the revolutionary courts. This appendix draws on police reports and archival documents from the Archives nationales. ^[source: raw/filles-publiques-terreur-1908.txt]

Coverage Guide

  • The geography of prostitution in revolutionary Paris — The work provides a detailed street-level map of where and how prostitution operated in Paris during the Terror: the Palais-Royal gardens, the streets around the Tuileries, the arcades, the public promenades. Fleischmann’s reconstruction draws heavily on police surveillance reports.

  • The Palais-Royal as a microcosm of revolutionary society — Livre II is the most extensive portion of the work and treats the Palais-Royal not merely as a location for vice but as a complete social system: gambling, dining, fashion, literature, and politics all intersected there. The chapters on gambling (IV) and erotic literature (V) are useful for understanding the broader culture of the Palais-Égalité.

  • Prostitution and the revolutionary state — Throughout the work, Fleischmann documents the ambiguous relationship between the revolutionary government and prostitution: the police both regulated and tolerated it; the revolutionary tribunals occasionally prosecuted prostitutes for political offenses while leaving the trade broadly unregulated; the state’s concern with “public morality” coexisted with the practical toleration of vice as an economic necessity.

  • The case of Madame de Sainte-Amaranthe — The most sustained narrative in the book, this case study shows how a woman of the demimonde could be drawn into the machinery of the Terror — accused of involvement in a foreign conspiracy, tried by Fouquier-Tinville, and executed alongside her daughter. The case is valuable for understanding how the Revolutionary Tribunal constructed conspiracy narratives.

  • The Marquis de Sade under the Revolution — Fleischmann’s treatment of Sade during the revolutionary period is one of the earliest sustained analyses of this subject. It covers Sade’s political activities, his imprisonment, and the relationship between his life and his literary production during the 1790s.

  • Methodological approach — The Avant-Propos is a significant statement of historiographical method: Fleischmann’s explicit rejection of the generalizing approach (Taine, Michelet) in favor of a documentary, particularist approach has implications beyond the subject matter. He argues that the sexual behavior of a minority cannot be generalized into conclusions about an entire society.

Fleischmann’s work belongs to the “history from below” tradition of French historiography, though with a deliberate sensationalist edge. The book is both a documentary study and a work of provocation — Fleischmann’s framing of prostitutes as “sisters of Madame Roland” and his planned “Apologie de Maximilien de Robespierre” and “Réhabilitation de Fouquier-Tinville” signal his intention to challenge conventional judgments. The work should be read as a social history with a strong polemical orientation, drawing on genuine archival research but shaped by the author’s provocative agenda.
