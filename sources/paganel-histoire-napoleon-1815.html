Pierre Paganel, Histoire de Napoléon Bonaparte, depuis ses premières campagnes jusqu’à son exil à l’isle de Sainte-Hélène. Liège: C. A. Bassompierre, 1815. The extracted raw text runs to approximately 15,750 lines and 644 KB, drawn from a New York Public Library copy. Paganel was a former member of the legislature and former secretary-general of the Ministry of Foreign Relations. This is a political and moral history of Napoleon’s career from the Egyptian expedition through his second abdication and exile to Saint Helena, written in the weeks immediately after Waterloo by an author who had watched the entire period from inside the French state and who was suppressed by it. ^[source: raw/paganel-napoleon-1815.txt]

Contents at a Glance

The book’s Avant-propos is itself a primary document. Paganel explains that he published a first historical-critical essay on the Revolution of France in 1810. Napoleon read it, was offended by the words souveraineté du peuple and représentation nationale, and ordered the edition seized from the printer’s warehouse by the prefecture of police — without review, after the work had already passed censorship and been printed with ministerial authorization. The seizure, Paganel notes with controlled fury, was a violation of imperial dignity as much as of justice: a great empire did not make itself secure by suppressing books. The preface thus frames everything that follows: this is a judgment delivered by a man who was silenced under the regime and now speaks freely because that regime has fallen.

Paganel writes Napoleon as a historical and moral problem rather than a military epic. He does not deny Bonaparte’s genius; he locates its limits. Bonaparte, in his account, had the scientific knowledge, the cold resolution, and the vast intelligence of a great commander, but lacked a sensitive soul, a calmer head, and the harmony between passion and reason that a great statesman required. A single passion — ambition — consumed all the others. The book traces how this ambition worked through the institutions of the French Republic, wore them down, and replaced them with a military monarchy that by 1815 had exhausted France and was rejected even by the army that had made it.

The structure is chronological across fifteen chapters, moving from the pre-Brumaire moment through the Consulate and Empire to the Hundred Days and Waterloo. The final section of the text is a large chronological table (covering the entire period) and a table of contents, followed by what appear from the OCR to be substantial statistical or documentary tables that may form an appendix to the main narrative.

Avant-propos

Paganel explains the circumstances of the 1810 suppression of his earlier work and his reasons for now writing openly. He reflects on the moral position of a historian under despotism: one can give the tyrant patient silence while his authority stands; the moment it falls, justice and freedom of expression require speaking plainly. This preface also contains his central characterization of Napoleon’s soul: a man whose marriage to an Austrian archduchess intoxicated his pride to delirium, who confused being surrounded by sovereigns with being above them, and whose birth and the regime under which he was born had originally ranked him among the subaltern officers of an artillery regiment. ^[source: raw/paganel-napoleon-1815.txt]

Chapter I — Bonaparte before Brumaire; the Egyptian Expedition

Paganel opens with the central character problem: Bonaparte possessed “a rich fund of positive science,” a vast genius, and an enterprising, cold, resolute character — but lacked a more sensitive soul, a calmer head, and harmony between his passions and his reason. A single passion — ambition — absorbed all the others and gave it long brilliance sustained by brilliant successes, but made him ultimately incapable of the retreat and consolidation that would have preserved his empire. He draws a sharp contrast with Moreau: where Napoleon’s Italian campaigns made him a spectacular offensive commander, Moreau’s Rhine retreat of 1796 showed the art of the strategically wise withdrawal — an art Napoleon never possessed or valued.

The Egyptian expedition is presented as two things simultaneously: a genuine ambition (the Orient as the road to India, the contest with England on its most vulnerable flank) and a flight from the Directory’s failures. Paganel notes that Bonaparte had to choose between watching France drift from Egypt or returning through a British-dominated sea — and chose to return, slipping through the blockade in a gamble that succeeded and that he never admitted was a gamble at all. ^[source: raw/paganel-napoleon-1815.txt]

Chapter II — 18 Brumaire; the Consular Constitution; Bonaparte as First Consul

Paganel’s account of 18 Brumaire insists it was a personal political achievement of extraordinary craft, not a rescue of France. Bonaparte cultivated a collaborator (Sieyès, whose name Paganel renders as “S…”) whom he intended from the start to use and discard — he “attached himself to S… to deceive him; he deceived him in order not to have to fear him.” He saw his future colleagues as “coarse and inert mud, deprived of feeling, ideas, and will.” From the morning of 19 Brumaire, the coalition of experienced revolutionaries that had made the coup broke its prior confederacy and clustered around Bonaparte “without mission, from various motives” — and from their conceptions emerged the consular republic, whose chief had declared himself its first magistrate “in the manner of Pope Sixtus V.”

Paganel’s sharpest observation is about the reception: military glory was the avowed title of Bonaparte’s elevation; “fear and venality were its true causes.” The court of courtiers that immediately formed at the rue de la Victoire and the Luxembourg is described with contempt: “Women — and what women! — remained in possession of credit and favor.” The Constitution of Year VIII receives a strict verdict: nothing was lacking to guarantee the citizen’s liberty except everything that was lacking in the men who would execute it. The principal danger Paganel identifies is structural: “Everything seemed arranged, men and things, for the advantage of a single man.” The Senate’s “anticipated devotion” merely confirmed a servility already inherent in the design. ^[source: raw/paganel-napoleon-1815.txt]

Chapter III — The Consul’s Secret Aims; Corruption; Vendée; Émigrés

The Senate’s self-abasements are traced step by step: doubling the duration of the Consulship before it had barely begun; proclaiming the Life Consulship; finally erecting “dictatorial authority” by a single sénatus-consulte without the authentic concurrence of the people. Paganel asks why the first bodies of the State pressed these accelerations on Bonaparte before he could have demanded them. His answer is damning: “The chiefs of intrigue assured themselves thereby of the first dignities, the favors of the master, and the faculty of crushing the most audacious censor of their guilty felony.” The passage of dissidents onto proscription lists — quiet during the Consulate, not yet deadly — is noted.

The alleged conspiracies against Bonaparte’s person (some doubtful, one certain: the Cadoudal-Pichegru affair) produced a great crisis of public silence: “all mouths were shut, all pens chained, all spirits calm, all hearts indifferent” at precisely the moment when the consul was elevated above the law. The Vendée pacification and émigré reconciliation are presented as the first great diplomatic feats of the Consulship — genuine achievements, but achievements whose purpose was as much to remove potential nuclei of opposition as to heal France. Lyon, Paganel notes, received particular protection from the consul because it had been the great martyr-city of the Revolution; winning it over was symbolically and politically essential. ^[source: raw/paganel-napoleon-1815.txt]

Chapter IV — Interior Administration; Codes; Education; Concordat

This is the chapter where Paganel’s ambivalence toward the Consulate is most concentrated, and where he is most willing to credit genuine achievement. The opening paragraphs are among the most generous he writes about Napoleon: canals, roads over the Mont-Blanc and the Simplon, great public works, engineers who were proud to receive either his criticism or his praise, mountain rivers harnessed for Paris’s water supply. The Legion of Honor — merging military and civil service in a single order of merit — is presented as a genuinely philosophical act: “the great captain seems called upon to repair the ills of war and make the arts of peace triumph.” The education law under Fourcroy, the civil and criminal codes — Paganel grants these their importance. “What a good it was permitted to hope for France and all peoples!”

But the chapter’s second movement traces the corruption of these achievements by the same process — the Senate’s repeated preemptive capitulations to the consul’s presumed wishes, each making the next easier. The famous footnote on the consul’s reported response to General Lannes, who reproached him for trusting only disgraced men: “Imbecile, does one found a monarchy with virtues and men of integrity?” Paganel cannot verify the quotation but places it because it fits so exactly. The Concordat is treated with ambivalence: restoring Catholic worship was necessary; making the clergy agents of the state’s social-control apparatus was something else. ^[source: raw/paganel-napoleon-1815.txt]

Chapter V — Saint-Domingue; the English Threat; Camp de Boulogne

The Saint-Domingue expedition (1801–02) is the first clear exhibition of the pattern that will define the Empire: an ambition that disregards consequences, announced with the “imposing apparatus of dictatorial authority” against a public opinion that clearly disapproved. Paganel reports the widespread accusation — which he presents without fully endorsing — that the expedition’s actual purpose was to purge France of its most energetic republicans by sending them to die in a tropical climate, while at the same time reasserting colonial sovereignty over the richest of France’s Caribbean possessions. “An army had already undergone numerous reforms under the consular government. The purification reached equally the gown and the arms.” Whether or not this reading is correct, the expedition was a disaster: the army melted, the colony was lost, and the financial and military cost were enormous precisely at the moment France needed to prepare for the renewal of war with England.

The Camp de Boulogne — the assembly of a great army on the Channel coast nominally aimed at invading England — is presented as the period’s signature operation: genuinely conceived as a possible invasion (Paganel does not dismiss it as pure theater), but ultimately serving Napoleon’s organisational purposes as much as any strategic end. It produced a superbly organized army that would fight at Austerlitz. The chapter includes Paganel’s broader verdict on the consular government as a whole: brilliant on the surface, corrupted at its foundation, moving always “toward the advantage of a single man.” ^[source: raw/paganel-napoleon-1815.txt]

Chapter VI — Imperial Constitution; Military Despotism; Moreau Trial

The proclamation of the Empire in December 1804 receives a double account: the formal constitutional process (which Paganel finds contemptible in its mechanism) and the broader moral atmosphere that made it possible. He describes the period as one of “singular epidemic” — not just the vulgar who always create supernatural powers, but the educated classes, senators, and men of letters who proclaimed Napoleon’s fortune a certain sign of divine vocation to universal empire: “the most absurd adulation.” He counts on one hand the men who did not descend to this.

The chapter’s specific focus is military despotism as a structural tendency: “every warlike despot is a tyrant; it is a consequence of the regime.” Napoleon dismissed the Imperial Guard’s political significance, but as he concentrated power, his need for his praetorians grew with his distance from public opinion. Special tribunals replaced ordinary law. Paganel deals at length with the Moreau trial: the prosecution of the most distinguished French general after Napoleon himself, whose military reputation was a standing reproach to the claim that only Bonaparte could win France’s wars. Moreau’s conviction on essentially political grounds, and his exile, marks the moment when the Empire moved from institutional despotism to personal vendetta against independent prestige. The chapter closes with the question of how to judge the Senate and Corps Législatif — bodies that had constitutional existence and chose to make it worthless. Paganel’s verdict: not merely cowardly but actively complicit. ^[source: raw/paganel-napoleon-1815.txt]

Chapter VII — Napoleon and Rome; the Coronation; Italy

Two objects occupy Napoleon in this period: destroying or circumventing British maritime power, and clothing his empire in divine sanction. Paganel traces the negotiations with Rome with notable irony: Napoleon “commands rather than negotiates” the Pope’s presence in Paris. Pius VII comes from the capital of the Christian world to the capital of the political world — and the ceremony that follows reveals Napoleon’s characteristic disdain for subordination to any authority higher than himself. The Pope consecrates the crown; Napoleon takes it from the altar and places it on his own head. The footnote is devastating: Charlemagne had sought the crown from Rome as a favor from the church; Bonaparte, by contrast, “not recognizing any intermediary between himself and Providence, commands.” He contrasts with Charlemagne “as one wishes to distinguish oneself from a man whom one recognizes as great.”

The Italian voyage and the assumption of the Lombard crown complete the picture: Italy is not liberated but organized as an extension of French imperial administration. Paganel notes the lost opportunity — it would have been easy for Bonaparte as general, consul, or emperor to permanently fix Italy’s independence — and attributes the failure to the same family-dynasty logic that drove every decision: “reasons of family prevailed over reasons of state.” ^[source: raw/paganel-napoleon-1815.txt]

Chapter VIII — War against Austria; Austerlitz; Confederation of the Rhine

Paganel’s account of Austerlitz (December 2, 1805) is among his most admiring passages: Napoleon reaches his army, harangues it “with the confidence that inspires certainty of victory,” sweeps through Austrian territory, enters Vienna “abandoned by the great men of state, deserted by the government,” and reigns there more through the rights of conquest than by exercising them. The battle of Austerlitz shakes the throne of Emperor Francis; Napoleon reassembles it by signing peace at Pressburg. The generosity shown to Austria — the enemy who had attacked under cover of negotiated peace, who engaged in a coalition “with the secrecy that conspiracies use,” who humbled himself to be a British subsidiary — makes the conquest more brilliant and the magnanimity more conspicuous.

But the political analysis that follows the military narrative is the chapter’s real contribution. Paganel argues that the post-Austerlitz reorganization of Germany into the Confederation of the Rhine was the moment when Napoleon’s structural preference for military government over constitutional life became irreversible: “how many brilliant details” the military historian will find in the campaign; “but then let the philosopher appear on the scene, deplore the fate, claim the rights of humanity.” The Council of State, which had been a genuine deliberative body in the early Consulate, was being emptied of deliberation; every institution was being converted into a transmission mechanism for imperial will. ^[source: raw/paganel-napoleon-1815.txt]

Chapter IX — War against Prussia; Treaty of Tilsit; Poland; Sciences and Arts

Jena-Auerstedt (October 1806) is presented as the most complete and rapid of Napoleon’s victories: Berlin occupied, Potsdam as imperial headquarters, Prussian fortresses falling in sequence. But the chapter’s moral center is Poland. The Grand Duchy of Warsaw is constituted out of Prussian-held Polish territory; Saxony is enlarged; the Elector becomes a King. And Paganel writes what becomes one of the key passages in his indictment: “It was also the moment that Providence seemed to have marked for the re-establishment of Poland… Under the auspices of Napoleon and his victorious army, under the shelter of a tempered monarchy, she would have risen to the level of free and powerful nations.” The opportunity was missed — not because it was impossible but because Napoleon chose family dynasticism over national liberation: “reasons of family prevailed over reasons of state; honor and liberty were nothing less than Napoleon’s thought.” He kept Polish expectations alive and unfulfilled, needing their manpower while refusing to commit to their national cause — a gamble that would cost him the Russian campaign’s political foundation.

Tilsit and the alliance with Alexander I is read as Napoleon at the height of his power but beginning to overreach: treating Alexander as a partner in European domination while actually preparing to subordinate Russia to French commercial interests. The Continental System against Britain begins here in earnest. The suppression of the Tribunate is noted as another constitutional amputation. The paradox of this period — Napoleon genuinely protecting sciences, letters, and arts while dismantling political liberty — is registered, but Paganel refuses to let the cultural patronage redeem the political destruction. ^[source: raw/paganel-napoleon-1815.txt]

Chapter X — Invasion of Spain; the Austrian Marriage; Decline Begins

The Bayonne affair (1808) is the hinge of the book and the moment Paganel describes as Napoleon acting most purely as his own worst instinct: seizing the Spanish royal family under pretext of mediation, detaining them, and placing his brother Joseph on the Spanish throne. Paganel apostrophizes the French army that entered Spain as friends of an allied people and would be fought there as hated tyrants: “sublime example of resignation, obedience, and patriotism!” But the armies’ devotion would not compensate for the strategic catastrophe of igniting a national resistance that bled the Empire for six years and gave Wellington his war.

The Austrian marriage of 1810 is examined with the sustained irony Paganel reserves for Napoleon’s gravest self-delusions. He distinguishes himself from the Austrian court’s “truly calculated humiliation” — in offering an archduchess, Vienna was buying time, reducing its own pride to the minimum required to prevent annihilation, while ensuring that Napoleon’s pride would swell to the maximum that would guarantee his eventual ruin. “His marriage with an archduchess exalted his pride to the point of delirium; and such was the foreseen effect, that he alone, among the sovereigns, did not penetrate the true motive of the abasement to which Austrian foresight had decided the monarch’s pride.” Ministers who understood said nothing; courtiers who understood flattered. ^[source: raw/paganel-napoleon-1815.txt]

Chapter XI — Tyranny Consolidated; the Continental System; Road to Russia

Paganel opens this chapter with a precise diagnosis: “The more Napoleon isolates himself from the nation and concentrates in himself alone the exercise of supreme power, the more he feels the necessity and danger of supplementing the natural limits of power with artificial and tyrannical ones.” The Empire at this point is “a camp, Paris a war-position.” A secret police and an armed police have banished gaiety and pleasure; espionage surrounds the first bodies of the State, “sullies the sanctuary of laws, besieges families, obstructs public places, and daily drags new guests to the dungeons.” Theatre pieces consecrated by two centuries of admiration are subject to imperial revision. Paganel notes that his own 1810 book belongs here — a concrete instance of the suppression he is describing.

The Continental System is analyzed as a strategic conception that was not absurd — cutting Britain off from European markets was a genuine lever — but that was impossible to execute without a continental compliance Napoleon could not maintain. Spain’s resistance had opened the peninsula to British trade and British armies; Russia could not close its Baltic ports without destroying its own economy; every maritime state in Europe had a stake in evading the embargo. The chapter closes with an assessment of Napoleon’s army on the eve of Russia: the Guard superb, the line armies increasingly conscript-heavy, the Emperor’s own physical and intellectual condition showing the first signs of the deterioration that his doctors would later document. ^[source: raw/paganel-napoleon-1815.txt]

Chapter XII — The Russian Campaign; Moscow; the Retreat

Paganel establishes the political framework before the military one: France believed Napoleon was in Poland organizing a constitution and a government for the Poles; he was in fact entering Russia. Alexander’s reply — a frank and motivated declaration of war, an appeal to his own people, and an invitation to Prussia and Germany to unite against their common enemy — is presented as strategically superior to Napoleon’s proclamation, which “filled the cabinet of Saint James with a secret joy without carrying any alarm into that of Saint Petersburg.”

The Polish question is the chapter’s analytical core. Paganel argues that if, instead of drawing Poland into Russia’s deserts, Napoleon had organized its government, fixed its borders, armed it, and made it capable of defending itself against Cossack raids, Russia would have been forced to respect it — and without Russia’s advance, the entire coalition against France in 1813 would not have formed. The opportunity was lost “because the glory of creating a people would have few attractions for the pretended protector, while he found peoples to subjugate, states to conquer.”

The military narrative — victories in Lithuania, advance to Moscow, the burning of the city — is covered more briefly than the political failure. The retreat from Moscow is described not primarily as a meteorological catastrophe but as the consequence of a campaign that had no exit: having sought a battle the Russians refused to give, an occupation the city’s inhabitants burned rather than concede, and a political settlement with Poland that he had no intention of honoring, Napoleon found himself deep in Russia with an army it was impossible to feed and a political situation it was impossible to manage. ^[source: raw/paganel-napoleon-1815.txt]

Chapter XIII — The Last Campaign; the Abdication of 1814

The 1813 Germany campaign opened with successes — Paganel calls them “a deceptive prelude to the reverses fortune was preparing.” But he focuses on a structural argument: a peace was available throughout 1813 on terms that, while not restoring the pre-1812 position, would have preserved France and its emperor. Austria offered itself as mediator between Napoleon its son-in-law and the allied powers. “Napoleon could have concluded a treaty no less advantageous than honorable, if he had wanted it in good faith. But he claimed to dictate peace, not negotiate it.” He wanted to be restored to the political and military situation of before the 1812 winter — something the allied powers were not prepared to concede and France’s exhaustion could not support.

The 1814 campaign in France itself — fought on French soil for the first time since 1792 — produced some of Napoleon’s most brilliant tactical improvisations, and Paganel acknowledges them: a commander of his quality, fighting at home with shorter interior lines, produced results that for weeks made the outcome uncertain. But the fundamental political decision — to reject Austrian mediation, to refuse the Frankfurt peace terms — made the military brilliance irrelevant. The abdication at Fontainebleau is presented as the deserved consequence of a politics “in opposition to the lights of the time, the dispositions of the peoples, and local conditions.” ^[source: raw/paganel-napoleon-1815.txt]

Chapter XIV — Elba; the Return; the Hundred Days Begins

Paganel describes Napoleon on Elba as “a great error of nature that she cannot commit twice; his system of politics, strongly conceived, is outside human proportions and almost ideal.” He had no model; he will have no imitators. The chapter’s central claim is that the entire Elba period was a fiction that both parties — Napoleon and the restored Bourbon government — chose to maintain, Napoleon because he was gathering intelligence and preparing his return, the Bourbons because they lacked the resolution to prevent it. “He had already reknotted the threads of the plot he had previously woven to subjugate us.”

The return is reconstructed almost scene by scene: the landing at Cannes, the deliberate march through mountain roads to avoid the Rhône valley, the confrontation at Grenoble where the battalion sent to arrest him was won over at pistol range, the entry into Lyon as Bourbon authority dissolved, the triumphal approach to Paris as Louis XVIII fled. Paganel’s verdict on the party that rallied to Bonaparte is carefully calibrated: he distinguishes between those moved by genuine military devotion, those moved by hatred of the Bourbons, and those moved by personal advantage — and declines to treat all three as equivalent. The Acte additionnel, whose appearance is anticipated at the chapter’s close, is already characterized: Napoleon’s proclaimed return to constitutional principles disproved by the evidence of his proclamations, which said what was needed and meant what he always meant. ^[source: raw/paganel-napoleon-1815.txt]

Chapter XV — The Hundred Days; Waterloo; the Second Abdication

The Acte additionnel (April 22, 1815) is analyzed with contempt: “a clandestine work, written by valets under the master’s dictation,” in which “despotism veils itself with some modesty, torments the principles, disguises its venom, and pushes to its last terms the doctrine of Machiavelli.” Its invocation of the Champ-de-Mai — ancient Frankish assembly revived as a Napoleonic electoral spectacle — is described as Napoleon “more blinded about our dispositions, when we are more enlightened about his inflexible character.” He hoped to seduce France with antique dénominations as he had decorated his consular dictatorship with Roman names in Year VIII.

The Chamber of Representatives that emerged from the electoral process is treated more favourably: it “resolved to oppose an inflexible resistance to Napoleon’s despotism” and maintained that resolution. Paganel’s reading is that the constitutional opposition in France was, for the first time, organized and principled — which is precisely what made it politically irrelevant once Waterloo removed the question. The battle of Fleurus (Ligny, June 16) and Waterloo (June 18) are covered in outline; the rout of the French army; Napoleon’s return to Paris; his second abdication — this time in favour of his son, Napoleon II, a constitutional fiction that the Chambers declined to accept and the allied armies dissolved by their advance. The chapter ends with the provisional government, the army cantoned beyond the Loire, and the King’s return to Paris. ^[source: raw/paganel-napoleon-1815.txt]

Coverage Guide

  • Napoleon’s character and political psychology — Paganel is one of the most analytically serious near-contemporary assessments. He was close enough to the imperial government to understand its machinery and distant enough (as a suppressed critic) to describe it without flattery. The Avant-propos and Chapter I are the densest passages for this.

  • The Consulate’s institutional history — Chapters II through IV give the clearest account of how the Republic was dismantled step by step through the Senate’s willing capitulations. Useful for understanding how constitutional forms survived as shells while actual power concentrated.

  • The Concordat and religion under Napoleon — Chapters IV and VII touch on Napoleon’s management of the Church: the Concordat as political instrument, the Pope’s visit, and the limits of clerical independence under the Empire.

  • The Saint-Domingue expedition — Chapter V gives a near-contemporary assessment of the expedition’s logic and failure. Cross-reference with the Statistique’s treatment of Saint-Domingue.

  • The Spanish catastrophe — Chapter X is the source’s clearest statement of how Spain became the pivot of Napoleon’s decline.

  • The Continental System — Chapter XI examines the economic warfare against Britain as a strategic concept and as a practical failure.

  • The Russian campaign — Chapters XI and XII. Paganel is particularly useful on the Polish question and on what Napoleon’s political ambiguity toward Poland cost him in terms of committed allied support.

  • Waterloo and the Hundred Days — Chapters XIV and XV. Written immediately after the events, by a French political insider, not a military specialist.

  • Press censorship and intellectual suppression under the Empire — The Avant-propos is the primary source here: a first-hand account of what it meant to have a book seized on imperial order.

Quality

NYPL copy OCR. The text is approximately 644 KB of readable prose, much cleaner than the Almanach because it is narrative rather than tabular. Proper names of people are generally reliable. Place names are consistent. The OCR degrades at page turns and in passages with footnotes (the book has many footnotes, which are picked up mid-sentence by the OCR and interrupt the main text). The chronological table section is heavily garbled because the OCR cannot reliably handle the columnar date-and-event format. Quotations and citations from classical authors (Tacitus, Montaigne) appear in the original Latin or French and are legible. The appendix tables require page-image verification for any precise use.
