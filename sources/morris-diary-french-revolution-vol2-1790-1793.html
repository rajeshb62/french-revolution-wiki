Gouverneur Morris, A Diary of the French Revolution, Volume II. Edited by Beatrix Cary Davenport. Boston: Houghton Mifflin / The Riverside Press, 1939. Copyright 1939 by Beatrix Cary Davenport. The extracted raw text runs to approximately 30,600 lines and 1.53 MB, from a University of Michigan library copy. Volume II covers November 7, 1790 through January 5, 1793, spanning the French Constitution of 1791, the Legislative Assembly, Morris’s appointment as United States Minister to France in May 1792, the storming of the Tuileries on August 10, 1792, and the opening of the First Republic. The diary proper is suppressed after August 10 — when Morris’s house was being used to shelter royalist refugees — and the remainder of the volume is composed of letters and dispatches, with editorial bridging. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol2-1790-1793.txt]

Contents at a Glance

Volume II opens where Volume I left off — Morris returned from his London diplomatic mission in late 1790, still engaged in his business negotiations for Robert Morris while living at the center of Parisian political society. The Revolution has advanced: Mirabeau is dying, the Constituent Assembly is completing its constitutional work, the émigrés are accumulating on the borders, and the king’s position is deteriorating. Morris watches with the same skeptical precision, still predicting — correctly — that the Constitution of 1791 gives the legislature too much and the executive too little to be stable.

The diary through 1791 alternates between the social and the political: dinners with Montmorin and Madame de Flahaut, visits to the Assembly debates, conversations with Lafayette and Talleyrand, his own persistent commercial negotiations, observations on the assignat inflation and the financial chaos. The flight to Varennes (June 1791) is covered through Morris’s letters to Robert Morris — a long retrospective letter written several days after the event, describing the king’s capture at Varennes and the political consequences.

In May 1792 Washington and Jefferson appoint Morris as United States Minister to France — the appointment was controversial because Morris’s sympathy with the monarchy was well-known and the French republicans had lobbied against him. He accepts and presents his credentials. Within weeks the political situation passes the point of no return: the invasion of the Tuileries on June 20, 1792; Morris’s continued contacts with the court; the storming of the Tuileries on August 10; the September Massacres; the abolition of the monarchy; and the opening of the National Convention.

After August 10, Morris’s house on the rue de la Planche shelters royalist refugees — the former minister Monciel, others implicated in royalist counter-conspiracy — and Morris himself is holding funds (5,000 louis d’or) that had been entrusted to him for a royalist defense operation that never took place. The diary is suppressed. What remains is dispatches to Jefferson and later to Thomas Pinckney, and a remarkable accounting he later presents to Madame Royale (Marie-Thérèse) covering what became of the royal funds. The volume closes at the end of 1792 / January 1793, as the trial of Louis XVI is reaching its conclusion. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol2-1790-1793.txt]

Parts VI–VII — Whales, Nicotine, and the Constitutional Endgame (November 1790 – February 1791)

Morris is back in Paris after his London mission. The diary resumes with his regular social round and business negotiations. Two commercial matters run through this section: the whale oil trade (a major American export to France, the terms of which the Constituent Assembly’s committees are debating) and the tobacco contract with the Farm. He meets with various French ministers and Assembly committee members on these subjects while continuing his social visits.

He observes the declining political vitality of the Constituent Assembly in its final months. Mirabeau is ill and increasingly absent. The constitutional structure being finalized is, in Morris’s view, defective: it will produce legislative tyranny over a passive executive. He records conversations with Montmorin and with the Comte de La Marck (a Belgian who serves as the king’s confidential intermediary) in which they discuss what can be done — Morris’s advice consistently being that the king should hold to the constitution, however flawed, rather than attempt to subvert it, because any attempted escape or coup would fail and worsen his position. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol2-1790-1793.txt]

Part VIII — Whales and Nicotine (1 February to 28 February 1791)

This section covers a concentrated period of commercial and diplomatic lobbying — Morris working the Assembly committees on the whale oil and tobacco questions simultaneously. The commercial entries are unusually detailed: the specific tariff rates under discussion, the arguments made by American competitors (the New England whale-oil merchants), the Assembly members most accessible on these questions, the text of memorials Morris drafts or assists. He also notes the political atmosphere: assignat depreciation accelerating, emigration increasing, the king’s aunts attempting to leave France (they eventually succeed, after some public drama). ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol2-1790-1793.txt]

Part IX — The French Constitution of 1791 (1 March to 30 September 1791)

The longest single section of Volume II, covering the Constituent Assembly’s final months and the presentation of the completed constitution to the king. The diary’s density increases: Morris is attending more political gatherings, seeing more of Montmorin, Lafayette, and the court intermediaries.

Mirabeau’s death (April 1791). Morris records the impact of Mirabeau’s death on the political situation. The one figure who might have found a constitutional path between royal authority and popular sovereignty is gone. The diary notes the public grief, the political void, and the absence of any successor with comparable ability. The editor’s footnote gives Mirabeau’s last exchange with the king, which Morris had heard from La Marck.

The Constitution of 1791 and Salon of 1791. Morris attends the opening of the Salon — the first opened to all artists regardless of Academy membership, organized by Talleyrand as administrator of the Paris department, with David exulting at the expanded access — while simultaneously observing the Assembly debate the final constitution. He visits Choisy to dine with Marmontel, who contests the droits de l’homme by asking for a definition of the word droit and concluding from it against asserted equality. Morris records these philosophical conversations alongside his business appointments.

The flight to Varennes (June 1791). Morris was at his country property outside Paris when the king attempted to escape. His accounts are given in letters written several days after the fact, reconstructing events from what he has gathered. His analysis: the flight was a political blunder. Public affairs were such that if the king had been quiet he would have soon regained authority as anarchy demonstrated the necessity of stronger executive power; his departure “changed every Thing and now the general wish seems to be for a Republic, which is quite in the natural Order of Things.” He notes the contradiction: the Assembly declared the king inviolable and therefore could not include him in prosecutions relating to his attempted escape — producing intense heat against the Assembly. Lafayette was nearly killed: “a Pistol snapped at his Breast. The Assassin was immediately secured but he ordered him to be discharged.”

The Champ de Mars massacre (July 17, 1791). Morris describes coming from the British Ambassador’s in the evening, meeting a detachment of militia with the red flag flying. He went to a height to see the battle but it was over before he arrived — the militia, being pelted with bricks instead of obeying the order to ground arms, fired and killed “a dozen or two of the ragged Regiment.” He gives a figure of perhaps five or six militia subsequently assassinated in retaliation, and notes that Lafayette was again nearly killed.

The constitution accepted by the king (September 1791). Morris records the king’s formal acceptance of the constitution and the political atmosphere around it. His private assessment is unchanged: the constitution lacks adequate executive authority and will not survive. The Constituent Assembly dissolves; the Legislative Assembly convenes October 1, 1791. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol2-1790-1793.txt]

Part X — Legislative Assembly (1 October 1791 to 29 January 1792)

The new Legislative Assembly — composed entirely of men who had not served in the Constituent (the self-denying ordinance Robespierre had proposed) — meets in October 1791. Morris’s diary records the changed political atmosphere: the Girondins gaining influence, the war party growing, Montmorin increasingly uncomfortable in the foreign ministry, the assignat continuing to fall. He observes that the Assembly lacks the experienced constitutional politicians of the Constituent and is more susceptible to radical pressure.

Commercial negotiations continue. Morris is working on the whale oil question, the tobacco accounts, Robert Morris’s debt to French creditors, and various real estate transactions for American speculators. He records the complexity of conducting American commercial business in a country where the political situation changes the legal framework of commercial contracts from month to month. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol2-1790-1793.txt]

Part XI — Spring in London (30 January to 5 May 1792)

Morris returns to London for a further period on business — the debt accounts with London bankers, more real estate negotiations, meetings with British ministers. The diary continues from London, recording his observations on British reactions to the Revolution, the progress of the war crisis, and his own commercial work. He notes the appointment discussions that will make him United States Minister to France: the Washington administration has settled on him despite French republican protests. He accepts. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol2-1790-1793.txt]

Part XII — United States Minister to France: Transition from Monarchy to Republic (6 May 1792 to 5 January 1793)

Appointment and credentials (May 1792). Morris returns to Paris, presents his credentials as United States Minister, and formally enters the diplomatic corps. His position is awkward: the French republican faction knows he is sympathetic to the monarchy; he is expected to maintain American commercial interests while the political situation is collapsing.

The June 20 invasion of the Tuileries (1792). Morris records the mob invasion of the Tuileries on June 20, 1792, when armed sections forced their way into the palace and the king was compelled to put on a red cap and drink to the nation from a bottle handed to him by a rioter. Morris does not witness it directly but gathers accounts and records his assessment: the king’s behavior was remarkably composed, and the day demonstrated both the degradation of royal authority and the surprising durability of the king’s personal dignity under pressure.

The royalist counter-conspiracy and the 5,000 louis. This section of the diary contains one of its most sensitive and historically significant elements. Morris was drawn into a royalist counter-conspiracy organized by Monciel (a former interior minister) and Brémond (a Provençal agent) to organize armed resistance from inside Paris in the event of an insurrection. Morris held 5,000 louis d’or (convertible gold coin) earmarked for paying reliable men who would rally to the king’s defense at designated points. The money was paid out of royal funds through Brémond; the purses of twenty louis were divided and held at Morris’s house pending the activation of the network. The network was never activated: the storming of the Tuileries on August 10 overtook the plan entirely. The 5,000 louis, which Brémond had by then removed to Swiss bankers, became a prolonged and frustrated effort at recovery that Morris was still pursuing years later. The accounting of what became of this money — written out for the Princess Royale (Marie-Thérèse) when she was finally released from the Temple — is included in the volume and gives the clearest narrative of Morris’s involvement with the royal household’s financial arrangements in 1792.

August 10, 1792 — the storming of the Tuileries. The diary proper is suppressed after August 10. The editor’s note explains: after the Tuileries fell to the insurrectionary sections, former minister Monciel and his family took refuge in Morris’s house on the rue de la Planche, along with others implicated in royalist activities. The diary could not be maintained under these circumstances without endangering the people sheltered there. “The events of August tenth are too well known to admit of repeating their painful story.” What remains is reconstructed from letters and from Morris’s later accounting to Madame Royale.

September Massacres (September 1792). Morris was still in Paris during the September Massacres, sheltering refugees and managing the aftermath of August 10. His dispatches to Jefferson describe the political situation — the abolition of the monarchy, the proclamation of the Republic, the formation of the National Convention. He does not describe the massacres themselves in graphic detail in what survives; his position as US Minister required him to maintain a diplomatic register that restrained his personal assessments.

Louvet’s Robespierride (October–November 1792). Morris’s diary notes, in the compressed style of its final pages: “Robespierre has got thro his Affair with Éclat.” The editor’s note explains: on October 29, Louvet had electrified the Convention by formally accusing Robespierre before the assembly — each charge beginning Je t’accuse! Robespierre’s retreat and comeback are tracked through Morris’s subsequent brief entries. “Nothing in his speeches explains” Robespierre’s growing ascendancy, the editor comments; “unless their lack of eloquence gave an impression of great sincerity to audiences overfed on revolutionary bombast.”

Dumouriez’s campaign (autumn–winter 1792). Morris records the military news from Belgium: Dumouriez’s victory at Jemappes (November 6), the French entry into Brussels (November 14), the opening of the Scheldt. He notes in his dispatches the British reaction to the Scheldt decree and the trajectory toward war between France and Britain. The volume closes in early January 1793 as the trial of Louis XVI is approaching its conclusion. ^[source: raw/morris-diary-french-revolution-vol2-1790-1793.txt]

Key Figures and Running Subjects

  • Montmorin — The Foreign Minister appears in Volume II as the court’s most accessible and most troubled member. Morris dines with him regularly through 1791, discussing the fiscal crisis, the constitutional situation, and American commercial claims. Montmorin’s position is increasingly untenable; he resigns and will die in the September Massacres.

  • Madame de Staël — Enters the diary in this volume as a social presence at the British Ambassador’s and at Talleyrand’s salon. Morris records her views on the constitution and on Talleyrand’s career prospects. She and Talleyrand press Montmorin hard on some political subject at a dinner Morris attends; he learns afterward it was a request to revoke the decree barring Talleyrand from the ministry.

  • The Comte de La Marck — The Belgian prince who served as the court’s confidential intermediary with Mirabeau, and who continued to try to broker a constitutional settlement after Mirabeau’s death. Morris meets with La Marck in a small conciliabule (La Marck, Pellin, Beaumetz, Montmorin, and Morris) to discuss how to manage the political situation. La Marck represents the category of noblemen who genuinely wanted constitutional monarchy and found the choices narrowing around them.

  • Monciel and Brémond — Former interior minister Monciel and his Provençal agent Brémond are the principal figures in the royalist counter-conspiracy described in Part XII. Their relationship with Morris was commercial and conspiratorial simultaneously; after August 10, Morris sheltered Monciel and managed the fallout from Brémond’s mishandling of the 5,000 louis. The editor traces this story through letters and the later accounting to Madame Royale.

  • Washington’s watch — A detail that threads through both volumes: Washington commissioned a watch before Morris left for France, Morris bought one in Paris and reported on it with deadpan precision to the General, and the watch recurs in letters as a symbol of the relationship between Morris and Washington that underlies the entire enterprise.

Coverage Guide

  • The Constitution of 1791 in formation — Morris’s diary provides a continuous view of the constitutional debates from inside the reforming circles: the Constituent Assembly’s late-stage work, the key political choices (single versus bicameral legislature, strength of the executive veto), the king’s acceptance, the Champ de Mars massacre as a turning point in public opinion. His American constitutional experience makes his observations on French constitutional design unusually grounded.

  • The Flight to Varennes — Covered through Morris’s letters rather than diary entries; gives a clear narrative of what he understood about the king’s motives, the capture, and the political consequences. Cross-reference roland-memoires-vol2-1823 for the Girondin perspective on the same event.

  • Mirabeau’s death and political vacuum — The diary records the political moment of Mirabeau’s death and the absence of any successor capable of managing the space between the monarchy and the Assembly. Morris had known Mirabeau through the previous volume; his assessment of what was lost is implicit in subsequent entries.

  • Morris as US Minister during the collapse — Part XII is the most historically consequential section: Morris navigating American commercial interests, maintaining diplomatic relations with an increasingly radical government, sheltering royalist refugees, holding royal funds, and dispatching reports to Jefferson that describe the fall of the monarchy and the opening months of the Republic from an insider-outsider position no other American witness possessed.

  • The 5,000 louis and the royalist counter-conspiracy — The most sensitive element of the diary and the one most carefully handled by the editor. The accounting to Madame Royale, preserved in full, is a primary document for the royalist resistance operations of 1792 and for Morris’s personal entanglement with them.

  • August 10 and its aftermath — The diary suppression after August 10 is itself a historical fact. What survives — letters, dispatches, the Madame Royale accounting — documents the event from the perspective of a witness who had refugees in his house and royal gold in his custody.

  • The September Massacres — Recorded in dispatches rather than diary entries; Morris’s reports to Jefferson describe the political situation without the graphic detail found in French witnesses’ accounts. His role as US Minister requires a degree of diplomatic restraint that is itself historically significant.

Quality

Same University of Michigan copy and 1939 Houghton Mifflin Riverside Press edition as Volume I. OCR quality is equivalent: reliable prose, consistent abbreviations, French passages preserved. The shift from diary to letters in the final sections of the volume creates a somewhat different texture — the letters are more formal, more polished, and more diplomatically restrained than the diary entries. The editor’s bracketed notes are more extensive in Part XII, compensating for the suppressed diary by drawing on other contemporary sources. The index at the end of Volume II (which covers both volumes) is reproduced in the OCR and is readable, giving volume and page numbers for all named individuals.
