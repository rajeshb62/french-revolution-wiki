Antoine Fantin-Desodoards, Histoire Philosophique de la Révolution de France, depuis la première Assemblée des Notables jusqu’à la paix de 1801, Tome VII. 4th edition. Angers: Mame père et fils / Paris: Belin, An IX (1801). The extracted raw text runs to approximately 16,830 lines and 645 KB, from a University of Michigan copy. Fantin-Desodoards (1738–1820) was a French citizen writing a multi-volume synthetic history of the whole Revolution as it was still unfolding; by the fourth edition he could extend the narrative to the Peace of Lunéville (1801). Volume VII covers Books XX, XXI, and XXII, spanning approximately 1796 to September 1797 (the Fructidor coup). ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

Contents at a Glance

Fantin-Desodoards writes as an informed, moderately conservative republican — critical of the Terror, respectful of the Directory’s institutions, suspicious of both Jacobin extremism and royalist reaction, and deeply admiring of the military genius displayed in the Italian campaign. His “philosophical” history means he pauses regularly to assess causes, to moralize on consequences, and to reflect on the relationship between constitutional forms and political reality. He cites classical precedents, draws comparisons between French institutions and ancient republics, and quotes from documents (dispatches, proclamations, letters) at length.

Volume VII covers one of the most consequential years of the Revolution: the Italian campaign of 1796–97, in which Bonaparte conquered Lombardy, forced Austria to seek terms, and reorganized northern Italy around a cluster of new republican states. Simultaneously it covers the Directory’s dangerous domestic politics — the Babeuf conspiracy of Floréal Year IV (spring 1796), the federalist and royalist threats, and the crisis of 18 Fructidor Year V (September 4, 1797), when the Directory used army force to purge the two councils of royalist-leaning deputies. By the end of the volume, Austria has accepted the preliminaries of Léoben, the Treaty of Campo Formio is approaching, and France has prevailed on the continent while Britain remains at war.

Book XX — The Italian Campaign, 1796 (First Phase)

  • Ch. I: Rhine armistice breaks; state of the armies — The armistice between Austria and France on the Rhine breaks down; strategic situation of Moreau’s army (Rhine-Moselle) and Jourdan’s (Sambre-Meuse) facing the Archduke. Fantin notes that the French could have accelerated the Italian campaign’s results by sending Rhine forces to reinforce Bonaparte, but political and geographic constraints prevented it. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. II: Pichegru resigns; Moreau takes command; Austrians abandon the Palatinate — Pichegru’s disgrace and departure (his secret royalist contacts were already suspected). Moreau assumes command of the Rhine-Moselle army and pursues the Austrian withdrawal east of the Rhine. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. III: Moreau crosses the Rhine — The Rhine crossing by Jourdan’s and Moreau’s armies in early summer 1796. Coordination problems between the two commands. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. IV: Capture of Fort Kehl — The storming of the Austrian bridgehead at Kehl on the Rhine. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. V: Battle of Rhinchel; capture of Fribourg and Frankfurt — Desaix’s division advances; Frankfurt is occupied. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. VI: The French advance to the Rhaetian Alps — The deepest French penetration into southern Germany in this campaign. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. VII: Field Marshal Wurmser enters Italy; revolt of Lugo — The Austrian relief of Mantua under Wurmser begins; the rebellion at Lugo in the Romagna against French requisitions is suppressed. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. VIII–IX: Battle of Castiglione — The first great defensive-offensive battle of the Italian campaign: Bonaparte abandons the siege of Mantua to mass against Wurmser’s relieving columns, defeats them at Castiglione (August 5, 1796), and re-establishes the siege. Fantin gives the battle in considerable tactical detail, including the strategic situation before and the aftermath — Austrian flags sent to the Directory, the relief of Mantua reversed. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XI: Organization of the Trentino principality — Bonaparte’s administrative reorganization of Trento after its occupation. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XII: Austrians in the Frioul; Battle of Bassano — A second Austrian attempt to relieve Mantua, this time through the Brenta valley; defeated at Bassano (September 8, 1796). ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XIII: Wurmser retreats into Mantua — Wurmser’s battered army takes refuge inside Mantua, which the French then besiege again. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XIV: Italian reactions to Austrian defeats — The political impact of French victories on the Italian populations: Milan, Bologna, Ferrara oscillating between hope and fear; the occupying French requiring requisitions that alienate support the ideology of liberation had generated. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XV: The Pope breaks the armistice — Pope Pius VI, emboldened by a temporary Austrian resurgence, breaks the armistice Bonaparte had imposed after the Faenza campaign. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XVI: Modena and Reggio raise the flag of liberty — Republican movements in the Este duchies, anticipating the later Cispadane Republic. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XVII: The British take Porto-Ferraio in Elba — The British seizure of the principal port of Elba (which Fantin notes Bonaparte had intended as a forward naval base to threaten British commerce through Livorno). ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XVIII–XX: Corsica — Pasquale Paoli’s power in Corsica; George III assumes the title “King of Corsica” under the Anglo-Corsican union; insurrections against the British; Corsica’s return to the French Republic. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

Book XXI — Consolidation and Internal Crisis, 1796–1797

  • Ch. I (opening): Interior of France, Year IV — A panoramic survey of France’s internal condition in the months before Fructidor: economic hardship, assignat inflation, the political atmosphere in Paris, the Directory’s uncertain authority, the competing factions within and outside the constitutional framework. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. II: France-Spain and France-Genoa alliances — The Treaty of San Ildefonso (August 1796) bringing Spain into the war against Britain; the treaty with the Genoese republic, which ties Genoa’s commercial and political fate to France. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. III: Vacillations in public opinion — Fantin’s analysis of why France, feared by all neighboring states, could not yet secure a peace that would allow it to consolidate. He identifies the Directory’s internal divisions and the competing factional pressures as the primary obstacle. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. IV: Parties dividing France since the constitutional regime — A systematic analysis of the political factions: royalists, Jacobins, moderate republicans, and the new electoral class emerging from the Year III constitution. Fantin argues that the factions’ irreconcilability made stable government structurally impossible without periodic coercive interventions. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. V: Reflections on French constitutional regime vs. ancient republics — A philosophical digression: Fantin compares the Directory constitution to the republics of Greece and Rome, arguing that the French system lacked the civic traditions and moral education that made ancient republicanism functional. A representative passage of his “philosophical” method. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. VI–XI: The Babeuf Conspiracy (Conjuration de Floréal) — The most detailed section in Volume VII devoted to domestic politics. Fantin covers the conspiracy in six chapters of escalating specificity.

    Ch. VI — Context and the conspiracy’s origins. Fantin opens with a reflection on opinion publique as a political force: “one must have studied it, in the midst of revolutionary convulsions, in the sections of Paris, or at the bar of the National Convention — always unjust in its impatience, but almost always in good faith in its aims — the abusive power of institutions it reprobated, trampling laws it had demanded; arbitrary and mysterious power, it always has a laudable goal and almost always overshoots it.” The conspiracy emerged from the Jacobin rump that had survived Thermidor and Vendémiaire: men who found all regular government intolerable once they had grown accustomed to power through anarchy, “like those whirlwinds of wind that, in a violent fire, constantly rekindle the devouring flames that generous hands strive to extinguish.” Babeuf himself, Fantin notes, was “only the obscure and secondary agent of a faction of whose secrets he perhaps himself knew only a part.” ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

    Ch. VII — Organization of the conspiracy. The operational structure was elaborate: a directoire secret de salut public directing the movement, with agents in each of Paris’s twelve arrondissements. These agents reported on food stores, arms depots, workshops, workers’ numbers and opinions; compiled lists of well-off patriots who could house provincial militants; organized subscription to fund the insurrection; provided lists of police spies to be neutralized or enlisted; and organized teams of groupeurs — men who circulated daily in the Tuileries gardens and other gathering-places reading aloud the most recent numbers of patriot journals to crowds. The design was for everything to be activated simultaneously on 22 Floréal Year IV. Drouet — the postmaster famous for recognizing Louis XVI at Varennes — was chosen as the conspiracy’s public face, “very suitable, by his enthusiasm and his ignorance, to the role of Seïde they had destined for him.” ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

    Ch. VIII — Babeuf’s letter to Bodson. Fantin quotes at length from a letter found at Babeuf’s address and included in the minister of police’s published collection. In it Babeuf defends the Robespierre Terror as correct in principle: “I think that government was the one best suited to make a revolution succeed… I do not think, as you do, that they committed great crimes and killed good republicans.” He argues that Robespierre could justifiably have said: “Let us suppress these tiresome nuisances and their good intentions — my opinion is that he did well. The salvation of twenty-five million men must not be weighed against consideration for a few equivocal individuals. A regenerator must see on a grand scale; his duty is to mow down everything that hinders him, everything that obstructs his passage, everything that can retard his arrival at the goal — scoundrels, idiots, the presumptuous alike, it is all the same; too bad for them, why were they in the way?” Babeuf adds that invoking Robespierre is tactically essential: “to revive Robespierre is to revive all the energetic patriots of the republic, and with them the people, who long listened to and followed only them.” Fantin quotes this letter at such length precisely because it is the most explicit surviving statement of the conspiracy’s ideology. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

    Ch. IX — Arrest of the conspirators. The conspiracy’s plan was to assassinate “a part of the deputies, the entire Directory, the ministers, and all members of the constituted authorities of Paris,” then proclaim a new regime. An informer in the conspiracy’s inner circle revealed the plan to the Directory, which arrested the leading figures before the date of action. Fantin is careful to note the distinction between those who were genuinely committed communists and those who were simply disaffected Jacobins riding the conspiracy for factional reasons — the distinction becomes important at the trial.

    Ch. XI — Trial at Vendôme. The conspirators were sent before a specially constituted high court at Vendôme rather than the ordinary courts, to prevent the political circus that a Paris jury might have produced. Babeuf and Darthé were condemned to death; others were deported. Fantin’s treatment is hostile to the conspiracy’s program but scrupulous about procedure — he regards the Vendôme court as a legitimate extraordinary jurisdiction rather than a new form of Terror, and is at pains to distinguish the Directory’s management of the trial from the Jacobin show-trials of 1793. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XII: Second Fructidor conspiracy — A further royalist-Jacobin agitation following the Babeuf trial; suppressed before reaching the scale of Floréal. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XIII–XIV: French enter Bavaria; Jourdan retreats; Battle of Neresheim/Battle of Teming — The Archduke Charles maneuvers against Jourdan on the Danube; Jourdan is badly beaten and forced to retreat hastily to the Rhine, threatening Moreau’s flanks. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XV: Moreau’s retreat; Battle of Biberach — Fantin presents Moreau’s strategic retreat from deep in southern Germany as one of the campaign’s great military achievements: an orderly withdrawal over immense distances under constant pressure, described admiringly as rivaling the classic retreats of military history. Biberach is a rearguard victory during the withdrawal. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XVI: The Irish descent (1796) — The French expedition to Ireland under Hoche: the fleet’s departure from Brest, the storm that scattered the fleet at Bantry Bay and prevented a landing. Fantin covers the strategic rationale (opening a second front against Britain using Irish republican sentiment) and the operational failure caused by weather and poor fleet cohesion. Cross-reference with mahan-sea-power-french-revolution-vol1 for the British naval perspective on this operation. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XVII–XVIII: Siege of Kehl; fall of Kehl — The Austrians besiege and eventually retake the bridgehead of Kehl on the Rhine that the French had captured in 1796. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XIX–XX: Alvinzi commands in Italy; Battle of Arcola — Field Marshal Alvinzi makes his second attempt to relieve Mantua through the Brenta and Adige valleys. At Arcola (November 15–17, 1796), the French face a village fortified by nature at the center of marshes and deep canals lined with artillery. Fantin traces the three-day battle in close tactical detail. On day one: Augereau seizes a flag and charges the bridge at Arcola himself — forced back; Bonaparte commands from the front of Augereau’s division and leads grenadiers at the same bridge at thirty paces when murderous Austrian fire breaks the column, and Bonaparte himself is knocked into a marsh from which his staff struggles to retrieve him under fire. A flanking column under General Guieux crosses the Adige two miles downstream, enters Arcola from the rear at midnight, and withdraws with hundreds of prisoners and four guns. Day two: equally fierce, equally indecisive. Day three: Massena presses from the left, Augereau from the front, a diversion from Porto-Legnago’s garrison hits the Austrian rear — Alvinzi abandons the field and retreats on Vicenza. Fantin compares the four-day engagement, by the marshal Trivulzio’s phrase, to Marignano as “a battle of giants.” ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XXI: Alvinzi retreats; Italian republics formed — Alvinzi withdraws; Mantua remains besieged. The political map of northern Italy begins to reshape around French-sponsored republics. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XXII: Alvinzi returns; Battle of Rivoli — The fourth Austrian attempt, again under Alvinzi: the battle of Rivoli (January 14, 1797), Napoleon’s most complete Italian victory. The Austrian force is destroyed; the way to Vienna is open. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

Book XXII — The Peace Settlement, 1797; 18 Fructidor

  • Ch. I: Pope resumes war; French in the Romagna — With Austria collapsing, Pope Pius VI’s gamble on an Austrian resurgence fails; French forces under Victor advance into the Romagna. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. II: Mantua surrenders — The capitulation of Mantua (February 2, 1797) after a six-month siege: Field Marshal Wurmser surrenders with his garrison. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. III: The Duchy of Urbino; negotiations with the Pope — The French occupy Urbino and the Papal legations; negotiations begin at the Hotel d’Espagne. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. IV: Peace with the Pope; Archduke Charles takes Italian command — The Treaty of Tolentino (February 19, 1797) strips the Pope of the Legations, Avignon, and cash and art; the Archduke Charles — the most capable Austrian commander, who had defeated Jourdan — is transferred to the Italian theatre. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. V–IX: The drive toward Vienna — Bonaparte crosses the Tagliamento, defeats the Austrians at Gradisca, takes Trieste, drives through the Norican Alps to Klagenfurt in the Austrian heartland. Bonaparte’s proclamation from Klagenfurt and his famous letter to the Archduke (offering peace while advancing) are quoted. The French reach Leoben, less than 100 miles from Vienna, and the Austrian court sues for terms. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. X: Bonaparte’s position amid the Alps — Fantin assesses the strategic risk Bonaparte accepted by advancing so deep while his communications were precarious and the Italian rear was potentially unstable. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XI: Armistice between Bonaparte and Archduke — The cease-fire that preceded Léoben. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XII–XIII: Sambre-Meuse and Rhine-Moselle cross the Rhine — Simultaneous Rhine crossing operations in support of Bonaparte’s advance: Hoche’s victory at Neuwied, the Rhine-Moselle army recaptures Fort Kehl. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XIV: Preliminaries of Léoben — The preliminary peace treaty signed April 18, 1797, between France and Austria, ending the Italian campaign’s military phase. Fantin gives the terms and the background of the secret articles (which ceded Venice to Austria in exchange for the Belgian Netherlands — a transaction not publicly disclosed). ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XV: Reflections on war — A philosophical chapter on the miseries and waste of war, unusually direct given that Fantin has been narrating a string of French victories. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

  • Ch. XVI–XXVII: 18 Fructidor and the Genoese crisis — The final section covers the Fructidor coup in detail alongside simultaneous events in Genoa. The Genoese crisis: the Genoese republic’s ambivalent posture toward France, French pressure, popular insurrection in Genoa against the oligarchy, the installation of a new government friendly to France. The Fructidor 18 (September 4, 1797) coup: the Directory, facing a royalist-leaning majority in the two councils after the elections of Year V, uses General Augereau (sent by Bonaparte from Italy with troops) to arrest or deport two Directors and 53 deputies. The journals are shut; the royalist press is suppressed. Fantin gives the full sequence: the Directory’s intelligence about royalist contacts with the Bourbon pretender (the Duverne de Presles revelations), the Directory’s session of 10 Fructidor, the coup itself, the permanent session of the surviving councils, the deportation law, the adresses to the French people. He is critical but not simplistic — he accepts that a genuine royalist threat existed while questioning whether the Directory’s methods were constitutional. ^[source: raw/fantin-histoire-philosophique-revolution-1801.txt]

Coverage Guide

  • Bonaparte’s Italian campaign, 1796–97 — This is the wiki’s most detailed narrative source for the operational campaigns: Castiglione, Bassano, Arcola, Rivoli, the drive to Leoben. Fantin gives tactical detail, strategic context, and political consequences for each battle. For the naval and strategic context from the British side, cross-reference mahan-sea-power-french-revolution-vol1.

  • The Babeuf conspiracy — Chapters VI–XI of Book XXI give the fullest account of the Conspiracy of Equals in the wiki: ideology, organization, Babeuf’s letter, the Vendôme trial.

  • Moreau’s retreat — Book XXI Chapter XV. One of the great operational feats of the campaign, treated admiringly by Fantin as a model of controlled withdrawal under pressure.

  • The Irish expedition of 1796 — Book XXI Chapter XVI, French side. Cross-reference mahan-sea-power-french-revolution-vol1 Chapter XI for the British/naval view of the same operation.

  • Fructidor 18 (1797) — Book XXII, final chapters. A detailed account of the Directory’s constitutional coup against the royalist-leaning councils: the intelligence, the preparation, the operation, and the deportation law.

  • Italian political reorganization — The formation of French-sponsored Italian republics (Cispadane, anticipations of Cisalpine) is traced through Books XX–XXII as a running theme alongside the military narrative.

  • The Peace of Tolentino — Book XXII Chapter IV. The treaty that stripped the Pope of the Legations and effectively ended Papal temporal power in central Italy for this period.

  • The Preliminaries of Léoben — Book XXII Chapter XIV. The end of the first Austrian war; the secret articles pointing toward the Treaty of Campo Formio are identified.

Quality

University of Michigan copy, OCR from an Angers/Paris 1801 print. The text is 18th-century French administrative and historical prose: long, complex sentences, frequent classical allusions, chapter headings that summarize contents in the manner of the period. Running page headers (“RÉVOLUTION / DE FRANCE” alternating) are picked up by the OCR. Chapter numbers are sometimes misread by the OCR (Roman numerals — “CHAPITRE XI” vs “CHAPITRE XII” vs “CHAPITRE XII I”). Quotations from official documents (proclamations, letters, Directory resolutions) are readable. Battle sequences with place names are generally accurate though accent marks are inconsistent. Medium difficulty for French readers; high difficulty for readers unfamiliar with the period’s geographic and institutional vocabulary.
