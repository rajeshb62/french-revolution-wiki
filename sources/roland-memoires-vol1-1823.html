Marie-Jeanne Roland de la Platière (Madame Roland), Mémoires, Tome Premier. Paris: Rapilly, Collin de Plancy, F. Baroyer, 1823. The extracted raw text runs to approximately 15,030 lines and 603 KB, from a New York Public Library copy. This is Volume I — the companion to roland-memoires-vol2-1823. Written in the Abbaye and Sainte-Pélagie prisons between August and November 1793. The two volumes together form the complete published edition; Volume I contains the personal memoir and the first Roland ministry; Volume II contains the second ministry, the imprisonments, the portraits, and the trial documents. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

Contents at a Glance

Volume I has a different character from Volume II. Where Volume II is political — prison-written accounts of events Roland witnessed in the corridors of the Girondin government — Volume I is primarily autobiographical. Roland began writing the personal memoir on August 9, 1793, the day she arrived at Sainte-Pélagie, specifically because the political notices she had written during the preceding five weeks at the Abbaye had just been destroyed by the authorities. She started over, this time reaching back to childhood.

The personal memoirs are written under the explicit knowledge that she may not survive. She says so in the opening: “a prisoner, perhaps destined for a violent and unexpected death.” This gives the three autobiographical parts their characteristic quality — the retrospective intenseness of someone deciding what, of her whole life, is worth preserving in the time she has. She covers her childhood in a Paris engraver’s workshop, her years at a convent, her intellectual formation, her reading, her religious crisis and loss of faith, her relationship with her mother and the grief of her death, her long courtship and eventual marriage to Jean-Marie Roland, and her political awakening through the Revolution.

The volume ends with the historical notices on the first Roland ministry (March–June 1792): how Roland came to the attention of the Girondins, his appointment as Minister of the Interior by Louis XVI, the cabinet’s attempt to govern constitutionally under increasing royal resistance, Roland’s famous letter to the king (which Roland partly drafted), his dismissal, and the political deterioration through to August 10, 1792 — the point where Volume II picks up.

Notice sur Madame Roland (1823 editors)

The biographical notice by the Rapilly/Collin de Plancy editors provides the external view of Roland’s life: birth on March 17, 1754, daughter of the engraver Gratien Phlipon; her precocious intellectual development; marriage to Jean-Marie Roland in 1780; her role as his intellectual collaborator and political hostess; the two ministries; her arrest; her execution on November 8, 1793 (18 Brumaire, Year II). The editors frame her as a woman of superior intellect and sound judgment who possessed, as a “rare and fatal gift,” an imagination easily exalted — who was a respectful daughter, devoted wife, tender mother, and excellent friend, but who stepped beyond the lines prescribed by those roles out of love of country. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

Première Partie — Mémoires particuliers (begun August 9, 1793)

Roland opens the personal memoir with a declaration of method and character that is also a portrait: “The tyrants can persecute me; but debase me? Never, never!” Her political notices have been destroyed; she will write memoirs instead, and “accommodating myself prudently to my own weakness in a moment when I am painfully affected, I am going to speak of myself, the better to distract myself from it.” She will tell the truth about herself, good and bad, with equal freedom: “one who dares not bear good witness to oneself is almost always a coward who knows and fears the ill that might be said of him.” She will apply the same standard to others: father, mother, friends, husband — “I will paint them as they are or as I have seen them.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

Father: Gratien Phlipon. Her father was an engraver and goldsmith, a craftsman of middling talent but considerable industry, who employed a number of workers on the Pont-Neuf. Roland’s description is precise and unsparing: he was robust, active, vain about his appearance, loved his wife and jewelry, had the superficial taste and knowledge that the fine arts give to those who practice them at a low level. “One cannot say that he was a virtuous man; but he had much of what is called honor: he would have overcharged for a thing more than it was worth; but he would have killed himself rather than not pay the price of something he had bought.” His ambition — the fatal trait Roland identifies in the account — pushed him toward commerce and gem-dealing rather than contenting himself with his craft; he ended by ruining himself. His social world was not merchants but artists: painters and sculptors came to the workshop, and this artistic milieu formed Roland’s earliest aesthetic education. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

Mother: Marguerite Bimont. Her mother brought to the marriage “with very little money, a celestial soul and a charming figure.” Eldest of six children, she had been a second mother to her siblings, and married at twenty-six rather late, “to make way for her sisters.” She deserved better: “her sensitive heart, her agreeable mind, should have united her with someone enlightened and delicate.” Instead she had a husband whose ambitions and commercial risks consumed the household. Roland’s portrait of her mother is the warmest in the volume — patient, tactful in her son’s reading supervision, protective without being restrictive. The loss of her mother in middle age is presented as the event that ended the protected world of childhood and forced full personal responsibility. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

Childhood reading and Plutarch. Roland’s access to books was early and intensive. She describes reading secretly in her father’s library, her parents tacitly permitting what they did not formally allow: “there seemed to be a tacit convention between us three.” She worked through travel narratives (Regnard’s voyages were the first), then theatrical works, then Dacier’s translation of Plutarch’s Lives. The Plutarch passage is the most celebrated in the memoir: “I will never forget the Lent of 1763 — I was then nine years old — when I carried it to church in place of my prayer book.” From that moment, she writes, dated “the impressions and ideas that made me republican, without my thinking of becoming one.” She read Fénelon’s Télémaque and Tasso’s Jerusalem Delivered — and found herself physically affected: “reading aloud the episode of Calypso’s island, my breath rose, I felt a sudden fire cover my face, and my altered voice would have betrayed my agitations. I was Eucharis for Telemachus, and Herminia for Tancred.” Then Voltaire — she records being reprimanded for reading Candide by a neighbor woman with a “sour face, fat to the belt, grimacing with importance,” while her own mother simply told her to put it back without changing the arrangement that gave Roland access to whatever she found. Her father gave her Fénelon’s Treatise on the Education of Girls and Locke on the education of children — she notes the comedy: giving to the pupil what is meant for the master. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

The convent. Roland entered the Congrégation des dames on the rue Neuve-Saint-Etienne, faubourg Saint-Marcel, on May 7, 1765 — at eleven years and two months. She had asked to go herself, presenting it to her parents as a desire for proper preparation for her first communion. The first night in the convent is described with the precision of someone who has lived with the memory for thirty years: the shared room with four other girls her age, the faint light, her getting up quietly to go to the window, the moonlit garden below, “the most profound silence reigning in these places — I listened to it, as it were, with a kind of respect; great trees projected here and there their gigantic shadow, and promised a sure shelter for tranquil meditation; I raised my eyes toward the sky, it was pure and serene; I believed I felt the presence of the Divinity smiling at my sacrifice.”

The religious life of the convent was intense and genuinely felt. Roland was a sincere Catholic at this period; the discipline, the prayer, the community of women — she found it, she says, not oppressive but nourishing. She describes individual nuns with the same sharp precision she applies to political figures: their characters, their vocations, which ones were genuinely devout and which performed piety. A nun called Sainte-Agathe was her closest companion; their friendship outlasted the convent. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

Deuxième Partie — Suite des Mémoires particuliers (begun August 28, 1793)

Roland resumes on August 28 with a passage of political grief before re-entering the personal narrative: France is “only a vast theatre of carnage, a bloody arena where its own children tear each other apart.” The armies advance and flee alternately; able generals are accused of treason because representatives who understand nothing of war find fault with what they cannot comprehend; those who wanted to establish liberty have been sacrificed to intrigue and brigandage. She invokes Brutus: “We have strayed like you. Those pure men whose ardent soul aspired to liberty… flattered themselves, like you, that the overthrow of tyranny would open the reign of justice and peace: it has been only the signal for hateful passions and the most hideous vices.”

Reading after the convent — religious evolution. Back in the personal account: after the convent years, she continued her reading program intensively. Saint Francis de Sales and Saint Augustine’s manual became her favorite meditations; then Bossuet’s works of religious controversy — which, though written to defend Catholicism, acquainted her with objections to it and set her on the path of reasoning her belief. “That was the first step; there was a long way from there to the scepticism I was to reach some years later, after having been successively Jansenist, Cartesian, Stoic, and deist! What roads, to end up with patriotism, which has put me in irons!” She adds Madame de Sévigné’s letters — “her agreeable facility, her graces, her gaiety, her tenderness” — and records the warm domestic scene of her mother and great-aunt Angélique arguing over whether Roland’s education would “spoil” her.

Scene at Madame de Boismorel’s. A vivid social comedy from this period: her mother takes her to visit Madame de Boismorel, a lady of quality, expecting to show off her granddaughter. Madame de Boismorel, sitting with her dog working tapestry, receives them with the automatic condescension of rank: “Come here, my heart, sit beside me. She is timid — what age is she, your granddaughter, mademoiselle Rotisset? She is a little dark, but the complexion is excellent; it will clear before long… You are not a little devout, are you?” The child answers with composed directness at each question. “Take care she doesn’t become a bluestocking, it would be a great pity,” Madame de Boismorel concludes. Roland uses the scene to establish the class humiliation at the heart of her childhood — her mother called “mademoiselle” (unmarried form) rather than “madame” — and her own early formation of a dignity that refused to recognize condescension as legitimate. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

Her mother’s death. Her mother was not yet fifty when she died — an abscess in the head, unrecognized until an effusion from the nose and ears at death explained the strange stuffiness she had suffered for a long time. Roland was away for two hours on the afternoon of the day it happened; she came home at five-thirty to find her mother “frappée.” By midnight she was gone. The grief was violent and physical: fainting, arms extended and rigid, losing consciousness repeatedly; unable to fix her attention on anything; seemingly deranged, answering her own thoughts instead of what was said to her. The abbé Legrand, visiting, had the insight that she needed to talk about her mother rather than avoid the subject — he led her through conversations that gradually brought other thoughts alongside. When he judged her ready for a book, he brought her the Héloïse of Jean-Jacques: “its reading was truly my first distraction. I was twenty-one years old… Rousseau made on me then an impression comparable to what Plutarch had made at eight years old; it seemed that he was the nourishment suited to my nature.” The discovery of Rousseau at twenty-one — as intellectual equal rather than childhood food — is the Deuxième Partie’s decisive intellectual event. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

Troisième Partie — Continuation des Mémoires particuliers (begun September 10, 1793)

Covers from age twenty-one until she met Jean-Marie Roland.

Young womanhood and the social world. Roland describes the Parisian world she moved in after her mother’s death: visits to relative and friends, observations of the society around her, her judgments of the people she encountered. She is already the sharp character analyst visible in the Portraits of Volume II — she notices vanity, self-deception, and the gap between people’s self-presentation and their actual conduct. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

First encounters with political life. The pre-revolutionary stirrings of the 1780s — the pamphlet literature, the Assembly of Notables, the general atmosphere of reform expectation — are traced as Roland experienced them: through reading, through conversations with her father’s circle, and through her own increasingly formed political opinions. She was not passive: she was already corresponding and forming views. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

Meeting Roland. Jean-Marie Roland entered her life through a correspondence that preceded their meeting — he was a manufacturing inspector in the Lyon generalship, contributing to the Encyclopédie méthodique, and came to know her father’s intellectual contacts. When he eventually visited Paris, his brother (a Benedictine prior at the Collège de Cluny) came to see her and communicated the notes Roland was sending from his Italian travels. She read his travel journals and manuscripts: “his soul, his austere probity, his rigorous principles, his knowledge and taste appeared there undisguised.” She found in him, she says, “an ami” — treating him as without sex, as a philosopher who existed purely through reason. A correspondence developed. She was thirty-two years old when she met him; he was forty-six. Three-part personal memoir ends here; the rest is in the Aperçu. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

Aperçu de ce qui me restait à traiter

The summary of what Roland would have written. She gives Jean-Marie Roland’s biography from his manuscripts: born to an old family of the robe distinguished for its integrity, whose fortune had already dissolved from disorder and extravagance. The youngest of five brothers who were expected to enter the Church, he alone refused — also refusing commerce — and left home at nineteen without resources. He went to Nantes, placed himself with a shipowner to learn various trades, and planned to sail to India. A hemorrhage stopped him: he was told he would die at sea if he went. He made his way to Rouen, where a relative who was an inspector of manufactures proposed he enter that branch of administration; he distinguished himself there by activity and work and found himself usefully placed. Travels and study divided his time. He published his Italian travel notes, cut into letter form and sent for printing to friends at Dieppe — “an excellent fund, lacking only better composition to be the first-ranked of Italian travel accounts.” She had intended to re-work it with him after their marriage but events overtook them. The Aperçu also names the people she would have written about: cousin Desportes, her old servant Mignonne who died in her arms saying “I am content” — and the entanglement of her father with a questionable associate named Leveilly, whose daughter she had tried to help, and who ended in abasement. These are fragments: “at such a pace I would have a long work ahead, for which I no longer have enough to live.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

Notes détachées

Scattered notes of varying length, expanding on individuals or incidents mentioned in the three main parts. Some overlap with the Portraits material of Volume II. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

Lettres de Madame Roland à M. Bosc

A selection of Roland’s letters to Louis-Augustin Bosc d’Antic, naturalist and longtime friend of the Roland household. The letters, written over several years leading up to the Revolution and into the revolutionary period, are different in register from the memoir: more immediate, more anecdotal, more spontaneous. They show Roland as correspondent — witty, engaged, full of observation — before the political burden of the Girondin years imposed its weight. They cover domestic life in Lyon and Paris, observations on people and events, philosophical reflections, and the gradual intensification of political engagement as the Revolution approached and began. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

Notices historiques sur la Révolution — Premier Ministère de Roland

The political historical section, written after the personal memoirs and covering the first Roland ministry.

Arrival in Paris, February 1791 — the Constituent Assembly and Brissot. The Notices begin not with the ministry itself but with the Rolands’ first extended Paris stay in February 1791, when Roland was sent as a municipal deputy by Lyon to present the city’s financial crisis to the Constituent Assembly. Twenty thousand workers had been without bread the previous winter; Roland was tasked with seeking relief. They arrived February 20. She had not seen Paris in five years and had followed the Assembly’s work from Lyon with intense engagement. She went immediately to the Assembly debates: “I saw the powerful Mirabeau, the astonishing Cazalès, the audacious Maury, the astute Lameths, the cold Barnave.” Her footnote on Mirabeau is one of the most quoted in the volume: “The only man in the revolution whose genius could direct men and impose itself on an assembly: great by his faculties, small by his vices; but always superior to the common, and infallibly its master when he took the trouble to command it. He died shortly after: I thought it was at the right moment for his glory and for liberty; but events have taught me to regret him more. The counterweight of a man of this force was needed, to oppose the action of a swarm of curs, and to preserve us from the domination of brigands.”

Brissot came to visit them. She describes their first encounter after their long correspondence: “There is nothing more pleasant than the first meeting of persons who have bound themselves through correspondence, without knowing each other’s faces reciprocally: one looks with curiosity to see whether the features of the face correspond to the physiognomy of the soul.” Brissot’s simple manners, his frankness, his natural negligence, seemed “in perfect harmony with the austerity of his principles; but I found in him a kind of levity of spirit and character that did not equally suit the gravity of philosophy.” She grew to know him better over the following months and years: “It is impossible to unite a more complete disinterestedness to a greater zeal for the public good, and to devote oneself to the good with more self-forgetfulness; but his writings are more suited than his person to effect it, because they have all the authority that reason, justice, and enlightenment give to works, while his person can take none, for lack of dignity.” Her portrait is nearly as detailed as those in Vol II: “The best of men, good husband, tender father, faithful friend, virtuous citizen… confiding to the point of imprudence, gay, naïve, ingenuous as one is at fifteen, he was made to live with sages and to be the dupe of the wicked.” He “judges mankind well and does not know individual men at all. He knows that vices exist; but he cannot believe that someone who speaks to him with a good face is vicious; and when he has recognized people for such, he treats them like fools one pities, without guarding against them.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

Roland’s route to ministerial office. Through Brissot’s circle the Rolands became part of the Girondin network in Paris during this 1791 stay. Roland was already known through his encyclopédiste writings and administrative record. When the Girondins formed a cabinet in March 1792 under the constitutional monarchy — Dumouriez at Foreign Affairs, Servan at War, Clavière at Finance — Roland was named Minister of the Interior: a genuine reformist appointment of an administrator with deep expertise in French industry and commerce. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

The ministry’s work. Roland describes the Interior Ministry’s actual operations: the breadth of its remit (internal administration, public works, manufactures, communications, education, public relief), the quality and character of the ministry’s staff, the difficulties of governing constitutionally under a king who was constitutionally obliged to accept ministers but not obliged to support them. She covers specific initiatives, specific obstacles, specific moments of political friction with the Tuileries. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

Roland’s letter to the king. The most politically significant act of the first ministry: Roland sent Louis XVI a long letter in June 1792 calling on him to promulgate the decrees he had vetoed (on the deportation of refractory priests and on the establishment of a camp of federal volunteers near Paris), warning him that resistance was dangerous to the constitutional order, and arguing in plain terms for the republican and constitutional position. The letter was substantially drafted by Madame Roland herself. She describes its composition, its intent, and the king’s reaction — immediate dismissal of Roland and his Girondin colleagues from the cabinet. The letter was read publicly to the Legislative Assembly and became a political manifesto; she gives her own assessment of its effect. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

The political deterioration to August 10. After the dismissal, Roland describes the period from June to August 10, 1792 as a sustained effort to prevent what they all feared: the court’s counter-revolutionary gamble and the popular explosion it would provoke. The discussions she records — planning with Barbaroux and Servan for a possible southern republic if Paris fell to the court, taking out maps, tracing lines of demarcation, calculating forces — are among the most historically significant passages in the volume. They show the Girondins, before August 10, actively contemplating federalism as a last resort against royalist reconquest: a contingency plan that would later be weaponized against them. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

The Sentinelle. Roland describes the origin of the newspaper La Sentinelle: the Girondin leadership’s decision to fund a placard journal to counteract the court press; Louvet chosen as its writer; the awkward financial arrangement whereby Dumouriez channelled funds from the Foreign Affairs secret account through Pétion as Paris mayor to the Interior Ministry to fund the paper. A minor but revealing passage about how Girondin political operations actually worked. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-vol1-1823.txt]

Coverage Guide

  • Madame Roland’s childhood and formation — The Première, Deuxième, and Troisième Parties are the only source in the wiki for Roland’s pre-revolutionary life: her artisan origins, her convent education, her reading of Plutarch and Rousseau, her religious formation and subsequent loss of faith. Essential for understanding the intellectual and moral foundations behind her political role.

  • The first Roland ministry (March–June 1792) — The Notices historiques are the primary source in the wiki for this period: how a Girondin cabinet formed under the constitutional monarchy, what it tried to do, and how it was dismissed. Cross-reference roland-memoires-vol2-1823 which begins with the second ministry.

  • Roland’s letter to the king (June 1792) — The political letter that ended the first ministry. Roland describes its composition (she largely drafted it), its content, and its consequences. One of the most cited documents of the constitutional monarchy’s last months.

  • Girondin contingency planning for federalism — The passages describing the discussions with Barbaroux and Servan about a possible southern republic if Paris fell are historically significant: they show the Girondins actually planning for federalism before August 10, which makes the later Montagnard accusation of federalism more complicated than it is usually presented.

  • Roland as correspondent (Lettres à Bosc) — The Bosc letters are the most personal and spontaneous of Roland’s writings in the wiki: they predate the political burden and show her voice in a different register. Useful for characterization and for the pre-revolutionary atmosphere in the Roland household.

  • Complement to Volume II — This volume fills the personal and pre-political background that Volume II assumes but does not provide. Reading the two together gives the complete picture: childhood and political formation (Vol. I), political crisis and prison (Vol. II).

Quality

NYPL copy, OCR from the same 1823 Rapilly/Collin de Plancy print run as Volume II. Quality is identical: readable French prose with running headers (“MÉMOIRES DE Mme ROLAND” / “NOTICES SUR LA RÉVOLUTION”) picked up by the OCR at the top of each page, accent marks sometimes lost, ligatures occasionally split. Editors’ footnotes are marked and readable. The Lettres à Bosc and the Notices historiques sections are somewhat denser with names and political references than the personal memoir sections; the personal memoir prose is the most fluid and readable portion of the volume.
