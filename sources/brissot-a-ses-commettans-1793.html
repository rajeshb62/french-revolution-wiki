J.-P. Brissot de Warville, À ses Commettans, sur la Situation de la Convention Nationale, sur l’Influence des Anarchistes, & les Maux qu’elle a Causés, sur la Nécessité d’Anéantir cette Influence pour Sauver la République; Précédé d’autres Pièces intéressantes de Brissot. Paris: Imprimerie de P. Provost; reprinted London: R. Edwards, No. 142 Bond-Street, & T. Spilsbury & Sons, Snow-hill, 1794. The extracted raw text runs to approximately 9,320 lines and 397 KB, from a New York Public Library copy (Gordon Lester Ford Collection, presented by his sons Worthington Chauncey Ford and Paul Leicester Ford). Brissot (1754–1793) was the leading Girondin journalist and politician, editor of the Patriote Français, deputy for Eure-et-Loir, one of the leading advocates for declaring war on Austria and later on Britain. He was arrested after the coup of May 31–June 2, 1793, condemned by the Revolutionary Tribunal, and guillotined on October 31, 1793. The texts in this volume were written between August 1792 and May 22, 1793. ^[source: raw/brissot-a-ses-commettans-1793.txt]

Contents at a Glance

This volume is a London reprint, produced for English readers, of four separate Brissot texts that circulated widely in France and were subsequently reprinted abroad. The publisher’s preface (Avertissement) explains: “The Address of Brissot to his constituents, which has made so much noise on the Continent, having also excited much curiosity in our Country, it was judged proper to reprint it here, and to precede it with some of the Decrees of the Brissotine Faction concerning the Government of Great Britain.” The framing — “Faction Brissotine” — signals that the London publishers are not sympathetic to Brissot; they are printing his texts as documents of an enemy of Britain, available for English readers to judge.

The text is in 18th-century French typography with long-s characters throughout (the character ſ is rendered in the OCR as “f,” making standard words appear in altered form: “fes” = ses, “eft” = est, “fufpenfion” = suspension, “puiffances” = puissances). The texts are readable with standard French literacy and awareness of this convention.

The four texts are printed in the order in which Brissot composed them: the post-August 10 declaration to foreign powers; the January 1793 report on British policy; the February 1793 report calling for war declaration; and the May 1793 address to his constituents. Together they form a continuous record of Girondin foreign and domestic policy positions from the fall of the monarchy through the eve of the Girondin overthrow. ^[source: raw/brissot-a-ses-commettans-1793.txt]

Text I — Projet de Déclaration de l’Assemblée Nationale aux Puissances Étrangères (post-August 10, 1792)

Drafted by Brissot and printed by order of the National Assembly. This is the Assembly’s formal statement to neutral foreign powers — particularly those that had recalled their ambassadors or suspended relations following Louis XVI’s suspension on August 10 — explaining the legal and constitutional basis of what had been done.

The case against the king. The declaration opens by summarizing the grounds for Louis XVI’s suspension: he had consistently surrounded himself with men who detested the Revolution; when patriots entered his council he expelled them; he formed a guard on counter-revolutionary principles; he paid his former body-guards (suppressed by decree) who were in open rebellion; he worked to destroy the popular societies, to undermine the National Assembly, to provoke conflict between the people and the National Guard, to discredit the assignats, to facilitate the entry of émigrés — “and the king followed and encouraged all these projects with criminal constancy.” The proof had been found in the registers of the civil list expenditures and in other authentic documents. He had used the money the nation gave him to maintain royal splendor to “crush the nation and stifle liberty” — to pay the assassins of the people who had raised him to the throne.

The legal argument. The Assembly had searched the constitution for the means to arrest the course of these betrayals, examining whether the king had fallen into the cases that pronounce abdication of the crown, when the insurrection of August 10 anticipated the decision. The declaration argues that the Assembly’s response — suspension of the king pending Convention judgment — was itself constitutionally grounded: the suspension power exists in the constitution for cases of absence, and by necessary analogy for cases of incapacity or when public safety imperiously demands it. The Convention is being called not as a revolutionary act but as an appeal to national sovereignty: “only it can, through new organs, pronounce between the two powers.”

The argument to neutral powers. Foreign governments that have recalled their ambassadors or suspended relations on grounds of the king’s suspension are, the declaration argues, contradicting themselves: they had recognized the French constitution, which contains the people’s inalienable right to change their government; to break relations on grounds of the suspension is to interfere in France’s internal government, the very thing they declared they would not do. The king under the French constitution “is only the first of the public functionaries, only the organ of the nation with foreign powers: a simple citizen if considered in himself.” Relations with France are formed for the nation, not for the king. The declaration notes the irony of Britain: France had not interfered when Britain’s “Glorious Revolution” changed its government and dynasty; how can Britain now adopt the principles it then combatted? ^[source: raw/brissot-a-ses-commettans-1793.txt]

Text II — Rapport fait au nom du Comité de Défense Générale sur les Dispositions du Gouvernement Britannique envers la France, & sur les Mesures à prendre (January 12, 1793)

Delivered by Brissot to the Convention on January 12, 1793, printed by order of the Convention.

Britain’s response to August 10. The report traces the deterioration of Franco-British relations from their pre-August 10 equilibrium. Before the suspension of the king, Britain had maintained exact neutrality in the Franco-Austrian/Prussian war — profiting commercially from staying out, and consolidating its ministry through commercial prosperity. “This neutrality was maintained until the immortal day of August 10.” After the suspension, Britain recalled its ambassador Lord Gower on August 17, citing the futile pretext that his letters of credence were only for a king, as if new credentials could not simply have been issued. Brissot reads this as interference in French internal affairs under cover of a principle of non-interference that Britain simultaneously professed.

The diplomatic sequence, August–January. After the French victories at Jemappes and Spires, and the conquest of Savoy and Brabant, British ministers concluded that this new Republic, “which was making its debut in such a brilliant manner amid the greatest difficulties, which at its dawn was crushing the best armies commanded by the ablest generals,” might consolidate itself and carry the flame of revolution across Europe. A negotiation was established in October–November 1792; at that point the British ministry did not even complain about the opening of the Scheldt.

The deterioration. The report then details how British policy hardened through November–December 1792: the Scheldt decree, the November 19 decree of fraternity to peoples wishing to recover their liberty, and above all the impending trial and execution of Louis XVI together produced a change in British public posture. Britain now demanded that France explain its intentions; France responded with dignity and moderation; Britain found reasons to escalate each time France conceded.

The three grievances and the draft decree. Brissot presents three French demands: (1) execution of Article 4 of the 1786 commercial treaty, which would allow French citizens to travel and reside in England without the humiliating restrictions imposed by a January act of Parliament; (2) free export of grain and provisions from Britain for French purchasers; (3) clarification of what the recent British armaments are directed at. If satisfaction is not given on all three points, the Republic reserves the right to take the measures that its interest and security require. A draft decree to this effect is printed with the report. ^[source: raw/brissot-a-ses-commettans-1793.txt]

Text III — Rapport sur les Hostilités du Roi d’Angleterre et du Stadhouder des Provinces-Unies, et sur la Nécessité de Déclarer que la République Françoise est en Guerre avec Eux (February 1, 1793)

Presented to the Convention on February 1, 1793, by Brissot in the name of the Committee of General Defense, printed by order of the Convention.

Britain wants war. The opening declaration: “The court of England wants war: Citizens, you can no longer doubt it.” Three acts prove it: the expulsion of the French ambassador within eight days, the public mourning displayed for Louis XVI, and the request to Parliament for a considerable addition of land and sea forces following news of the execution. To expel France’s representative and arm at the news of “the death of the traitor” is to say to Europe and the universe: “France condemned Louis, and I absolve him; she condemned him as a traitor, and I hold him innocent; I see in France only rebels and sacrilegists; France wants neither king nor royalty, and I wish to avenge the king and restore royalty. I wish to join the coalition of kings leagued to support the cause of all thrones.”

The Stadtholder. The report covers simultaneous Dutch hostility: the Stadtholder, acting in concert with the British court, has taken measures hostile to France in the United Provinces. The Republic cannot maintain French commerce and interests in Holland under these conditions.

The argument for declaring war. Brissot argues that France did not seek this war; it had used every means to preserve peace with England. But peace is no longer possible once England has chosen to treat the French Republic as an illegitimate rebel against monarchy. The Convention is asked to formally declare that the Republic is at war with the King of England and the Stadtholder. The execution of Louis XVI — which Brissot characterizes throughout as the just condemnation of a traitor — is the immediate occasion for British belligerence, but the deeper cause is that no tyrant can forgive France its liberty. ^[source: raw/brissot-a-ses-commettans-1793.txt]

Text IV — J. P. Brissot, Député du Département d’Eure et Loire, À ses Commettans, sur la Situation de la Convention Nationale, sur l’Influence des Anarchistes, & les Maux qu’elle a Causés, sur la Nécessité d’Anéantir cette Influence pour Sauver la République (May 22, 1793)

Dated Paris, May 22, 1793 — nine days before the Montagnard coup that would proscribe Brissot and the other leading Girondins. This is the text for which the volume is principally named: Brissot’s address to his constituents in Eure-et-Loir, his political reckoning with what has gone wrong in the Convention, and his call to the nation to judge between the two parties.

The “Avis aux Lecteurs” preface. Brissot opens with a preface in which he acknowledges that “complete truth has never been told since the beginning of the Revolution” because people feared that saying it would harm the cause of liberty, discourage the people, and obstruct affairs. This circumspection was appropriate when nations save themselves through a few men or a few bodies; it is fatal when only the nation itself can save itself. “To conceal from it its situation is to encourage its lethargy; it can only be brought to make great efforts, to direct them well, when it is well enlightened about that situation.” He acknowledges he may be thought too severe toward certain individuals and bodies. “I have been truthful, and I had to be.”

The three propositions. The main text is organized around three demonstrations:

  1. That a party of anarchists has dominated and still dominates almost all deliberations of the Convention and operations of the Executive Council.
  2. That this party has been and still is the sole cause of all the ills, both internal and external, afflicting France.
  3. That the Republic can only be saved by taking rigorous measures to wrest the nation’s representatives from the despotism of this faction.

The definition of anarchy. Before presenting the evidence, Brissot insists on defining his terms: “Laws without execution, constituted authorities without force and debased, crime unpunished, property attacked, the security of individuals violated, the morals of the People corrupted; neither constitution, nor government, nor justice: these are the traits of anarchy.” He warns against the abuse of words — the “political charlatans” who confuse anarchism with patriotism.

Brissot’s own principles. He describes what he believed on arriving at the Convention: since the monarchy was annihilated and the Republic established, since all powers were in the hands of the people or its representatives, the patriots had to change their conduct in accordance with the changed situation. The insurrectional movement had to cease — where there is no tyranny to overthrow, there should be no force in insurrection; where one is only building, one needs only order and reason. He acknowledges that after three years of revolution it was difficult to calm the people’s movements immediately; a people coming out of slavery “is a child, and it is natural that it sometimes breaks even its own work, that it rises against the authorities created by itself.” But if these movements — “which throw society into frightful convulsions” — were prolonged too long, the people would tear itself apart, believing it was only breaking its instruments. It would soon regret the lethargic calm of its first slavery, if not given a republican calm in its place: “the people wants to be happy… happiness is not in the eternal repetition of violent convulsions; if brigands live by seditions, the people lives by repose.”

The indictment of the “anarchist party.” The body of the Adresse presents Brissot’s account of how the anarchist party — centered on the Paris Commune, Marat, Robespierre, and their allies in the Mountain — has systematically used insurrectionary pressure to dominate the Convention, prevented constitutional government from functioning, prolonged the war through military incompetence and political interference, and brought France to the brink of dissolution. He argues that every major catastrophe of the Convention period can be traced to this faction’s actions: the September Massacres, the policy toward the generals, the management of the war, the financial disorder.

The appeal to constituents and to the nation. The address closes with a direct appeal: “It is time that our fellow-citizens, that foreign peoples, judge between us and our adversaries; it is time to see which men have wanted to save the Republic, and which men have wanted to lose it. The evil is at its height; mystery would be only a dangerous palliative.” He asks his constituents to read, to judge, and to act — to use their civic authority to demand that the Convention be freed from the faction that oppresses it. ^[source: raw/brissot-a-ses-commettans-1793.txt]

Coverage Guide

  • The Girondin case against Louis XVI, August 1792 — Text I gives the fullest Girondin constitutional argument for why the king’s suspension was not a coup but a constitutionally grounded act: the evidence of betrayal from the civil list accounts, the legal analogy to the suspension power, the appeal to national sovereignty through a Convention. Cross-reference roland-memoires-vol2-1823 for a parallel Girondin reading of the same events.

  • The argument for war with Britain, January–February 1793 — Texts II and III document the diplomatic sequence from August 10 through the declaration of war: Britain’s recall of Lord Gower, the failed November negotiations, the expulsion of the French ambassador after Louis XVI’s execution, and Brissot’s case that Britain had chosen war to avenge the king and restore monarchy across Europe. For the British naval side of the war that followed, cross-reference mahan-sea-power-french-revolution-vol1.

  • Girondin self-description of the Convention’s internal conflict — Text IV is the most complete Girondin account of the Convention period before May 31, written from inside the conflict. Brissot’s three propositions (anarchist party dominates the Convention; anarchist party is sole cause of all troubles; only rigorous measures can save the Republic) are the Girondin reading of the situation that louvet-memoires-1823 describes from the fugitive’s perspective after June 2, and that roland-memoires-vol2-1823 describes from prison.

  • Definition and critique of revolutionary anarchism — Brissot’s careful definition of anarchism (laws without execution, authorities without force, crime unpunished, property attacked, individual security violated, morals corrupted) is one of the clearest contemporary definitions of what the Girondins meant by the term they applied to the Mountain.

  • The Girondin theory of post-revolutionary order — Brissot’s account of his own political principles — that once the monarchy fell, insurrection should cease and order and reason replace it; that prolonged popular convulsions destroy the revolution that produces them — is a statement of Girondin republican theory as distinct from Montagnard theory.

  • The execution of Louis XVI and its international consequences — Texts II and III track in detail how the execution transformed Britain from neutrality to belligerence, and give the Girondin argument that France was the victim of British aggression rather than its initiator.
