The extracted raw text runs to approximately 13,420 lines and 535 KB, from a New York Public Library copy. This is Volume II only — Volume I is not in the collection. Written in the Abbaye and Sainte-Pélagie prisons in 1793, before Roland’s execution by the Revolutionary Tribunal on 8 November (18 Brumaire, Year II). Published posthumously in 1823 as part of the Restoration-era recovery of Girondin testimony. The edition is annotated with notes and an appendix. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Contents at a Glance

Madame Roland (1754–1793) was the intellectual center of the Girondin circle. Her husband Jean-Marie Roland served twice as Minister of the Interior; their apartment and salon were the meeting point of the leading Girondins — Buzot, Vergniaud, Brissot, Pétion, Barbaroux — throughout 1791–1793. She was not merely a political wife: she drafted many of her husband’s ministerial letters, held her own correspondence with deputies and writers, and formed sharply independent political judgments on every figure she encountered.

Volume II of the Mémoires, written during her two imprisonments, has three distinct registers. The Notices historiques are political history from the inside: her account of the second Roland ministry (from August 10, 1792), the political atmosphere after the fall of the monarchy, and then the two arrests. The Portraits et Anecdotes are character studies — some of the finest near-contemporary pen portraits of major Revolution figures. The Derniers écrits are documents of the last weeks: her political testament, notes on the accusation against the Girondin deputies, notes on her own trial, and a projected defense she did not deliver.

The Appendice is a substantial documentary section: twelve editorial notes (A–L) reproducing primary documents — the official procès-verbal of the Champ-de-Mars massacre (17 July 1791), Roland’s ministerial letters to the King and to the National Assembly during the September Massacres, the Commune’s circular justifying those massacres, Roland’s itemized account of his propaganda fund, and Madame Roland’s defiant letter from Sainte-Pélagie to her section. This is followed by the text of the Tribunal’s judgment condemning her to death, her first interrogation record, and an extract from the Jacobin press (Feuille du Salut Public) denouncing Roland, Marie-Antoinette, and Olympe de Gouges as examples of women who had overstepped their natural role — a primary document of the Revolution’s hostility to politically active women.

The Table of Contents at the end describes the contents of Volume I (not in this collection): a biographical Notice on Roland’s life, three parts of her personal memoirs from childhood through young womanhood, letters to Bosc, and Notices historiques covering the first Roland ministry.

Suite des Notices Historiques — Second Ministère de Roland

Roland opens in medias res: she already knows the revolution of August 10 from what the public knows, because her husband’s ministerial activity did not include “petites manœuvres.” She begins with the reconstituted council and the critical problem at its center: the inclusion of Danton.

Danton. The Notices historiques contain the most extended and specific treatment of Danton in the volume — far more developed than any of the named Portraits, because Roland knew him directly. She had warned friends that “Placer Danton, c’était inoculer dans le gouvernement” those men who torment a government when excluded and degrade it once they participate in it. No one with Mirabeau’s authority existed to make this case publicly. Danton came frequently to the Roland apartment during the second ministry, arriving a little before council time, staying after with Fabre d’Églantine — showing every sign of zeal and desire to work with his colleagues. Roland watched him: “I looked at this repulsive and atrocious face; and although I told myself not to judge anyone on appearances… I could not apply the idea of an honest man to that face. I have never seen anything that so perfectly characterized the violence of brutal passions and the most astonishing boldness, half-veiled by an air of great joviality, the affectation of frankness and a kind of good nature.” Her imagination placed him “a dagger in hand, inciting with voice and gesture a troop of assassins more timid or less ferocious than himself — or content with his crimes, indicating by the gesture that characterizes Sardanapalus, his habits and inclinations. I defy an experienced painter not to find in Danton’s person all the desirable properties for such a composition.”

She then gives a biographical sketch of Danton from the beginning of the Revolution: “miserable lawyer, burdened with debts more than cases, whose wife said that without a louis a week from her father she could not maintain her household” — rising through his district section by force of his lungs, a great partisan of the Orléanists, acquiring an ease of living without visible work, becoming substitute prosecutor of the Commune, consolidating his influence at the Jacobins on the ruins of the Lameths. After August 10 he entered the Ministry of Justice “as a tribune agreeable to the people, to whom it was necessary to give the satisfaction of putting him in the government.”

Her account of his conduct during the September Massacres is the sharpest passage. While the prisons were being attacked, Danton was at the Commune’s surveillance committee, from which the arrest orders were flowing — and had just embraced Marat there “after the parade of a feigned twenty-four-hour quarrel.” He went up to Pétion, told him that the committee had issued an arrest warrant against Roland, said he had taken it — and when Pétion looked at it and said “leave it, it will have a good effect,” Danton replied that he would not allow it and went to bring them to reason. The warrant was not executed. Roland’s reading: “Who does not see, in Danton’s move toward the mayor, that of a conspirator who wants to gauge the effect of the blow, or to take credit for having parried it, when it turns out to have failed or been delayed by involuntary circumstances?” Her most damning line, from a footnote she added: at the end of that night’s council, a citizen came to Danton and recounted his attempts to protect prisoners from the massacres then underway. Danton, irritated by the report, cried out in his bellowing voice — “in the presence of twenty people who shuddered” — “Je me f… bien des prisonniers! qu’ils deviennent ce qu’ils pourront!” (“I don’t give a damn about the prisoners! let them become whatever they will!”) and passed on with ill humor. The footnote ends: “Danton enjoys his crimes, having successively attained the various degrees of influence, and persecuted, proscribed the probity that declared war on him, the merit whose ascendancy he feared; he reigns.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Marat. Roland never met Marat directly — she asked Danton to bring him; he deflected, calling the visit useless and disagreeable since Marat “would respond to nothing.” She had sometimes doubted Marat was a real being; Danton’s evasion convinced her he was. The most vivid passage about him comes through an anecdote told by a Toulouse woman detained at Sainte-Pélagie, whose husband was a judge and whose cousin was imprisoned. She went to Marat to beg for her cousin. He received her himself when he heard a woman’s voice. His appearance: boots without stockings, old leather breeches, white taffeta waistcoat, filthy open shirt exposing a yellowing chest, long dirty nails. His salon: blue and white damask furniture, silk curtains in elegant draperies, a brilliant chandelier, superb porcelain vases full of natural flowers — then rare and expensive. He led her to a voluptuous ottoman, listened to her, kissed her hand, squeezed her knees, promised to free her cousin. “I would have let him do all of it,” said the little woman with her Toulouse accent, “quitte à aller me baigner après, provided he gave me back my cousin.” He kept his promise — and within twenty-four hours wrote to the husband demanding a reciprocal service. Roland uses this to establish Marat as not a disorganized lunatic but a deliberate political operator who extracted personal favors through fear and calculation. She also traces the pattern by which Danton used Marat as his instrument against the Roland ministry: the funds for Marat’s manuscripts were routed through Danton; every time Marat’s Ami du peuple attacked Roland or his wife directly, she recognized “Danton pure — he wants to attack you, he begins by prowling around you.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Robespierre. Less developed than Danton or Marat in the Notices, but present at key moments. The most quoted line occurs in a footnote to her account of the Convention’s late period: “there were only a small number of unyielding men who still dared to combat for principles, and toward the end, it was reduced almost to Buzot and to Robespierre; the latter, she adds, seemed to me then an honest man.” From her second prison at Sainte-Pélagie, she observes the factional struggle beginning to devour the Montagnards themselves: “Robespierre jealous rises against him [Danton]; the Cordeliers and Jacobins are ready to divide. Grand spectacle preparing for us victims: the tigers are going to tear each other apart; they will perhaps forget us, unless the fury of their last moments carries them to exterminate everything before their own defeat.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

The Paris Commune. The Commune of August 10 — the insurrectional body that replaced the legal Commune and then became the Terror’s institutional base in Paris — runs through the Notices historiques as a constant threat. Roland describes it as “composed in large part of those men who, having nothing to lose, have everything to gain in revolutions; this Commune, already guilty of a thousand excesses, had need to commit new ones, for it is by the accumulation of crimes that impunity is assured.” Her account of the September Massacres centers on the Commune’s choreography: the black flag raised on Notre-Dame, the alarm cannon fired, citizens called to the Champ-de-Mars while simultaneously the barriers are closed — “and nobody is struck by these contradictory dispositions.” Roland’s ministry wrote urgent letters to the Commune through Pétion, formally requisitioned the commander-general to guard the prisons, had the requisition printed and posted at street corners. None of it was heeded. She describes herself receiving two hundred men who arrived at the Interior Ministry on the evening of September 2 demanding arms — Roland was out; she received ten of them, asked them calmly what they wanted, engaged with their requests — while simultaneously understanding that the arrests in the prisons were already underway and that the Commune and the commander-general were complicit in not preventing them. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Roland’s resignation and the fall of the second ministry. Before the Première Détention section properly begins, the Notices cover the deterioration of the second ministry from January to May 1793. Roland resigned in January: the council’s weakness had become so pronounced that he could not even get his dissenting opinions recorded in the council minutes. The immediate occasion was a decree about a play (L’Ami des Lois) whose second part he found ridiculous — he refused to sign it, and from January 15 onward signed no more council deliberations. Garat succeeded him at the Interior — “amiable man of society, mediocre man of letters, detestable administrator,” who “had not even the intelligence to stay in the department where there is least to do.” The consequence was visible within weeks: the departments grew agitated, scarcity spread, the Vendée war sharpened, the Paris authorities anticipated the council, the Jacobins took the reins of government. Roland had already published accounts of his ministry that no minister before him had supplied — detailed, documented, inviting examination. He wrote to the Convention seven times in four months asking for a report on his administration. The Convention never responded: to examine and vindicate his accounts would have been to expose the slanders against him, the infamy of his detractors, and the Convention’s own cowardice in failing to defend him.

May 31 — the coup and Roland’s flight. The Notices cover the evening of May 31 in precise detail. Roland and his wife were at home. She was preparing to leave Paris with their daughter — her passeports had been delayed by “zealous Maratists” in her section who considered her suspect, and she had just received them when a colic attack put her to bed for six days. She was to leave the next Friday. On May 31, the tocsin sounded. At five-thirty in the evening, six armed men arrived with a warrant from the revolutionary committee to arrest Roland. He refused to recognize the authority: “I know no law that constitutes the authority you cite, and I will not comply with orders emanating from it; if you employ violence, I can only oppose you with the resistance of a man of my age; but I will protest against it to the last instant.” The officer left to report his response, leaving his colleagues at the door. Roland immediately understood he needed to denounce this publicly. She had a letter written to the Convention president, called a cab, and went alone. The Tuileries courtyard was full of armed men. She passed through them “jumping like a bird.” At the Convention doors, closed and guarded, she used a trick: adopting the tone “some devout of Robespierre” might use — “In this day of salvation for the country, among the traitors we must fear, you don’t know how important the notes I need to pass to the president may be? Fetch me an usher to entrust them to.” The door opened. Inside the petitioners’ hall she waited an hour for Vergniaud, who finally came. He told her plainly: “In the state the Assembly is in, I cannot flatter you, and you can barely hope; the Convention can no longer do anything.” During her wait she heard the chaos: pétitionnaires at the bar demanding the arrest of the twenty-two, Rabaud being helped out unseen, deputies fleeing by various exits. She apostrophizes the “good Parisians who don’t want another September 2” — “You surround the legislative body with your bayonets and let them dictate whatever decrees they want made… don’t come and glory in defending it. You who chain it, you who deliver to oppression its members most distinguished by their virtues and their talents.” Roland, meanwhile, had left for a friend’s house and did not return. He remained in hiding in Paris and then fled to Rouen, where he died on learning of his wife’s execution — by his own hand, a sword-cane found beside him in a road. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Suite des Notices — Première Détention (First Imprisonment)

Roland was arrested on June 1, 1793 — not May 31 when the coup happened, but the following day on a separate warrant — and taken to the Abbaye prison. This section is titled with an epigraph: “Today on the throne, tomorrow in chains: that is the fate of virtue in times of revolution.”

She narrates her own arrest with precision. She had stayed the night of May 31, expecting nothing personal against herself. The next morning commissioners came with a warrant. She examined it, found it defective in form, and refused to accompany them until they produced a valid order. They returned with a proper warrant; she then went without protest, making no scene, taking what she needed quietly. She was conducted to the Abbaye — and notes the irony that the arrest order bore, among other signatures, that of Dorat-Cubières, the verse-writing “bel-esprit” who had tried to ingratiate himself with her through flattery when Roland was minister.

A footnote by the editor Champagneux, inserted here, notes that her stated reason for wanting to leave Paris (concern for Roland, domestic affairs) was “not my strongest reason.” The footnote then adds: “I know the motive the citoyenne Roland means: she had confided it to me; but the time to publish it has not yet come. Malevolence would seize on it… this century is too corrupt to believe in the efforts of virtue of which the citoyenne Roland then gave proof, all the more worthy of admiration in that they had no publicity and were concentrated entirely within the interior of her household.” The allusion, withheld in 1823, concerns her relationship with Buzot — a love she acknowledged to trusted friends but did not publicly confess.

The section covers her Abbaye imprisonment: the letters she wrote to the Committee of General Security asking to know on what grounds she was held (“I have no offenses to reproach myself; those who speak worst of me have never seen me… I am a mother, this title imposed duties I cherish and cannot fulfil; I am a wife, and I do not know if I will ever again be given to soften the sorrows of the respectable man to whom I had united my destiny”); her ambivalent view of the Caen and Bordeaux federalist resistance; her reflection on what it means to have acted politically as a woman. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Suite des Notices — Seconde Arrestation (Second Arrest)

After a brief release, Roland was re-arrested and transferred to Sainte-Pélagie. This section covers the second and final imprisonment through the weeks before trial. It is the most compressed of the three Notices sections, written under increasing time pressure as she knew the trial was approaching. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Portraits et Anecdotes

The most mined section of the volume: pen portraits of fifteen individuals in Roland’s circle, followed by anecdotes. Written in August 1793 as replacement material for the political notices destroyed by the Abbaye authorities. Roland notes she completed roughly 300 pages in twenty-two days in her moments of mental liberty. These are not biographical sketches but moral and political character studies, written by someone who knew the subjects closely, observed them under pressure, and had nothing left to lose. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Buzot — François-Nicolas-Léonard Buzot, Girondin deputy from the Eure, is the longest and most personal portrait. Roland describes him as having elevated character, proud spirit, “boiling courage,” sensitive, ardent, melancholic, and lazy — prone to extremes. A passionate contemplator of nature, nourishing his imagination with all its charms, he “seems made for domestic happiness.” In public life he knows only the rules of austere equity, pursues injustice with heat, and can never compose with crime. His reserve and energetic freedom of expression made him accused of arrogance; “mediocrity does not readily forgive merit, but vice hates and pursues the courageous virtue that declares war on it.” He is, she writes, the gentlest man in the world with his friends and the most formidable adversary of scoundrels.

His specific parliamentary works are praised in detail: his report on the departmental guard (“containing reasons to which no answer was ever given”), his law against inciters to murder (“the soundest policy, and that philosophy, true as nature, strong as the reason on which it rests”), his motion for the banishment of the Bourbons (“precise, well-motivated, graceful and warm”), his opinion on the trial of the king (“rich in substance and reasons, with none of the pathos and digressions to which so many harangues gave rise”), and his letters to his constituents of January 6 and 22 (“they paint his soul with a truth that will make them sought after”). She enumerates the charges against him: accused of royalism because he claimed morals were necessary in a republic; of calumniating Paris because he abhorred the September massacres; of aristocracy because he wanted the people called to sovereignty in judging Louis XVI; of federalism because he demanded equality between all departments against the Commune’s tyranny. His crime was also sartorial: while the dregs of the nation affected to prove their patriotism by dressing in coarse clothes and drinking coarsely, “Buzot professed the morals of Socrates and preserved the politeness of Scipio.” For which he was declared a traitor to the country, his house razed, his goods confiscated — “as once they banished Aristides and condemned Phocion.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Pétion — Jérôme Pétion de Villeneuve, former mayor of Paris, is rendered with genuine warmth but also clear-eyed limitation. “A true honest man and a good man, incapable of doing the least thing that wounds probity.” The serenity of a good conscience, the mildness of an easy character, frankness and gaiety distinguish his face. He was a prudent mayor and a faithful representative — but too trusting and too peaceful to foresee storms and avert them. “Sound judgment, pure intentions, that which one calls precision of mind” mark his opinions and writing, “marked by the stamp of good sense more than by talent.” He is a cold orator and slack in his prose style. “Made to practice the virtues in a republic, and not to found such a government among a corrupt people who regarded him for a time as their idol, and rejoiced at his proscription as at an enemy’s.”

Two anecdotes give him life. First, during the Constituent Assembly — when the court was painting the Girondins as dangerous intriguers — Buzot brought Pétion to dine late one night. After the meal Pétion sat on a wide ottoman, began playing with a young hunting dog “with the abandon of a child,” and both fell asleep together “lying one on top of the other” while the conversation of four people did not prevent him from snoring. “See this factious man,” Buzot said laughing — “those who accuse us imagine we are scheming.” Second, and historically significant: Lacroix (Danton’s future partner) offered Pétion three million livres from the court if he would use his influence to support Louis XVI. Roland also records that when Louis XVI summoned Pétion privately to the Tuileries — alone in his cabinet, without the usual entourage — and showered him with affability and “those small agreeable cajoleries he knew how to distribute at will,” Pétion noticed the faint rustle of silk behind the tapestry and concluded the queen was present unseen. He remained firm. “And it is Lacroix, who had served him, and probably been paid for it, who did not find they could send the king to death fast enough.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Pache — Jean-Nicolas Pache, Roland’s own recommended successor as Interior Minister and then War Minister, eventually mayor of Paris — the portrait is a sustained anatomization of betrayal. “In politics, the Tartuffe of Molière.”

Roland describes how they came to know Pache through a mutual friend who vouched for him as a man of patriarchal virtue who had voluntarily left a comfortable government position to live simply in Switzerland. When Roland needed a trusted presence in his cabinet during the first ministry, Pache was proposed. He arrived every morning at seven with a piece of bread in his pocket and stayed until three, refusing all pay and title, “attentive, prudent, zealous… placing a word, softening Roland when irritated by the contradictions of his aristocratic clerks.” Roland treated him as a precious friend. “And I, touched by the usefulness I believed he was to my husband, lavished on him demonstrations of esteem and attachment.” It was Roland who recommended Pache to Servan for the War Ministry when Servan asked for him, and Roland who later recommended him as his own successor at the Interior.

The unmasking is detailed and methodical. Pache at the War Ministry began receiving Fabre, Chabot, and other Montagnards while refusing Roland’s dinner invitations on the pretext of overwork. His chief clerks Hassenfratz and Vincent denounced Roland at the Jacobins as an enemy of the people. Roland wrote Pache on November 11 warning him of the murmurs against him — no reply ever came. As War Minister: 130 million in accounts impossible to render; complements paid as full-strength for under-strength regiments. In the twenty-four hours after he was forced to resign, he made appointments to sixty positions — “from his son-in-law, formerly a vicar, made an ordonnateur at 19,000 livres, to his hairdresser, a scoundrel of nineteen, made a war commissary.” Called then to the Paris Mairie, where “supported by the Chaumettes, Héberts, and other scoundrels, he favored the oppression of the legislative body, the violation of national representation, the proscription of all the virtuous men, and secured the ruin of his country.”

Roland appends the Biron story: Biron came with documentary proof of Pache’s malversations, was charmed into believing Pache was merely incompetent rather than malicious, handed over his evidence to Pache directly — and then found himself dispatched to the Italian army, denied supplies, ignored when he reported victories, ordered back to Paris, and imprisoned at Sainte-Pélagie, where Roland learned all this. “He recognized in this blow the hand of Pache and the tyrant who oppresses him.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Guadet et Gensonné — Marguerite-Élie Guadet and Armand Gensonné are presented as a complementary pair who “perhaps love each other because they do not resemble each other.” Guadet is impetuous; Gensonné is cold. “The second has made himself a logician; he often loses, by deliberating, the time that should be used to act.” Guadet “dissipates in happy but passing and short bursts a heat that should sometimes be concentrated and always more sustained to produce lasting effect.” Guadet “had brilliant moments in both Assemblies, due to the empire of honesty seconded by talent; too sensitive to fight long without fatigue, he earned the hatred of the wicked without being truly formidable to them.” Gensonné “useful in discussion, which he has the fault of extending too much, worked in committees and drafted part of the proposed constitutional plan.” His speech in the king’s affair “elevated by traits of that sarcasm which an apparent coolness sharpens — that the Mountain’s children will never forgive him.” Both were “tender husbands, good fathers, excellent citizens, virtuous men, sincere republicans” who “succumbed under the accusation of conspirators only for having failed to coalesce themselves in favor of the good cause, the only one for which they fought and deserved to exist.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Vergniaud — Pierre-Victurnien Vergniaud receives Roland’s most double-edged assessment. “He was perhaps the most eloquent orator in the Assembly.” He did not improvise like Guadet, but his prepared speeches — “strong with logic, burning with warmth, full of substance, sparkling with beauties, supported by a very noble delivery” — one still read with great pleasure. Then the turn: “However, I do not love Vergniaud; I find in him the egoism of philosophy. Disdaining men, certainly because he knows them well, he does not trouble himself for them.” The moral consequence she draws is strict: “If you disdain men, you must remain a private idle person; otherwise laziness is a crime, and Vergniaud is greatly guilty in this regard. What a pity that a talent such as his was not employed with the ardor of a soul devoured by the love of the public good and the tenacity of a laborious spirit!” Roland admires the instrument and indicts the will behind it. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Grangeneuve — Jean-Antoine Lafargue de Grangeneuve is introduced as “the best human being one could find under a face of lesser appearance; ordinary mind, but truly great soul — he does beautiful things with simplicity, without suspecting all that they would cost others.” The whole portrait is built around a single extraordinary anecdote. In July 1792, Chabot was arguing in a small gathering that it would be useful if the court assassinated some patriot deputies — it would trigger a popular insurrection, he said, the only way to produce a salutary crisis. He warmed to this theme at length. Grangeneuve listened without a word. At the first opportunity he took Chabot aside: “I was struck by your reasons; they are excellent. But the court is too clever ever to furnish us such an expedient. We must supply it ourselves. Find men for the deed — I volunteer as the victim.” Chabot was so moved he said he would share the glory. They planned the hour and the street. Grangeneuve made his will, settled his domestic affairs without show of emotion, and did not fail the appointment. Chabot did not appear. Grangeneuve walked the appointed street twice at a slow pace, met no one, “and was obliged to return home safe and sound, unhappy at the uselessness of his preparation.” Chabot excused himself with “miserable pretexts — not belying the cowardice of a priest nor the hypocrisy of a Capuchin.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Barbaroux — Charles-Jean-Marie Barbaroux, Marseillais deputy: “painters would not disdain to take his features for a head of Antinous.” Active, hardworking, frank, brave, with the vivacity of a young Marseillais — “one of those subjects that a great politician would want to attach to himself, who should have flourished with brilliance in a happy republic.” But Roland notes the danger beside the strength: love of pleasure is adjacent to the love of glory, and “if it takes the place of glory after the disappointment of obstacles or the disgust of setbacks, it would crush an excellent temper and make it betray its noble destination.”

Roland describes the letters Barbaroux wrote during the first ministry, advising Roland to be less administratively austere with the Bouches-du-Rhône administrators who had irritated him — “dictated by the best spirit and consummate prudence”: they worked, Roland relaxed his tone and won back the Marseillais. She describes the discussions in July 1792 about a possible southern republic — spreading maps, tracing lines of demarcation, Servan studying military positions — with Barbaroux saying, “That will be our last resort, but I hope the Marseillais here will spare us from needing it.” His speechmaking she praises for logic and administrative knowledge of commerce; his written discourse on subsistences is “after Creuzé-la-Touche’s work, the best in the genre”; but he still had work to do to become an orator. She pairs him with Buzot as Nisus and Euryalus: “may they have a better fate than those two friends.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Louvet — Jean-Baptiste Louvet de Couvray (whose own Mémoires are a separate source in this wiki) is described as “small, slight, poor-sighted, with a neglected coat” — he “appears nothing to the vulgar who do not remark the nobility of his brow, and the fire that animates his eyes and face at the expression of a great truth, a fine sentiment, an ingenious sally, or a fine pleasantry.” Like Philopoemen (the Achaean general who struck people as unremarkable until they saw him act), he paid for his appearance. Known already for his Faublas novels — “where the graces of imagination ally with lightness of style, the tone of philosophy, the salt of criticism” — he had proven that “his skillful hand could alternately shake the bells of folly, hold the burin of history, and launch the thunderbolts of eloquence.”

Roland praises his specific political writings: the Catilinaire contre Robespierre (the October 1792 accusation speech — “deserved to be pronounced in a senate that had the strength to render justice”), his Conspiration du 10 mars, and his Sentinelle — “a model of that genre of poster and daily instruction destined for a people one wants to enlighten about facts, without ever influencing them except through reason, nor moving them except for the good of all.” The Sentinelle is placed as the antithesis of Marat’s press: “these atrocious and revolting sheets, whose coarse style and filthy expressions correspond to the bloodthirsty doctrine and impure lies of which they are the sewer.” Her summary: “Impossible to unite more wit with less pretension and more good nature; courageous as a lion, simple as a child, sensitive man, good citizen, vigorous writer — he can make Catilina tremble at the tribune, dine with the Graces, and sup with Bachaumont.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Lazowski — Félix Lazowski, Polish-born inspector of manufactures under the ancien régime, is a portrait in metamorphosis. Roland had encountered him in the old bureaucratic world: “elegant, well-coiffed, careful in dress, rounding his shoulders a little, walking on his heels, making a frill” — affecting that small air of importance “which fools then took for a mark of consideration and which sensible people mocked.” When the Revolution stripped him of his place and his patron the duc de Liancourt, “not daring to hope for a pension, he found himself without a sou, became a patriot, let his hair grow greasy, shouted in his section, and became a sans-culotte since he was threatened with lacking one.”

His transformation was rapid: captain of a National Guard quarter, active at August 10, then — and here Roland’s tone hardens — “his exploits date from September 2,” the September Massacres, specifically “the activity he maintained in the massacre of the priests at Saint-Firmin.” When she saw him at the Interior Ministry: “The pretty man with little grimaces had taken on the brutal air of an enraged patriot, the face flushed with a drinker, and the haggard eye of an assassin.” He died of an inflammatory fever before this was written — “the fruit of debauchery, late nights, and eau-de-vie.” The portrait ends with savage irony at his elaborate state funeral: Robespierre’s “touching jeremiads and pompous eulogy of the great ignored man”; the Commune’s spectacular funeral arrangements; his child adopted by “papa Pache” at the Hôtel Commun; burial near the liberty tree at the Place du Carrousel. “A people who took Marat for their prophet and Danton for their lord could well honor Lazowski as a saint or a hero — they are the same thing in the religion of the Septembristes.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Robert — François Robert, journalist and deputy, husband of Louise Kéralio (herself a journalist and co-editor of the Mercure national). Roland saw him briefly before the Revolution: “a fat husband with the face of a canon — broad, bright with health and self-satisfaction, with that freshness never altered by profound combinations.” The portrait is built around an escalating series of encounters that ends in open enmity and a revealing tableau of the revolutionary patronage economy.

Madame Robert and her husband showed up at Roland’s hotel room at eleven at night on July 17, 1791, fleeing the Champ-de-Mars massacre (Robert had been at the altar when the firing began). Roland sheltered them, fabricating a story for the concierge. Next morning, instead of writing urgent dispatches, the Roberts sat on the balcony calling acquaintances up from the street — including a man who boasted of having run his sword through a National Guardsman — and received him loudly in the next room. Roland made them dismiss him and asked them to be more discreet. They came back for lunch dressed for a ball: “The wife had tall plumes and much rouge; the husband had put on a sky-blue silk coat, on which his black hair, falling in thick curls, contrasted strikingly. A long sword at his side added all that could make him remarkable.” Roland stared at them, wondering if they had lost their minds; they ate enormously and chattered pleasantly and left.

The denouement came when Roland was called to the ministry. Madame Robert appeared within twenty-four hours to ask for a position for her husband. What position? The ambassadorship to Constantinople — which Dumouriez had already been asked for and refused (“I’m not employing a madman like that… if he presents himself again I’ll have him rolled to the street”). After this failure Robert joined the Cordeliers, attached himself to Danton, was pushed into the Convention by Danton’s influence at the elections, “paid his debts, entertained lavishly, received Orléans and a thousand others to dinner, is rich today, slanders Roland, and tears his wife to shreds — all understandable; he does his trade and earns his money.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Chamfort et Carra — A paired portrait of Sébastien-Roch Nicolas Chamfort, the famous aphorist, and Jean-Louis Carra, journalist and deputy, both placed by Roland’s husband as co-directors of the Bibliothèque nationale.

Chamfort: “knowing court and city, intrigues and characters, politics and his species, better than his century itself.” He “shared the extreme confidence I have always reproached in philosophers who acted in the new order of things; he could not believe in the ascendancy of a few bad heads.” He told Roland she exaggerated: “placed at the center of the movement, you think it has a great extent of action… these people destroy themselves through their own excesses; they will not set back the lights of eighteen centuries.” — “And yet these people are masters, and Chamfort is today a prisoner.”

Roland’s character assessment of Chamfort is the most quoted passage in the portrait. Someone asked her whether Chamfort was truly a patriot. “Let us understand each other. Chamfort sees and judges well… recognizes and venerates the principles of public liberty and the happiness of men, and would not betray them. But would he sacrifice for their triumph his repose, his tastes, his life? That is another question: then I believe he would calculate.” — “Then he is not a virtuous man.” — “But he is virtuous as Ninon was honest; and in the corruption gnawing at us, you would be very happy to have many such virtues.”

Carra she describes as “a very good man with a very bad head — one is not more enthusiastic for revolution, republic, and liberty; but one does not judge men and things worse.” He arranged facts and interests in his head as suited French success, “seeing everything rosy, dreaming of his country’s happiness and the liberation of all Europe with inexpressible complacency.” His Annales “succeeded marvellously with the people by a certain prophetic tone, always imposing on the vulgar.” Seeing him now on trial as a traitor to the republic, Roland observes: “one is tempted to ask if Robespierre is working for Austria? But it is quite clear he is working for himself; and in his devouring ambition to pass as the unique liberator of France, he wishes to annihilate all those who, in some way, served their country with a kind of brilliance.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Chénier — Marie-Joseph Chénier, playwright and deputy (brother of the poet André Chénier, who died on the scaffold). Roland knew his verse as “rather harsh” and his play Charles IX as “weak by character, bad by style, good by intention.” Roland charged him with drafting a council proclamation — he brought it and read it aloud: “a true amplification of rhetoric, declaimed with the affectation of a schoolboy with a Stentor’s voice. It gave me his measure. One can write verse and carry to another kind of work the precision of a good mind; but Chénier still wanted to be a poet in writing prose and politics.” Her verdict: “a man misplaced, who would be useful in the Convention only for planning national festivals — and even then I would fear the reports would not be deeply calculated for the effect to be produced on character and morals.” He “faded or drowned in the Plain” — the undecided center between Gironde and Mountain. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Dusaulx — Jean-Charles Dusaulx, the veteran translator of Juvenal, who had been present at the storming of the Bastille. “Venerable for his age and morals, he spoke like Nestor — and what is worse, like a man of letters, that is, far too much in society.” But his stories always bore “the mark of his excellent heart and precise mind. Upright and sensitive, warm friend of truth, he needed only ten or fifteen fewer years to be one of the Convention’s most daring defenders.” A short portrait — affectionate, a little melancholy in its precision about the limits imposed by age. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Mercier — Louis-Sébastien Mercier, author of the Tableau de Paris and L’An 2440, “a new proof that the talent for writing is but a small merit for a legislator. Easy, agreeable in the commerce of life, more than the common run of men of letters, he is but a zero in the Convention.” Roland’s explanation is her most general political aphorism: “It is character that constitutes what one can call a man; it is character that directs revolutions, influences assemblies, and governs the crowd. Wit is less than nothing by comparison.” Followed by its devastating application: “Despotism, in its long proscription, had left us little but wit; that is why our revolution goes as it pleases God — or the devil.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Dorat-Cubières — Michel de Cubières, called Dorat-Cubières, poet and deputy, is the last portrait and one of the most pointed. Roland had seen his name so often in the Almanach des Muses and similar collections that she laughed when she found it coupled with the title of secrétaire-greffier of the municipality: “it resembles an incongruity; and it truly is one.” Cubières “preaches sans-culottisme as he once sang the Graces; writes verses to Marat as he wrote them to Iris; and bloodthirsty without fury, as he was apparently amorous without tenderness, he prostrates himself humbly before the idol of the day, whether Tantalus or Venus. What does it matter? Provided he grovels and earns his bread — yesterday by writing a quatrain, today by copying a procès-verbal or signing a police order.”

He had visited her twice during the ministry, presenting himself as a man of wit; she found him “singular at the first, insupportable at the second; flattering courtier, insipid complimenter, foolishly presumptuous and basely polite.” She deployed the polite coldness that signals to unwanted guests what they should do; he understood and left. He then wrote to ask permission to introduce a prince who wished to be admitted to her circle — “he dwelt on this title of prince with an emphasis quite risible, adding the most disgusting flatteries about my person.” She answered as she knew how to: politely declining without giving him grounds to complain. He replied with lengthy apologies, asking for a single instant to explain himself “at my feet.” She did not answer. She thought nothing more of him until the day of her arrest — when she saw his signature on the warrant from the commune, alongside the warrant from the insurrectionary committee. “Both were shown to me, out of fear that I would contest the committee’s; and yet it was the latter alone that my guards invoked before the concierge of the Abbaye, where they conducted me.” She later learned that the prince he had wanted to introduce was pursuing the ministry for some indemnity of possessions in Alsace — “I judged I had guessed well, and that one had only sought to see me in the idea that it might be as in the ancien régime, where women were engaged to solicit their husbands.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Derniers Écrits

Written in the final weeks at Sainte-Pélagie, these four texts are Roland’s political and legal testament.

Mes dernières pensées. Opens with Shakespeare’s Hamlet: “To be or not to be: it is the question. It will soon be resolved for me.” The text is addressed to “Jany” — her friend Bosc d’Antic — asking him to communicate her last thoughts to the people who will raise her daughter if the copy she intends for them fails to arrive. She writes three apologies in sequence: to Roland (“Forgive me, respectable man, for disposing of a life I had consecrated to you; your misfortunes would have kept me attached to it, had I been permitted to ease them; the faculty is taken from me forever, and you lose only a shadow, useless object of lacerating anxieties”); to her daughter (“Forgive me, dear child, young and tender daughter whose sweet image penetrates my maternal heart, astonishes my resolutions… my example will remain for you; and I feel, I can say to myself at the very gates of the tomb, that it is a rich inheritance”); to her friends, asking them to care for her “orphaned one.”

She then states directly why she will not wait for the Revolutionary Tribunal’s procedures: “this vain formula of judgment is only an insulting apparatus from which, for the victims, they take care to remove the faculty of expressing oneself.” She cites Gorsas — condemned, about to die, forbidden to speak — as proof. Waiting for the Tribunal means breathing “at their good pleasure until it pleases them to make me appear in turn on the stage, to decide finally the disappearance of a redoutable witness of their scoundrelism. Yes, redoutable — because my eyes have long since penetrated them, my soul vomits them, and my courage has defied them.” She addresses those who say perhaps the Montagnards will spare her: “I would not love better to owe my safety to the Austrians than to receive death from the French who reign today; they are equally enemies of my country.” Her harshest words are for the twenty-two Girondin deputies who did not act decisively on June 2: “They temporized with crime, the cowards! They had to fall in their turn; but they succumb shamefully without being pitied by anyone… rather than obeying their tyrants, descending to their bar, leaving the Assembly like a timid flock the butcher has just branded — why did they not do themselves justice by falling on the monsters to annihilate them, rather than receive their verdict from them?” The text closes with a religious invocation: “Divinity, Supreme Being, soul of the world, principle of what I feel of the great, the good, and the happy, you whose existence I believe because I must emanate from something better than what I see — I am going to reunite with your essence.” ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Observations rapides sur l’acte d’accusation contre les députés, par Amar (October 25, 1793). Roland refutes Amar’s indictment of the proscribed Girondins by tracing the accused deputies through their pre-revolutionary and revolutionary lives, asking at each point where the conspiracy was. She takes four figures in sequence and sketches them with the same precision as the Portraits: Pétion — “simple in his habits, modest in his needs, married to a sensible woman, living at Chartres, esteemed by his fellow-citizens who had seen him born”; Buzot — “distinguished at Évreux by a severe probity and a premature prudence… the warmth of feeling, the facility of expression, the austerity of principles made him judged worthy of carrying to the States the complaints and demands of his country”; Gorsas — a journalist who from the first days of the Revolution attacked the still-powerful court and devoted himself to the defense of the people’s rights; Brissot — “preached liberty under despotism, humanity under tyranny, called the Revolution by his vows, and prepared its movements by reclamations against the abuses of the day… suffered captivity as the punishment of his frankness.” She then asks of each: did they seek power? did they make profit? is there a single circumstance in which they showed themselves contrary to their principles? Her argument is that the conspiracy the indictment invokes cannot have been made by these people, and that what has actually been defeated is the Republican defense of order and legality against the anarchist faction. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Notes sur son procès et l’interrogatoire qui l’a commencé — Her notes on the interrogation that opened her own trial, recording the questions put and her answers, and her assessment of the procedure’s character as a political spectacle rather than a legal proceeding.

Projet de défense au tribunal — The defense she drafted in full, covering every charge against her, which she was not permitted to deliver at her trial. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Appendice aux Notices Historiques

The Appendice is divided into two parts: twelve documentary notes (A–L) added by the 1823 editors, cited to specific pages of Volumes I and II, followed by the judgment and the Jacobin press extract. The notes contain primary documents from Roland’s ministerial correspondence and the Commune’s records — many of them reproduced here for the first time in the 1823 edition, and together they constitute a documentary appendix on the September Massacres, the Champ-de-Mars massacre, Roland’s financial administration, and the Roland family’s legal defense.

NOTE A (pag. 411, tom. 1º) — Procès-verbal des événements du 17 juillet 1791, drawn up by the municipality of Paris. The official municipal record of the Champ-de-Mars massacre: the municipal council’s arrêtés on 17 July (banning all assemblies, ordering the National Guard to disperse them), the sequence of events (the murder of two individuals whose heads were carried on pikes, the proclamation of martial law, the red flag unfurled), the municipal officers’ march to the Champ-de-Mars preceded by infantry, cannon, and the red flag, the crowd’s cries of “Down with the red flag! Down with bayonets!”, the stone-throwing, a pistol shot striking a dragoon, the National Guard firing into the air at first but then at the crowd when martial law summons proved impossible to execute. The final count: eleven or twelve dead, ten or twelve wounded, several National Guard officers stoned, two guardsmen assassinated separately, five or six arrested. The Assembly ordered the procès-verbal printed and approved the municipality’s conduct.

NOTE B (pag. 430, tom. 1º) — Letter from Roland and his fellow ministers to the King, 19 May 1792 (Year IV of Liberty). A formal ministerial address warning Louis XVI that the people cannot see the king’s constitutional sincerity when his household employs non-juring clergy and when counter-revolutionary talk circulates in his entourage. The ministers write that their duty is to tell the truth to the throne, that they serve a constitutional king of a free people, but that the king’s own language — however well-intentioned — is not heard by citizens who see only the facts: his almoner has not taken the civic oath, non-juring priests take encouragement from this, the public’s confidence is being eroded.

NOTE C (pag. 434, tom. 1er) — Content from the raw text not fully extracted — the original footnote reference to page 434 of Volume I.

NOTE D (pag. 435, tom. 1º) — Content from the raw text not fully extracted — the original footnote reference to page 435 of Volume I.

NOTE E (pag. 444, tom. 1ª) — Content from the raw text not fully extracted — the original footnote reference to page 444 of Volume I.

NOTE F (pag. 17, tom. II) — Content from the raw text not fully extracted — the original footnote reference to page 17 of Volume II.

NOTE G (pag. 33, tom. 11) — Arrêté of the Conseil-général de la Commune, 2 September 1792, signed Huguenin (president) and Tallien (secretary-greffier). Published in the Moniteur of 3 September. The arrêté ordered the disarmament of suspects, the dispatch of twenty-four commissioners to the armies, the permanent session of the military committee — and was, as the 1823 editor notes, “the signal for the massacres.” The Commune cites the “frightful conspiracy” by the court to slaughter patriots and claims the people’s act of putting conspirators in prison to death was “indispensable to hold back by terror the legions of traitors locked within our walls at the moment we were to march against the enemy.”

NOTE H (pag. 34, tom. 11) — Letter from Roland, Minister of the Interior, to Santerre, 4 September 1792. Roland writes: “In the name of the Nation, and by order of the National Assembly and the executive power, I enjoin you, Monsieur, to employ all the forces the law puts in your hands to prevent the violation of persons’ and property’s safety; and I place on your responsibility any attack committed on any citizen whatsoever in the city of Paris.” Santerre’s answer: he had already given the most precise orders for patrols after news came that the people were at the prisons, and pledges that his own body “will serve as a shield to the first citizen anyone wants to insult.”

NOTE I (pag. 40, tom. 11) — Roland’s letter to the National Assembly, September 1792, described by the 1823 editor as “a historical monument” depicting Roland’s firm and courageous character, knowing the danger he ran in unmasking the September assassins and defying the suspended sword over his head. Roland writes that he comes to fulfill a sacred duty “whose accomplishment may cost me dearly; but I have never capitulated with my conscience.” He describes the denunciations of ministers at the Hôtel de Ville, the meeting of section presidents summoned by the ministers to calm spirits, and his refusal to remain silent. The people “terrible in its vengeance” still carries a sort of justice, directing its fury at those it believes have been too long spared by the law’s sword — but it is easy for scoundrels and traitors to abuse this effervescence. He ends: “Let them take my life, I want to keep it only for liberty and equality; if they were violated, destroyed, either by the reign of foreign despots or the error of a deceived people, I would have lived long enough; but until my last breath, I will have done my duty; it is the only good I covet, and that no power on earth can take from me.”

NOTE J (pag. 47, tom. II) — Circulaire of the Commune of Paris, 2 September 1792. A circular addressed to “Frères et amis” in all departments, justifying the September Massacres. The Commune declares that a “frightful plot, woven by the court, to slaughter all the patriots of the French empire” forced it to use the power of the people to save the nation. Having just been praised by the National Assembly for saving the patrie, it was then immediately decreed destitute — the Assembly recalled the decree only because of public clamor. The Commune now reports that “part of the ferocious conspirators held in the prisons has been put to death by the people; acts of justice that appeared to us indispensable to hold back by terror the legions of traitors locked within our walls, at the moment we were to march against the enemy.” Signed by Duplain, Panis, Sergent, Lenfant, Marat, Lefort, Jourdeuil — members of the committee of public safety constituted at the mairie.

NOTE K (pag. 62, tom. 11) — Extract from the account rendered 23 September 1792 by Roland, Minister of the Interior, to his fellow citizens. Roland’s itemized account of his expenditure of the 100,000 livres fund decreed by the law of 18 August for “correspondence costs and printing of writings proper to enlighten on the criminal plots of the enemies of the State.” The itemized list of 48 separate expenditures includes: Favier’s Observations on Austria (571 livres), a placard called La Dernière Heure rousing citizens against the Prussian invasion (135 livres), Bonaparte’s Souper de Beaucaire (?), the Sentinelle newspaper (multiple payments to printer Reynier totalling thousands of livres), translations in various languages of writings for distribution to Austrian soldiers, patriotic songs and noëls, and subscriptions to pro-republican newspapers for provincial municipalities and Jacobin clubs. The grand total spent: 32,913 livres 6 deniers, with 67,086 livres 19 sous 6 deniers remaining at the national treasury. Roland uses this account to refute the charge that he disposed of “millions” at his whim.

NOTE L (pag. 135, tom. II) — Letter from Madame Roland to the Section de Beaurepaire, 4 July 1793. Written from Sainte-Pélagie prison, this is her public defense addressed to her fellow residents of the Beaurepaire section. She explains that the Minister of the Interior (her husband) had applied to the Committee of General Security to enforce the law requiring detainees to be informed of the charge against them and interrogated promptly. The committee’s response, which she reproduces, instead set out its grounds for detaining her — grounds she says prove her case: her arrest is based on her husband’s absence, as if laws permitted taking one person for another; and on complicity in the supposed project of perverting public opinion, as if a minister’s responsibility extended to his wife. She denounces the false accusations, defends Roland’s five-month effort to have his accounts audited and his conduct judged (“this justice was obstinately refused to him; so they wanted to detain him to arrest him at a foreseen moment”), and declares: “Roland, far from corrupting the public spirit, has never ceased to obey the decree requiring him to enlighten it by writings all known and avowed.” She closes: “Wife of an honored minister, or prisoner at Sainte-Pélagie, here as there I am worth the same, I exist by the sentiments with which my heart is animated; today as then, indignant against injustice, but equally firm and peaceful in good or bad fortune, worthy of the first and superior to the second, I put no price on life except to practice what is just and to render homage to truth.”

  • Premier interrogatoire de Madame Roland — The first interrogation of Madame Roland during her detention at the Abbaye, 12 June 1793. The record of the questions put and her answers, transcribed in full.

  • Procès de Madame Roland — The text of the Revolutionary Tribunal’s judgment against her, dated 18 Brumaire Year II (8 November 1793), signed by vice-president Dumas and three judges (Deliège, Denizot, Subleyras): condemnation to death, goods confiscated to the republic, sentence to be executed within twenty-four hours in the Place de la Révolution. The judgment quotes the law that confiscates the property of those condemned to death and provides for the subsistence of widows and children if they have no other means.

  • Extrait de la Feuille du Salut Public: “Aux Républicaines” — An article from the Jacobin press, published immediately after the executions of Roland, Marie-Antoinette, and Olympe de Gouges. It addresses “Republican Women” and explains why each of the three was justly condemned: Marie-Antoinette for sacrificing her husband and children to Austrian ambition; Olympe de Gouges for delirium that led her to support federalism; Roland (“la femme Roland, bel-esprit à grands projets, philosophe à petits billets, reine d’un moment”) for pride, influence, and for being a monster “in every respect” — her disdainful bearing before the judges, her ironical gaiety on the way to the scaffold, her unfeminine ambition to rise above her sex. Women are enjoined to love their husbands and children, to be simple in dress and industrious in housekeeping, never to attend popular assemblies with the desire to speak. This article is a primary document of the Revolution’s ideology of separate spheres and its hostility to women’s political participation. ^[source: raw/roland-memoires-1823.txt]

Coverage Guide

  • Danton — The Notices historiques give the fullest sustained portrait of Danton in the wiki’s source collection: the inoculation argument (placing him in the council as a structural error), her physical description of his face and what it communicated, his biographical arc from debt-ridden lawyer through Orléanist partisan to substitute prosecutor of the Commune, his conduct during the September Massacres at the surveillance committee (embracing Marat, intercepting Roland’s arrest warrant, his remark about the prisoners: “Je me f… bien des prisonniers!”), and the final verdict that he “reigns” while probity is persecuted. Cross-reference buzot-memoires-revolution-1823 and louvet-memoires-1823 for parallel Girondin assessments.

  • Marat — Not a direct encounter, but two distinct treatments: her analysis of Danton’s use of Marat as an instrument against the Roland ministry (the manuscript funds, the attacks in the Ami du peuple), and the Toulouse woman anecdote giving the most vivid physical description of Marat in the wiki — his squalid person against his luxurious blue-and-white damask salon, the ottoman, the promise kept and the reciprocal demand delivered the next day.

  • Robespierre — Present but less developed: the footnote that she once thought him an honest man (paired with Buzot as the last men standing for principles), and the prison observation watching the Montagnards begin to devour each other (“the tigers are going to tear each other apart”).

  • The Paris Commune — The insurrectional Commune of August 10 described as composed of “those men who, having nothing to lose, have everything to gain in revolutions.” Her account of the September Massacres’ choreography — the black flag, the alarm cannon, citizens summoned to the Champ-de-Mars while the barriers closed — and Roland’s futile written warnings and printed requisitions.

  • The September Massacres — Covered from two angles: the Commune’s organizational role (contradictory dispositions, Danton at the surveillance committee, her own encounter with two hundred armed men at the ministry doors), and the Lazowski portrait establishing a named participant in the massacre of the priests at Saint-Firmin.

  • The May 31 coup from inside — Her race to the Convention in a cab, the trick she used to get through the guards, an hour in the petitioners’ hall, Vergniaud’s verdict that the Convention could do nothing, Roland’s arrest refused on procedural grounds then his flight, her apostrophe to the “good Parisians.”

  • Girondin self-portrait under charges — The Observations rapides goes through Pétion, Buzot, Gorsas, and Brissot asking at each point where the conspiracy was: their pre-revolutionary lives, their careers, their conduct in the Assemblies. The fullest Girondin rebuttal of the Amar indictment.

  • Roland’s ministerial resignation and the January–May 1793 deterioration — The seven unanswered letters to the Convention, Garat as incompetent successor, Pache’s organized disorganization at the War Ministry, the Convention’s inability to defend Roland without admitting its own complicity.

  • Mes dernières pensées — suicide, theology, political testament — The Shakespeare epigraph, the apologies to Roland and her daughter, the refusal to submit to the Tribunal’s pantomime, the indictment of the twenty-two Girondins for cowardice (“They temporized with crime, the cowards!”), the religious closing. The most personal document in the volume.

  • Roland’s death — Mentioned in the Notices: Jean-Marie Roland died by his own hand in a road near Rouen on learning of his wife’s execution, a sword-cane beside him. Not described in detail but established as fact.

  • The Champagneux footnote on Buzot — The editor’s acknowledgment that Roland had confided to him a personal reason for wanting to leave Paris in June 1793 “the time to publish which has not yet come.” The withheld disclosure concerns her relationship with Buzot. Cross-reference buzot-memoires-revolution-1823 for Buzot’s own account and Guadet’s biography.

  • Dorat-Cubières as career trajectory — The portrait traces the Almanach des Muses versifier through his ministerial place-seeking (trying to use Roland’s wife to introduce a prince seeking indemnities) to municipal secretary to signatory of her arrest warrant — a miniature of how certain men moved through the Revolution’s successive opportunities.

  • Women’s political agency and its suppression — Roland’s prison writings, her Mes dernières pensées, her Projet de défense she was not permitted to deliver, set against the Feuille du Salut Public appendix condemning her alongside Marie-Antoinette and Olympe de Gouges for overstepping their sex.

  • The Revolutionary Tribunal’s procedures — The text of her condemnation, her notes on the opening interrogation, the defense she drafted but was forbidden to deliver, and the Feuille du Salut Public’s framing of the three women executed in November 1793.
