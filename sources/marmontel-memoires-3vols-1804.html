Jean-François Marmontel, Mémoires d’un père pour servir à l’instruction de ses enfans, 3 vols. Paris: 1804 (posthumous). Extracted raw text from Project Gutenberg ebooks: Vol. 1 (EBook #27773) approximately 2,887 lines and 517 KB; Vol. 2 (EBook #27807) approximately 2,806 lines and 584 KB; Vol. 3 (EBook #27808) approximately 2,887 lines and 574 KB. Marmontel (1723–1799) was a French writer, poet, philosophe, and academician — a leading literary figure of the Enlightenment generation. He was a contributor to the Encyclopédie, historiographer of France, and from 1783 secrétaire perpétuel of the Académie française. His memoirs, written in retirement during the Revolution, cover his entire life from childhood in Bort-les-Orgues (Bas-Limousin) through his Paris literary career under the ancien régime and his direct experience of the French Revolution, ending with his retreat to Abbeville in 1795. Books XII–XX (Volume 3) constitute a moderate-constitutionalist eyewitness account of the Revolution from its pre-1787 origins through the Terror, written by a man who had lived at the centre of the intellectual and institutional life of pre-revolutionary France and lost nearly everything in the revolutionary crisis. ^[source: raw/marmontel-memoires-vol3.txt]

Volumes and Book Structure

The complete memoirs run to 20 books across three volumes. The first two volumes (Books I–XI) cover Marmontel’s life under the ancien régime — his provincial origins, literary career, the salons, his role at the Mercure de France, and his academic life. The third volume (Books XII–XX) covers the Revolution and its aftermath.

Revolution-Related Coverage Guide

Books XII through XX (all in Volume 3) constitute Marmontel’s account of the Revolution. His perspective is that of a constitutional monarchist and moderate philosophe — a man of letters who had been at the centre of the Académie française, the Encyclopédie circle, and the salon society of the ancien régime, who watched the monarchy collapse, survived the Terror, and wrote his memoirs in retirement. The following sections are of particular interest to the wiki:

  • Book XII: Causes of the Revolution — Marmontel’s analysis of the structural causes: the character of Louis XVI, the weakness of the ministries, the fiscal crisis, and the political miscalculations of the court. His portrait of the king is one of the most extended by a contemporary moderate. ^[source: raw/marmontel-memoires-vol3.txt]

  • Book XIII: The Brienne Ministry and the Pre-Revolution — The most detailed section on the 1787–88 pre-revolutionary crisis: the struggle between Brienne and the Parlement over the stamp tax and land tax, the lit de justice, the exile of the Parlement to Troyes, the séance royale, the resistance of the Breton and Dauphiné estates, Brienne’s resignation, the suicide of Lamoignon, Necker’s return, and the Second Assembly of Notables. Marmontel, as a member of the Académie and a client of the salons, had access to the political elite of this period. This is an insider’s view of the mechanism of government collapse. ^[source: raw/marmontel-memoires-vol3.txt]

  • Book XIV: The Electoral Assemblies of 1789 — This is one of the most valuable sections of the memoirs for the wiki: Marmontel was himself a member of the primary assembly of the Feuillants district and of the electoral assembly. He describes the electoral meetings, the role of Duport, the election of Target as president, the growing influence of the avocats, and the election of the duc d’Orléans. He also records his own relations with Lafayette and Sieyès during this period. ^[source: raw/marmontel-memoires-vol3.txt]

  • Book XV: The Estates-General and the Tennis Court Oath — The constitutional struggle between the orders, the Third Estate’s self-proclamation as the National Assembly, the séance royale of 23 June, the Tennis Court Oath, and Necker’s recall and dismissal. ^[source: raw/marmontel-memoires-vol3.txt]

  • Book XVI–XVII: The Fall of the Bastille — Books XVI gives the narrative of the July crisis: Lambesc’s charge, the seizure of arms from the Invalides, the storming of the Bastille, and the massacres of de Launey, Flesselles, and the Bastille garrison. Book XVII contains the remarkable eyewitness account of Élie, one of the “vainqueurs de la Bastille,” which Marmontel collected directly from him. Élie’s récit is one of the few surviving participant narratives of the Bastille’s fall from the insurgent side, embedded within a moderate memoirist’s frame. ^[source: raw/marmontel-memoires-vol3.txt]

  • Book XVIII: The October Days — The events of 5–6 October 1789: the women’s march to Versailles, the invasion of the château, the murder of the gardes du corps, and the forced return of the royal family to Paris. Marmontel records these events from Paris. He also describes the murder of Foulon and Bertier. ^[source: raw/marmontel-memoires-vol3.txt]

  • Book XIX: The Terror — The Terror seen from the perspective of a constitutional monarchist academician in hiding or retirement: the law of suspects, the trial and execution of the king, the proscription of the Girondins, Charlotte Corday and Marat, the suppression of the académies (which Marmontel felt with particular force as secrétaire perpétuel), the decrees against ex-nobles and ex-priests, and the death of the queen. Marmontel writes as a man whose entire social and institutional world was systematically dismantled. ^[source: raw/marmontel-memoires-vol3.txt]

  • Book XX: Retrospect — Marmontel’s final reflections and his account of his literary work in retirement: the Nouveaux Contes moraux, the educational courses in grammar, logic, metaphysics, and morality that he wrote for his children, and the composition of these memoirs themselves. ^[source: raw/marmontel-memoires-vol3.txt]

Quality

Project Gutenberg ebooks produced from images generously made available by the Bibliothèque nationale de France (BnF/Gallica). French text, 18th-century orthography and typography preserved (e.g., “foible” for “faible,” “événemens” for “événements”). Marmontel’s prose is clear, classical, and measured in the academic style — one of the most readable of the French memoirists of this period, far less digressive than Buzot or Paganel. The PG editions are cleanly transcribed with page notes. Medium difficulty for French readers; low difficulty for those used to 18th-century French prose. ^[source: raw/marmontel-memoires-vol1.txt]

Value to the Wiki

Marmontel’s memoirs are one of the most valuable moderate-constitutionalist sources in the wiki’s collection. He writes as:

  • An institutional insider of the ancien régime (Académie française, Mercure de France, the salons)
  • A participant in the 1789 electoral assemblies, giving a rare ground-level view of how the Paris electoral machinery worked
  • A collector of participant testimony (e.g., Élie’s Bastille narrative)
  • A victim of the Terror (he was proscribed, his academic world was destroyed) but without the Girondin partisan intensity of Buzot or Roland
  • An elder of the Enlightenment generation (born 1723, friend of Voltaire, d’Alembert, Diderot), writing a synoptic view of his entire life across both the ancien régime and the Revolution

The memoirs are especially valuable for the pre-1789 period (Books XII–XIII) where they cover the Brienne ministry and the collapse of the royal administration — a period less well covered by other memoir sources in the wiki, which tend to begin with the Estates-General.
