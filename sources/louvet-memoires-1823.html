Jean-Baptiste Louvet de Couvray, Mémoires, in Collection des Mémoires relatifs à la Révolution Française. Paris: Baudouin Frères, 1823. The extracted raw text runs to approximately 14,010 lines and 674 KB, from a Harvard/Widener Library copy. Louvet (1760–1797) was a Girondin deputy to the National Convention, best known for his speech of October 29, 1792, in which he formally accused Robespierre before the Convention (“Je t’accuse, Robespierre!”). After the Montagnard coup of May 31–June 2, 1793 proscribed the leading Girondins, Louvet went into hiding; he wrote these memoirs in April 1794 from a cave in the Jura, expecting to die. He survived, returned to the Convention after Thermidor, and died in 1797 as French consul-designate to Palermo. Published posthumously as part of the Baudouin Frères series of revolutionary memoirs. ^[source: raw/louvet-memoires-1795.txt]

Contents at a Glance

Louvet’s Mémoires are explicitly unpolished — he says so himself in his prefatory note, written from hiding on 19 April 1794: “I throw down notes, and that is all. Do not expect either stylistic concision or abundance of detail. Truly, I am writing neither history nor what is called memoirs. I am recording notes that may help me, if some day of genuine leisure is given to me — or help someone else, if I do not survive.” The rawness is part of their value. This is not a text composed for literary effect after the fact; it is a record written under mortal danger by someone who did not know he would survive.

The Mémoires cover two intertwined things: the political history of the Girondin faction from the perspective of one of its most combative members, and the personal narrative of Louvet’s flight, concealment, and eventual return. The political sections give the Girondin reading of the May 31–June 2 coup, the responsibility of Robespierre and the Mountain, the federalist resistance in Caen and Bordeaux, and the fate of various colleagues. The personal narrative — hiding in Caen, escaping to Brittany, crossing to Bordeaux, taking refuge in the Gironde, making his way to the Jura, waiting in a cave — is among the more vivid fugitive accounts of the Terror period.

The volume also includes a biographical Notice on Louvet by the Baudouin editors, and a crucial related text: his “Lettre à la Convention,” written from hiding, in which he addresses the survivors of the Convention still under Montagnard control and argues for his own rehabilitation and for the victims of the June 2 coup.

Cross-reference: Madame Roland’s Mémoires (Volume II in this wiki) include a portrait of Louvet; he appears in her Portraits as “courageous and vivacious, with political limitations.” The two texts illuminate each other from complementary Girondin positions.

Editors’ Avertissement and Notice on Louvet’s Life

The Baudouin Frères editors explain the series: the Collection des Mémoires relatifs à la Révolution Française is organized around the principle of reproducing primary documents without alteration — not even removing passages that are objectionable, since accuracy matters more than editorial comfort. They had faced criticism for including a Maratist pamphlet comparing Marat to Christ; they defend it as evidence of the “monstrous absurdity” of the period, not endorsement.

The Notice biographique on Louvet’s life gives the basic facts: born 1760, author of the novel Faublas (one of the most popular erotic novels of the 1780s), journalist, elected to the Legislative Assembly and then the National Convention representing the Loiret, Girondin activist. His wife Lodoïska (the model for the heroine of Faublas) managed a bookshop in Paris under the Terror while he was in hiding. He died in 1797 aged thirty-seven, named but not yet installed as consul at Palermo. His manuscripts — poetry and correspondence — were destroyed during the Terror. ^[source: raw/louvet-memoires-1795.txt]

Prefatory Documents: The Manifesto of Caen

Before the main narrative, Louvet reproduces and defends the Proclamation de l’assemblée centrale de résistance à l’oppression (the so-called “Manifeste de Wimpfen”), drafted by him at Caen in June 1793 and adopted by the Girondin deputies who had gathered there. The document called on departments to resist the Convention’s coup as a usurpation, and to rally republican forces to restore the rightful deputies. He was charged with it (as a federalist manifesto); he defends it as a purely republican document aimed at the Montagnard usurpers, not at the Convention or the Republic as institutions.

He also prints his response to Lindet’s report accusing the Girondins, and references Faure’s report with its sixty-three signatures — a key documentary set for the Girondin-Montagnard conflict. ^[source: raw/louvet-memoires-1795.txt]

Author’s Avertissement (from the Jura, April 19, 1794)

The prefatory note from hiding. Louvet explains the conditions under which he is writing — cave in the Jura, in hiding for nearly a year, time limited, materials sparse. He dedicates the text to the memory of Madame Roland, quoting an extract from her defense manuscripts: “Liberty! it is for proud souls who despise death and know when to give it; it is not for those corrupt men who, rising from the bed of debauchery or from the mud of misery, rush to bathe in the blood streaming from scaffolds.” He introduces this as the only preface worthy of his notes. ^[source: raw/louvet-memoires-1795.txt]

Quelques Notices pour l’Histoire, et le Récit de mes Périls depuis le 31 mai

The main body of the text — the “Some Historical Notes and Account of My Perils since May 31” — is the combined political history and personal narrative. The opening section describes Louvet before the Revolution: living in the country near Paris, composing the novels that would become Faublas, with a small income (eight hundred francs a year “made me live”), and deeply in love with Lodoïska — a woman married off to a wealthy man when she was sixteen, who returned six years later and became his companion and eventually wife. The Revolution interrupted his literary plans and pulled him toward political journalism. He records writing his first political pamphlet, Paris justifié, in response to Mounier’s attack on the October Days, and entering the Jacobins in the club’s earliest months — “only admitted with the credentials of true civism and some talent.” He became the editor of the Sentinelle and the author of the famous Robespierre accusation. ^[source: raw/louvet-memoires-1795.txt]

The March 10 conspiracy and Marat’s tribunal. Before the June 2 coup proper, Louvet narrates the conspiration du 10 mars — a Montagnard attempt to seize power prematurely, which he had exposed in a pamphlet he describes as his “political testament.” He documents the creation of the Revolutionary Tribunal and how Marat intervened to force through his own jury list when the Girondins succeeded in nominating honest men: “he cried counter-revolution, threatened to call the people, had the ballot annulled, imposed his list.” The jurés were “for the most part massacreurs de septembre.” At this point Louvet prints a key anecdote: when Pache read the petition of the “twenty-two” to the Convention, Boyer-Fonfrède took the floor and said, roughly, “as for me, I regret not to be among those on whom the municipality of Paris today calls daggers” — and nearly the whole Assembly rose spontaneously, crying “All! all!” This moment of Convention solidarity was real; it did not prevent May 31. A Bordelais recently released from Austrian captivity had told Guadet that the Austrian general staff was confident twenty-two heads would fall in the Convention — and when Pache presented the petition naming precisely twenty-two, the coincidence seemed to Louvet proof of Montagnard collusion with Austria. ^[source: raw/louvet-memoires-1795.txt]

Political prelude: the May 31–June 2 coup. Louvet is categorical: May 31 was not a popular revolution but a criminal conspiracy. The Convention was held prisoner by armed sections and the Commune; its decrees were extorted at gunpoint; the republic was effectively overthrown. He documents his own near-arrest and flight: “I say this to the friends of liberty, if any remain.” His account of the weeks immediately after the coup is not triumphalist about the Girondins — he is honest that many were passive, many fled too quickly, many had been politically cautious when courage was needed. But his verdict on the coup itself is unequivocal: “these crimes were not those of the republic! They never permitted us to establish it! It was to debase it, to make it hateful, to destroy it forever, that they constantly mixed its name with their cruel turpitudes.” ^[source: raw/louvet-memoires-1795.txt]

Caen and the federalist resistance. Louvet was among the proscribed deputies who gathered in Caen under the Norman department administration’s protection. He drafted the Proclamation de l’assemblée centrale de résistance à l’oppression (the Manifesto of Caen) — he emphasizes that it was a collective document, approved by the assembled deputies, not his alone. The military force assembled under General Wimpfen attempted to march on Paris but was defeated at Pacy-sur-Eure in July 1793. Louvet is honest about why the federalist resistance failed: property-owning republicans in the provinces were fundamentally unwilling to leave their homes; they sent “hired men” whose loyalty could be bought against them; while “the ardent, audacious Mountain, inured to crime, drew the sword against the fatherland” and its Parisian emissaries arrived in the provinces offering provincial compliance the name of patriotism. “The most formerly proud cities began to fall before two or three Jacobins.”

The internal tensions of Caen are documented: genuine republicans who wanted to march on Paris; men who had their own reasons for not wanting a Montagnard victory but who were suspected of royalist sympathies; the impossibility of maintaining a disciplined political identity when the common enemy was pressing from outside and suspicion was corroding from within. Wimpfen himself was suspected of secret intelligences with the enemy — Louvet raises but does not resolve the question. ^[source: raw/louvet-memoires-1795.txt]

Charlotte Corday. The most celebrated passage in the Mémoires outside the political narrative. Corday came to Caen before the assassination, Louvet says, ostensibly to beg a favor for some of her relatives — but her real motive “was doubtless to know some of the founders of this republic for which she was about to devote herself; and perhaps she was also glad that one day her features would be vivid in their memory.” He declares: “I affirm that she never said to any of us a word about her design.” He adds the crucial detail: if she had consulted them, they would not have directed her toward Marat — they knew he had at most two days left to live from his disease. “Let us humble ourselves before the decrees of Providence; it wished that Robespierre and his accomplices live long enough to destroy each other, long enough for it to be well proven to the French nation… that some were traitorous royalists, and the other the most ambitious of tyrants.”

His portrait of Corday is among the most intensely felt passages in the text: “proud and gentle, decent and beautiful, as she always appeared to us; her bearing had that dignity full of assurance, and her gaze that fire tempered by modesty.” He had seen her the evening before she left for Paris. He is explicit that her act was just; he does not claim to have approved its direction. The footnote by the editors notes Adam Lux — the deputy from Mainz who wrote a public apology defending Corday as greater than Brutus, was imprisoned, and went to the guillotine saying “I am going to die for Charlotte Corday.” Louvet also notes an unnamed young man who asked to be imprisoned in Corday’s place; the Cordeliers granted him part of his request. ^[source: raw/louvet-memoires-1795.txt]

The failure of federalism and the broader Terror. Louvet provides a sweeping indictment of the Terror’s provincial manifestations: Collot-d’Herbois and Ronsin deliberating for hours at Lyon about which method of mass execution would be most solemn — mitraillade or sword — while the crowd applauded; Lebon at Arras forcing a wife to stand under the guillotine dripping with her husband’s blood. “Why did it require nothing less than this experience for me to be convinced of this unfortunate truth, that… men must be slaves, since men are wicked, or cringe before the wicked?” He pulls back from this despair in the next paragraph: “among so much depravity, it is consoling to declare that even in France there still exist some beings worthy of liberty. We found them above all among individuals of that sex reputed frivolous and timid. Women prodigued us the most touching attentions and all those courageous succors that generous compassion never refuses to unmerited misfortune.” ^[source: raw/louvet-memoires-1795.txt]

Escape to Brittany. After Caen, the Breton deputies and sympathizers who had formed the core of Wimpfen’s force were worked on by emissaries: their primary assemblies had accepted the new constitution, they were told; to continue fighting was now to constitute a faction. The Bretons — “naturally very brave” — were drawn off. Louvet’s account of the flight through Brittany emphasizes the political unreliability of the countryside: people who had been protected by the Girondins for ten months refused to give them shelter for a single night, or opened their doors only to close them when they heard footsteps on the road. “Friends ferocious through pusillanimity would refuse to recognize their friend… friends of twenty years would drive you from their door, repulse you to the foot of the scaffold!” ^[source: raw/louvet-memoires-1795.txt]

Bordeaux and the Gironde. Louvet and several other fugitives reached Bordeaux and its surrounding wine-country. The Gironde region — from which the Girondin party took its name and many of its leading figures — remained longer than other provinces in a state of relative republican independence. The title of the chapter’s section from Saint-Emilion’s grottos (given in the opening dateline) is significant: Louvet was writing some of his notes from hiding in the wine-cellar caves of that town, where several proscribed deputies sheltered together. Bordeaux’s submission to the Terror narrowed the window, and the decision was made to move further, toward the Jura. ^[source: raw/louvet-memoires-1795.txt]

The Jura and the cave. The dateline of the Avertissement — “From the grottos of the Jura, April 19, 1794” — gives the physical setting: a limestone cave in the Jura mountains, where Louvet and Lodoïska sheltered for several months. Cold, inadequate food, isolation, the sound of distant wind. He is writing by whatever light is available, at speed, knowing he may not live. “I throw down notes, and that is all… I am recording notes that may help me if some day of genuine leisure is given to me — or help someone else, if I do not survive.” The psychological portrait of this period is stark: a man who had been a journalist, novelist, political orator, and Convention deputy is now invisible, cold, waiting. His one solid political act from the cave is the Lettre à la Convention — the demand that the surviving Convention recognize the June 2 coup for what it was. ^[source: raw/louvet-memoires-1795.txt]

Return after Thermidor. The fall of Robespierre on 9 Thermidor Year II (July 27, 1794) ended the immediate mortal danger. Louvet’s account of the return to Paris is relatively compressed compared to the flight narrative — the Mémoires were written during the hiding, not the return. What he records is the first steps of political rehabilitation: the Convention under Thermidorian pressure began reversing the proscriptions, and the surviving Girondins returned to their seats. Louvet resumed his journalistic and political career, was among the founders of the journal La Sentinelle in its second form, and represented the moderate republican position through the remaining years of the Convention and the early Directory. He died in 1797, named as consul to Palermo, before he could take up the post. ^[source: raw/louvet-memoires-1795.txt]

Lettre à la Convention

A formal political address from hiding, directed at the surviving deputies of the Convention still under Montagnard control. Louvet argues for the return of the proscribed deputies, defends the Girondin record against specific charges, and calls on the Convention to recognize that the June 2 coup had violated every constitutional principle the Republic claimed to uphold. The letter is a political document, not a personal appeal; it is written in the register of constitutional argument. It ends with a demand that those responsible for the proscriptions be held accountable. ^[source: raw/louvet-memoires-1795.txt]

Coverage Guide

  • The Girondin coup of May 31–June 2, 1793 — Louvet’s account is the most detailed in the wiki for the coup itself from the Girondin side: who was where, what was said in the Convention, how the sections and the Commune operated, who resisted and who capitulated. Cross-reference with roland-memoires-vol2-1823 for the complementary Girondin account.

  • The federalist resistance at Caen — The only source in the wiki describing from the inside the Caen assembly’s organization, the drafting of the Manifesto, the military failure at Pacy-sur-Eure, and the political tensions within the federalist camp about royalism vs. republicanism.

  • Charlotte Corday and Marat’s assassination — Treated obliquely but memorably: Corday came from the same Norman milieu as the Caen resisters; Louvet’s disavowal combined with his evident sympathy for the act’s justice is historically significant.

  • The mechanics of clandestine survival during the Terror — The flight narrative is one of the more complete accounts of how proscribed republicans actually survived: false papers, sympathetic intermediaries, regional networks, the geography of resistance (Brittany, the Gironde, the Jura). Cross-reference with roland-memoires-vol2-1823 for the complementary experience of a prisoner rather than a fugitive.

  • Robespierre accusation (1792) — Referenced throughout: the famous speech of October 29, 1792 (“Je t’accuse, Robespierre!”) is the defining act of Louvet’s political identity and the origin of the Montagnard vendetta against him.

  • Thermidorian rehabilitation — The return to the Convention after Thermidor, and the beginning of the reversal of Girondin proscriptions.

  • Louvet as portrayed by Roland — The Portraits section of roland-memoires-vol2-1823 gives Roland’s character sketch of Louvet, written at roughly the same time. The two texts are complementary primary accounts of the same circles.

Quality

Harvard/Widener Library copy, OCR from a Baudouin Frères 1823 print. The text is cleaner than the Fantin (shorter sentences, more direct prose style); Louvet was a professional writer and the text’s roughness is intentional rather than editorial. Editors’ footnotes are numerous and marked as “(Note des éditeurs)” — they supply context and correct or qualify Louvet’s claims where they had documentary evidence. These notes are generally useful and readable in the OCR. The editors’ avertissement and the Notice biographique are in the same print run and fully legible. Running headers are simple and do not interfere with the prose extraction.
