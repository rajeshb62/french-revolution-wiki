Rear Admiral Alfred Thayer Mahan, The Influence of Sea Power upon the French Revolution and Empire, 1793–1812, Volume II. Published 1892. This volume continues Mahan’s great thesis from Volume I (which covered 1783–1801) through the remainder of the Revolutionary and Napoleonic wars, from the Second Coalition through Trafalgar, the Continental System, and Napoleon’s Russian invasion. The text runs to approximately 2 MB across eight chapters (XII–XIX) plus footnotes and an index.

Volume II covers the period from roughly 1798 to 1812. The two great set-pieces are the Battle of Copenhagen (1801) and the Trafalgar campaign (1805), but the volume’s analytical weight falls equally on the economic warfare that dominated the later years — the Berlin and Milan Decrees, the British Orders in Council, and the attempt by both belligerents to strangle each other through commerce. The final chapter (XIX) is Mahan’s comprehensive summary of the function of sea power in the Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars, drawing together the threads of both volumes into a single argument about British naval policy, national strategy, and the structural sources of Britain’s victory. ^[source: raw/mahan-sea-power-french-revolution-vol2.txt]

Contents at a Glance

Volume II opens with France in crisis under the Directory and the Second Coalition pressing on all frontiers; Bonaparte’s coup of Brumaire and the subsequent Italian campaign shatter the Coalition, but his attempt to isolate Britain through the Armed Neutrality of 1800 is broken by Nelson at Copenhagen. The Peace of Amiens (1802–1803) provides a brief interlude, then war resumes with Napoleon preparing the invasion of England and assembling the great flotilla at Boulogne. The Trafalgar campaign — the only great naval campaign ever planned by Napoleon — occupies two chapters and ends with the destruction of the Franco-Spanish fleet and the permanent frustration of any invasion attempt. The final three chapters turn to the economic war: the commerce raiding that preceded the Berlin Decree, the Continental System in full operation (1806–1812), and the conclusive summary of sea power’s role in the war’s outcome.

Full Contents Map

Chapter XII — Events on the Continent, 1798–1800. Disorders of France under the Directory. Disastrous War of the Second Coalition. Establishment of the Consulate. Bonaparte overthrows Austria and frames against Great Britain the Armed Neutrality of 1800. Peace of Lunéville with Austria.

The longest chapter in the volume, covering the period from Bonaparte’s Syrian campaign through the establishment of the Consulate. Mahan opens with the collapse of the Neapolitan campaign (November 1798) — a premature blow instigated by Nelson after the Nile — and traces the building of the Second Coalition: Russia, Austria, England, Turkey, Naples, Portugal, and the Two Sicilies united against France. The Coalition’s early successes are real but uncoordinated: Suwarrow’s Russian army sweeps through northern Italy, the French are driven from the peninsula, and Austria presses into Switzerland. But the Coalition’s fatal weakness, Mahan argues, was the same as the First’s — powers with divergent aims acting in mutual distrust. Russia’s withdrawal after Suwarrow’s disagreements with the Austrian council leaves the Coalition crippled; Bonaparte’s return from Egypt and the coup of 18 Brumaire (November 1799) give France a unified command just as the Coalition loses its coherence. The chapter follows Bonaparte’s Italian campaign of 1800 — the crossing of the Saint Bernard, the battle of Marengo (June 14), and Moreau’s concurrent victory at Hohenlinden — which shatters Austria. The Peace of Lunéville (February 1801) removes Austria from the war, leaving Britain isolated. At the same moment, Bonaparte is constructing the Armed Neutrality of 1800: Russia, Sweden, Denmark, and Prussia combined against British maritime rights, closing the Baltic to British shipping and challenging the right of search. Mahan presents this as Bonaparte’s most coherent attempt to counter British sea power through political combination rather than naval action. ^[source: raw/mahan-sea-power-french-revolution-vol2.txt]

Chapter XIII — Events of 1801. British Expedition to the Baltic—Battle of Copenhagen—Bonaparte’s futile attempts to contest control of the Sea—His Continental Policy—Preliminaries of Peace with Great Britain, October, 1801—Influence of Sea Power so far upon the Course of the Revolution.

The chapter’s centerpiece is the Battle of Copenhagen (April 2, 1801), narrated with the tactical precision characteristic of Mahan’s battle pieces. The British government, with Britain isolated after Lunéville and the Baltic closed, assembles a fleet of eighteen sail-of-the-line at Yarmouth under Sir Hyde Parker, with Nelson as second-in-command. Mahan traces the diplomatic negotiations with Denmark that precede the battle — the British demands, the Danish refusal, the decision to force the Sound. The tactical challenge of Copenhagen is presented as one of the most difficult of the age: the Danish fleet anchored in a shoal-strewn channel, protected by shore batteries, the Trekroner fortress, and a line of hulks and floating batteries. Nelson’s decision to run the channel from the south, his careful sounding operations aboard a hired pilot-boat the night before, Parker’s signal of recall, and Nelson’s legendary act (turning his telescope to his blind eye, saying “I really do not see the signal”) are all recounted. Mahan’s strategic point is classic: the Armed Neutrality, which had seemed to Bonaparte a brilliant diplomatic counter to British sea power, was broken in a single morning because it rested on paper — on conventions between sovereigns — while British sea power rested on ships, men, and a professional navy that could act at distance. The Danish fleet was destroyed, the Coalition dissolved, and the Baltic reopened. Mahan then traces the preliminaries of peace: the evacuation of Egypt (the French army in Egypt surrenders in September 1801), the resignation of Pitt, the negotiation with the new Addington ministry, and the signature of the Preliminaries of London (October 1, 1801). He closes with a short assessment of sea power’s influence to this point in the war, noting that at every critical juncture — the Nile, the Second Coalition, the Armed Neutrality — British naval action had determined the outcome of political combinations that seemed overwhelming on land. ^[source: raw/mahan-sea-power-french-revolution-vol2.txt]

Chapter XIV — Outline of Events from the Signature of the Preliminaries to the Rupture of the Peace of Amiens. October, 1801–May, 1803.

The Peace of Amiens (March 1802) was the longest interval of peace between Britain and France in the entire war — fourteen months. Mahan’s chapter is an anatomy of why it failed. The preliminaries had seemed to settle the main outlines: Britain would restore most colonial conquests (except Trinidad and Ceylon), France would leave Naples and the Papal States, and Malta would be returned to the Knights of St. John under a guarantee of independence. But almost immediately, Mahan shows, Bonaparte violated the spirit of the treaty: French troops remained in the Batavian Republic, French influence expanded in Italy (the Cisalpine Republic was transformed into the Italian Republic with Bonaparte as president), and Piedmont was annexed outright. Bonaparte’s interventions in Switzerland (the Act of Mediation, 1803) further alarmed Britain. Mahan reads these not as random aggressions but as a consistent pattern: Bonaparte was using the peace to consolidate French domination of the Continent, and he never intended the treaty as a final settlement. Britain’s response was belated and divided: the Addington ministry was accused of weakness, Pitt criticized the peace from opposition, and public opinion swung from relief to fear as Bonaparte’s intentions became clear. By early 1803 the breach was irreparable; Britain declared war on May 18, 1803. The chapter closes with Mahan’s assessment that the Peace of Amiens was never a real peace — it was an armed truce in which both sides prepared for the next round, and the only question was which side would be readier when it came. ^[source: raw/mahan-sea-power-french-revolution-vol2.txt]

Chapter XV — The Trafalgar Campaign to the Spanish Declaration of War. May, 1803–December, 1804. Preparations for the Invasion of England. The Great Flotilla. Napoleon’s Military and Naval Combinations and British Naval Strategy. Essential Unity of Napoleon’s Purpose. Causes of Spanish War.

The opening of the military narrative after the resumption of war, and the first of two Trafalgar campaign chapters. Napoleon’s grand design is presented: an invasion of England, the decisive stroke that would end British sea power at its source. The preparations at Boulogne are described in detail — the flotilla of hundreds of flat-bottomed barges, the Camp de Boulogne with 150,000 men encamped and training, the press of shipping in the Channel ports. But Mahan’s analytical purpose is to show that Napoleon understood the invasion could not succeed without at least temporary naval control of the Channel, and that he therefore framed the most elaborate naval combination ever attempted: a series of sorties by squadrons from Toulon, Rochefort, Brest, and the Spanish ports, designed to draw the British fleet into the West Indies, then double back and concentrate in the Channel for a brief window of superiority.

The chapter traces the early attempts at this plan. Missiessy escapes from Rochefort in January 1804 and reaches Martinique; Villeneuve sails from Toulon but is forced back by weather. Napoleon’s pressure on Spain — whose treasure fleets from America Britain had intercepted — forces Spain toward war despite her reluctance. The Spanish declaration of war (December 1804) brings the French-Spanish alliance into force, giving Napoleon a combined fleet that matches the British in numbers. Mahan devotes considerable space to the British defensive strategy: the close blockade of Brest, Toulon, Rochefort, and Ferrol; the strain this placed on British ships, crews, and supplies; the constant need to guess which sortie was the real one. The chapter closes with the strategic situation at the end of 1804: Napoleon’s plan intact but not yet executed, the British holding their breath, and Nelson returning to command of the Mediterranean station after a brief rest. ^[source: raw/mahan-sea-power-french-revolution-vol2.txt]

Chapter XVI — The Trafalgar Campaign—Concluded. Successive Modifications of Napoleon’s Plan. Narrative of Naval Movements. Final Failure of Napoleon’s Naval Combinations. War with Austria, and Battle of Austerlitz. Battle of Trafalgar. Vital Change Imposed Upon Napoleon’s Policy by the Result of the Naval Campaign.

The climax of the volume and one of the finest sustained passages in Mahan’s entire oeuvre — a combined narrative of the Trafalgar campaign, the Ulm-Austerlitz campaign, and their interaction. Mahan treats them as one campaign fought on two elements: Napoleon on land, the Royal Navy at sea, each trying to achieve a decisive concentration before the enemy could react.

The narrative is intricate. Villeneuve escapes from Toulon in March 1805, eludes Nelson (who is guarding the Strait of Gibraltar), reaches the West Indies, and joins the Ferrol squadron. Nelson pursues across the Atlantic — Mahan emphasizes that Nelson guessed the destination and went straight, while Villeneuve’s orders were to avoid action. Once in the West Indies, Villeneuve misses Nelson by days; the French fleet finds no strategic opportunity (the British islands take warning, colonial operations stall) and Villeneuve, against Napoleon’s explicit orders, returns to Europe rather than waiting. He is sighted off Cape Finisterre on July 22 by Calder’s squadron; the action is indecisive (two Spanish ships taken, Churchillian weather obscures the rest), and Villeneuve puts into Ferrol and then Cadiz rather than proceeding to Brest as ordered. Napoleon, waiting at Boulogne with his army and flotilla ready, hears that the combined fleet has gone to Cadiz instead of Brest. In a rage — Mahan quotes Napoleon’s letter to Decrès: “What a navy! What an admiral!” — he abandons the invasion of England, turns the Army of England eastward, and marches against Austria.

The land campaign is summarised: Ulm (October 1805), where Mack’s Austrian army is surrounded and captured before it can join the Russians; Austerlitz (December 2, 1805), where Napoleon destroys the combined Austro-Russian army in one of his greatest battles. But at sea, on October 21, 1805, off Cape Trafalgar, Nelson falls upon the combined Franco-Spanish fleet of thirty-three ships-of-the-line with his twenty-seven. Mahan’s account of the battle is tactical and analytical: the British approach in two columns, the break in the allied line, the yardarm-to-yardarm engagement, Nelson’s wound and death, the capture or destruction of nineteen enemy ships without the loss of a single British vessel. The strategic consequence is not that Britain gains control of the sea — it already had that — but that Napoleon’s naval arm is broken for the rest of the war. The Emperor would never again be able to assemble a fleet that could challenge the Royal Navy in a line-of-battle engagement. Mahan’s closing argument is that Trafalgar imposed a vital change on Napoleon’s policy: unable to fight Britain at sea, he turned to the economic warfare of the Continental System — the attempt to starve Britain by closing every European port to her trade. ^[source: raw/mahan-sea-power-french-revolution-vol2.txt]

Chapter XVII — The Warfare against Commerce during the French Revolution and Empire, to the Berlin Decree. 1793–1806.

A turning point in the volume’s structure. Mahan steps back from narrative to analysis, tracing the development of commerce raiding as a weapon against British trade from the opening of the war in 1793 through the eve of the Berlin Decree in 1806. The chapter covers the privateering system of the French Republic, the convoy system adopted by the British, the legal and diplomatic disputes over neutral rights, and the escalating measures of both sides. Mahan argues that commerce warfare was the one area where French naval weakness did not tell: a single privateer or small squadron could do disproportionate damage to British shipping, and the French government consistently overestimated the effect. He provides statistical evidence of British shipping losses year by year, showing that while losses were real and painful, they never approached the levels that would force Britain to sue for peace. The British merchant marine, with its enormous tonnage and global reach, absorbed losses that would have shattered any smaller trading nation. The chapter also covers the British reaction — Orders in Council, the blockade decrees, the tightening of the convoy system — and the steady expansion of the definition of contraband. By 1806, Mahan shows, both belligerents had moved far beyond the Law of Nations as it had been understood in 1793: the war was consuming the legal order as surely as it consumed men and treasure. ^[source: raw/mahan-sea-power-french-revolution-vol2.txt]

Chapter XVIII — The Warfare against Commerce, 1806–1812. The Berlin and Milan Decrees of Napoleon, 1806 and 1807. The British Orders in Council, 1807–1809. Analysis of the Policy of these Measures of the two Belligerents. Outline of Contemporary Leading Events.

The Continental System in full operation. Mahan opens with Napoleon’s Berlin Decree (November 21, 1806), issued from the Prussian capital after Jena: the British Isles declared in a state of blockade, all commerce and correspondence with Britain forbidden, all British subjects in French-controlled territory declared prisoners of war, all British goods and merchandise declared lawful prize. The Decree was not, Mahan argues, a military measure in any ordinary sense — it was economic war, an attempt to destroy British commerce by closing the entire European continent to its trade.

The chapter traces the system’s enforcement and its evasion. Napoleon demands that every state under his control or influence adopt the Decree; the British retaliate with the Orders in Council (1807, 1809), blockading any port that excluded British goods and requiring neutral vessels to call at British ports for inspection. Mahan analyzes the effect on European economies: ports rotting idle, colonial goods vanishing from markets, smuggling becoming a universal industry, and the resentment of European populations growing against the system that was impoverishing them to serve French strategic ends. The Portuguese royal family’s flight to Brazil under British escort (November 1807) is a set-piece illustrating the interaction of naval power and continental politics: the British could not save Portugal on land, but they could preserve the Portuguese crown and empire across the Atlantic. Napoleon’s attempt to close the entire Baltic by seizing the Danish fleet (the second Copenhagen expedition, 1807 — Canning’s preemptive strike that carried off the Danish fleet before Napoleon could take it) is narrated as a parallel to Nelson’s 1801 operation.

The chapter closes with the expansion of the system into Russia — the Tilsit agreement (July 1807) in which Alexander I accepted the Continental System — and the first cracks in the structure: the Peninsular War (1808), the Austrian revival (1809), the growing tension between Napoleon and the Tsar over the blockade’s enforcement. Mahan’s treatment is analytical rather than narrative: he uses the events as evidence for the proposition that economic warfare, however devastating to neutrals and civilians, cannot by itself defeat a nation with command of the sea and access to world markets. The Continental System inflicted serious hardship on Britain but did not bring her to terms; it simultaneously created the conditions for Napoleon’s eventual downfall by alienating the populations and rulers he needed to keep the system closed. ^[source: raw/mahan-sea-power-french-revolution-vol2.txt]

Chapter XIX — Summary—The Function of Sea Power and the Policy of Great Britain in the Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars.

The concluding chapter of the entire two-volume work, and Mahan’s final statement of his thesis. He opens with Britain’s unpreparedness in 1792 — Pitt’s small navy estimates, the expectation of peace, the complete absence of any plan for the war that was coming — and traces the development of British strategy through the twenty-three-year struggle. The chapter is not a narrative but an argument, organised around the question: what did sea power actually contribute to the defeat of Revolutionary and Napoleonic France?

Mahan identifies seven functions of British sea power in the war:

  1. Strategic mobility. Sea power allowed Britain to shift military force from one theatre to another faster than any continental power could march: troops landed in the West Indies, the Mediterranean, Portugal, Holland, and the Peninsula, at times of Britain’s choosing.

  2. Economic pressure on France. British control of the sea cut France off from its colonial revenues, destroyed its overseas trade, and forced the French treasury to rely on continental exactions and plunder — a method that worked in the short term but exhausted the territories subjected to it.

  3. Sustaining the coalitions. British subsidies — made possible by a thriving commerce protected by the navy — kept the Austrian, Russian, and Prussian armies in the field. Without British gold, Mahan argues, no continental coalition could have sustained war against France for more than a single campaign.

  4. The blockade of French naval ports. The continuous watching of Brest, Toulon, Rochefort, and later Cadiz prevented the French fleet from concentrating, exercising, or intervening decisively in any theatre. Mahan emphasizes that the blockades were not passive but aggressive: they forced the French to sea at disadvantage, or kept them in port where they could not influence events.

  5. Direct naval action. The Nile (1798), Copenhagen (1801), and Trafalgar (1805) were the three great fleet actions of the war. Each destroyed an enemy fleet and each had immediate strategic consequences: the Nile formed the Second Coalition, Copenhagen broke the Armed Neutrality, Trafalgar made the invasion of England impossible forever.

  6. Protection of British commerce. The convoy system preserved the flow of trade that underlay British credit, taxation, and subsidy capacity. Mahan provides comparative loss statistics to show that while commerce raiding hurt, it never approached the level needed to force Britain’s surrender.

  7. The Peninsular War as the culminating demonstration. The British army in Portugal and Spain under Wellington was supplied, reinforced, and sustained entirely by sea. The French could not turn the British flank because they could not control the sea; they could not starve the British army because it was fed by sea; they could not invade England because the navy stood in the way. The Peninsular War, in Mahan’s reading, was the final demonstration of what sea power meant: not a weapon of conquest but the condition that made endurance possible.

Mahan closes with a comparative assessment of Pitt’s war policy. Pitt’s great achievement, he argues, was not any single strategic decision but the steady refusal to treat sea power as an end in itself. Pitt understood that the navy’s function was to enable a comprehensive strategy in which British commercial strength, coalition diplomacy, and limited continental intervention worked together. The moments when British policy faltered — the Peace of Amiens, the costly West Indian expeditions, the hesitation over the Baltic in 1800 — were moments when this comprehensive view was lost. The final victory, Mahan concludes, belonged not to any single battle but to the twenty-three-year application of sea power as an instrument of national policy, sustained through changing ministries, fluctuating popular support, and the immense strain of the longest war in modern European history. ^[source: raw/mahan-sea-power-french-revolution-vol2.txt]

Coverage Guide

  • Battle of Copenhagen (1801) — Chapter XIII. The destruction of the Danish fleet and the breaking of the Armed Neutrality. Essential for understanding the Baltic theatre and the second of Mahan’s three great fleet actions.

  • The Trafalgar campaign — Chapters XV and XVI. The most extended naval narrative in the volume: Napoleon’s invasion plan, the great flotilla at Boulogne, the transatlantic chase, Villeneuve’s indecision, Calder’s action, and the Battle of Trafalgar itself. Cross-reference with standard naval histories for additional tactical detail.

  • Battle of Austerlitz and the Ulm campaign (1805) — Chapter XVI (integrated into the Trafalgar narrative). Mahan shows the interaction of the naval and land campaigns in 1805 as a single strategic event: Trafalgar made the Ulm-Austerlitz campaign possible because Napoleon abandoned the invasion of England.

  • The Peace of Amiens (1802–1803) — Chapter XIV. Mahan’s analysis of why the peace failed: Bonaparte’s consistent expansionism, British rearmament, and the structural impossibility of a durable settlement while Napoleon dominated the continent.

  • The Armed Neutrality of 1800 — Chapters XII–XIII. The diplomatic combination against British maritime rights and its destruction at Copenhagen.

  • Commerce raiding and the Economic War — Chapters XVII and XVIII. The longest treatment in either volume of the guerre de course, the Continental System, the Berlin and Milan Decrees, and the British Orders in Council. Chapter XVIII covers the period 1806–1812 in detail.

  • Mahan’s final synthesis — Chapter XIX. The only extended summary of sea power’s role across the entire 1793–1812 period. The seven-function framework is the most concise statement of Mahan’s argument in either volume.

  • Bonaparte’s rise and the Consulate — Chapter XII. The coup of 18 Brumaire, Marengo, Hohenlinden, and the Peace of Lunéville — the consolidation of Bonaparte’s power. Mahan reads these events through the lens of their naval and strategic implications.

  • Comparative navy quality — Throughout, but especially Chapters XIII (Baltic navies at Copenhagen) and XV (the condition of the Franco-Spanish fleet before Trafalgar). Mahan continues the argument from Vol. I, Chapter II about the institutional superiority of the British officer corps.

Quality

Clean text extracted from an epub of professional quality. Mahan’s text is heavily footnoted — most chapters contain several hundred numbered citations — and a substantial footnotes section is appended. The prose is 19th-century formal English: dense, precise, and argumentative. This volume contains Mahan’s most famous single sentence (“England expects that every man will do his duty”), his most quoted anecdote (Nelson’s blind eye at Copenhagen), and his most sustained analytical passage (Chapter XIX’s summary). No significant OCR degradation. Mahan is an American naval officer writing in 1892, not a Frenchman writing in 1815; his perspective is Anglo-American strategic analysis rather than French political judgment. Both volumes together form one of the foundation texts of modern naval theory.
